ABSTRACT

In a knowledge-based economy, university–industry collaborations play a pivotal role in

driving innovation and economic growth. This study investigates the research orientations

of these collaborations, focusing on the balance between basic and applied research. Using

data from 631 collaborative projects funded by the French Cifre PhD program, we find

that most projects emphasize basic research (61%), contradicting the common expectation

that  industry-led  partnerships  primarily  focus  on  applied  research.  Importantly,  our

analysis  reveals  that  this  preference  for  basic  research  is  not  contingent  on  traditional

contextual  factors  such  as  scientific  discipline,  company  size,  or  university  type.  These

findings  challenge  established  assumptions  in  the  literature  and  suggest  that  even  in

industry-sponsored  research,  fundamental  inquiry  is  critical  in  supporting  long-term

innovation strategies. This paper contributes to the understanding of research orientations

in university–industry collaborations and offers practical suggestions for enhancing these

partnerships and informing research policy. Additionally, we outline a research agenda for

further  exploration  of  the  factors  influencing research  orientations,  the  impact  of  early-

stage industry engagement, and the role of policies in shaping collaboration outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

In  a  knowledge-based  economy  (Powell  &  Snellman,  2004),  university–industry

collaborations  are  considered  a  key  element  and have  gained  tremendous  importance  in

the last two decades (Etzkowitz & Leydesdorff, 2000; Gibbons et al., 1994; He et al., 2021).

These collaborations, which notably expand universities’ traditional teaching and research

missions (Compagnucci & Spigarelli, 2020; Fleming et al., 2019; Laredo, 2007), have been

strongly encouraged by policymakers and the development of  technology transfer offices

(Miller et al., 2018).

University–industry  collaborations  include  various  forms  of  partnerships  and

interactions with the goal of taking advantage of the particular strengths of academia and

industry to promote research, innovation, economic growth, and societal impact. We can

distinguish  at  least  two  models  of  collaboration.  The  first  model,  which  has  been

extensively  documented  in  the  literature, is based  on knowledge transfer  (Chai  & Shih,

2016;  Rajalo  &  Vadi,  2017;  Siegel  et  al.,  2003;  Terán-Bustamante  et  al.,  2021).  This

collaboration  model  includes  technology  transfer  and  commercialization,  such  as  patent

licensing, entrepreneurship support to launch startup ventures based on academic research

findings, consulting and expertise to provide technical assistance, or collaborative facilities

to share access to specialized resources. The second model is based on knowledge creation.

This type of collaboration — which has received less attention than collaborations based

on  knowledge  transfer  (Wit-de-Vries  et  al.,  2019)  —  includes  joint  research  projects,

industry-sponsored

research,

industry-academia  partnerships,  university–industry

consortia,  and  university–industry  cooperative  research  centres;  this  latter  kind  of

collaboration,  representing  the main  focus  of this  study,  is  not based  on  the  transfer  of

already produced knowledge but rather aims to cocreate new scientific knowledge (Sieg et

al., 2010; Tijssen, 2018).

Collaborating to create new knowledge implies sharing objectives, expectations or,

in other words, the same research orientation for a given project. Here, research orientation

refers to the strategic direction and focus taken by researchers or collaborative groups in

their pursuit of knowledge creation, characterized by the intent concerning and approach

to either basic or applied research or a blend of both, known as use-inspired basic research

(e.g., Stokes, 1997). However, we know very little about the research orientation on which

university–industry collaborative research projects rely. This lack of knowledge is partly

due to the fact that academia and industry function within separate institutional contexts

that are defined by their own sets of norms and incentives, which may sometimes be at

odds with each other’s efforts (Bjerregaard, 2010; Dasgupta and David, 1994; Sauermann

and Stephan, 2013). The differences in research orientations often lead to tensions between

(1)  researchers  in  universities  and  public  laboratories,  who  are  more  prone  to  pursue  a

more basic  research  approach,  and  (2)  researchers in  corporate  R&D  departments,  who

are more prone to taking a more applied research approach (Balconi et al., 2010; Bentley

et al., 2015; Godin, 2006, 2011; Gulbrandsen & Kyvik, 2010). Indeed, if research questions

are  prescribed  by  the  corporate  business  strategy,  then  researchers  in  industrial

laboratories may  produce  new  knowledge  for the  immediate  needs  of  the  company,  and

research  investment  is  likely  to  be  profitable  through  the  commercialization  of  the

company’s products and technologies. In contrast, basic research cannot be motivated by

the  quest  for  immediate  results  or  subject  to  business  pressures.  While  the  research

orientation can be based on a more mixed approach, combining, for example, basic research

with a consideration of the usage of the produced knowledge sounds like a more exceptional

case than the norm in university–industry collaborations (Stokes, 1997).

Therefore, this research aims to explore the different types of research orientations

in  university–industry  collaborations  and  the  extent  to  which  they  are  chosen  due  to

certain characteristics of the projects. While the literature has extensively discussed the

To  help  fill  this  gap,  it  is  necessary  to  improve  our  understanding  of  how  the

university‒industry  relationship  may  (or  may  not)  be  related  to  different  research

specific  research  orientation  in  the  context  of  a  given  collaboration.  Understanding  the

nature  of  research  orientations  in  the  formation  of  university‒industry  collaborations  is

crucial due to their significant implications for innovation, economic growth, and societal

advancement  (Perkmann  et  al.,  2021).  Exploring  this  aspect  of  collaboration  can

contribute  to  uncovering  the mechanisms  through  which research  orientations influence

the  initiation  and  structure  of  these  partnerships.  This  is  particularly  important  in  a

knowledge-driven economy, where the seamless integration of basic and applied research

is  essential  for  addressing  complex  global  challenges,  fostering  innovation,  and  ensuring

industry  collaboration.  One  key  aim  of  exploring  the  Cifre  programme  is  that  this

programme  covers  all  types  of  scientific  disciplines  (ranging  from  science,  technology,

engineering, and mathematics (STEM) disciplines to the social sciences) and companies

(including startups, small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), and large groups) in every

sector  as  well  as  in  all  regions  of  France.  Our  research  is  based  on  a  study  of  631

coding.  The  Cifre  programme  exhibits  characteristics  that  are  similar  to  those  of  other

university–industry programmes in other countries, especially in Europe, where this type

of collaborative PhD has become highly important (e.g., Salimi et al., 2016).

LITERATURE REVIEW

Both universities and firms have benefits and motivations related to university‒industry

collaborations (Bishop et al., 2011; D’Este & Perkmann, 2011; Lee, 1996, 2000; Perkmann

& Walsh, 2009; Siegel et al., 2003). For universities, collaborating with a firm can support

development by fostering relationships with knowledge users and facilitating technology

transfer  (Etzkowitz  &  Goktepe,  2005;  Perkmann  et  al.,  2013),  which  also  includes

improving funding for research projects and sharing knowledge and resources (D’Este &

Perkmann, 2011; Garcia et al., 2019). For firms, close collaboration with universities allows

As  evidenced  by  the  large  number  of  systemic  literature  reviews,  university‒

industry  collaborations  include  several  motivations  and  objectives  based  on  different

practices  and  organizations  (Ankrah  et  al.,  2013;  Miller  et  al.,  2018;  Sjöö  & Hellström,

2019;  Skute  et  al.,  2019;  Vick  &  Robertson,  2018).  The  objectives  may  concern  the

resolution  of  technological  problems,  the  sharing  of  scientific  resources,  the

commercialization of university-based technologies, the creation of new knowledge or the

creation of new businesses. These objectives are based on formal or informal interactions

such as licensing agreements, royalty agreements, research partnerships and consulting.

In  recent  decades,  universities  and  firms  have  increasingly  been  encouraged  to

collaborate  on  research  and  development  activities,  guided  by  the  principles  of  Mode  2

knowledge  production  (Gibbons  et  al.,  1994),  the  triple-helix  model  (Etzkowitz  &

Leydesdorff, 1997, 1999, 2000), and the third mission of the university (Compagnucci &

Spigarelli,  2020;  Laredo,  2007).  These  new  approaches  aim  to  move  beyond  simple

knowledge  transfer  as described  by  the  linear  model  of  innovation  (Godin,  2006,  2011).

The linear model posits that innovation follows a sequential process, beginning with basic

research,  progressing  through  applied  research,  and  culminating  in  product  or  service

development  by  a  company,  which  is  then  introduced  to  the  market  (Gulbrandsen  &

Kyvik, 2010; Henard & McFadyen, 2005; Narayanamurti & Odumosu, 2016).

Gibbons et al. (1994) argued that scientific knowledge production has shifted from

a Mode 1 paradigm, characterized by the strict separation of basic and applied research,

to a Mode 2 paradigm based on the convergence of research orientations. While Mode 1

relies  on  stabilized  disciplines  and  the  distinction  between  fundamental  and  applied

University–industry collaborations: Knowledge transfer or creation?

Collaboration based on knowledge transfer is a process that allows scientific discoveries,

knowledge, and intellectual assets to flow from creators, such as universities and research

institutions, to both public and private users. The aim of such collaboration is to convert

inventions  and  scientific  knowledge  into  new  products  and  services  that  contribute  to

societal needs (de Wit-de Vries et al., 2019; Geuna & Muscio, 2009; Lundquist, 2003; Miller

et al., 2018). Knowledge is first produced by the university independently of application

issues, and then, this knowledge is transferred, absorbed and used by the private sector to

address  innovation  and  business  needs  (Ankrah  et  al.,  2013;  Miller  et  al.,  2018; Sjöö  &

Hellström, 2019; Skute et al., 2019; Vick & Robertson, 2018). In this context, university–

industry collaborations are a way for firms to obtain expertise that cannot be generated

in-house through knowledge transfer (Fernandes & O’Sullivan, 2023; Rajalo & Vadi, 2017;

Siegel et al., 2003; Terán-Bustamante et al., 2021).

There are at least two ways to transfer scientific knowledge: the direct and indirect

placement  of  products  and  services  in  the  market  (Carlsson  &  Fridh,  2002;  Doganova,

2013; Wright et al., 2008). University spin-offs are a direct mechanism, representing the

Collaboration based on knowledge creation is a process that allows the creation of

new  scientific  knowledge  by  building  on  a  common  research  programme  and  addressing

specific research questions that are useful to all parties (Engestrom et al., 2003; Hong &

Olander, 2010; Inkpen, 1996). Unlike those based on knowledge transfer, the ambition of

this  type  of  collaboration  is not  to  commercialize  existing  knowledge  but  to  create  new

knowledge.  These  collaborations  can  take  various  forms,  such  as  research  partnerships,

joint  venture  research  projects,  university–industry  consortia,  or  university–industry

cooperative research centres (Hagedoorn et al., 2000; Oliver, 2022; Mannak et al., 2019).

These collaborations often involve interdisciplinary teams working on projects funded by

grants, contracts, or collaborative agreements. These collaborations can lead to scientific

findings, new knowledge creation, new technology development, inventions, patent creation

and innovations.

Research orientations in knowledge creation collaborations

Collaborative research, where academic researchers collaborate with companies to conduct

knowledge creation to address industry-specific challenges, is a common activity for both

science-based  organizations  and

research-intensive  universities.  However,

these

collaborations also face theoretical and practical barriers and challenges related to various

aspects of partnership formation, dynamics, goals, effectiveness, and outcomes. Bruneel et

al.  (2010)  highlighted  the  following  two  types  of  barriers  limiting  university–industry

collaborations: barriers related to conflicts over IP and barriers related to differences in

the research orientations of industry and universities.

Research  orientations refer  to  the distinction  between  basic  and  applied  research

(Busch, 1945; Gulbrandsen & Kyvik, 2010; Henard & McFadyen, 2005; Hottenrott, 2012).

Research questions are prescribed by corporate business strategies; researchers in industrial

laboratories produce new knowledge for the immediate needs of companies, and research

spending is likely to be profitable through the commercialization of the company’s products

and technologies. In contrast, basic research is not motivated by the quest for immediate

results  or  subject  to  business  pressures.  Basic,  or  fundamental,  research  is  performed

without thought of practical ends and is driven by a quest to understand nature and its

laws. As a result, researchers in universities and public laboratories are motivated primarily

by  basic  research,  while  researchers  in  corporate  R&D  departments  focus  on  applied

research (Balconi et al., 2010). Academic researchers create new fundamental knowledge

and publish their results in scientific journals, while industrial scientists produce applied

knowledge in the form of products and manufacturing processes (Partha & David, 1994;

Tartari et al., 2012). Given the two different systems of knowledge creation, collaborations

between  universities  and  industries  are  likely  to  be  plagued  by  conflicts  due  to  a  weak

alignment of research orientations. According to Garcia et al. (2019, p. 350), academia and

industry  operate  in  distinct  institutional  environments  characterized  by  norms  and

incentives that can conflict with one another. As a result, we are faced with a paradox.

On  the  one  hand,  many  studies  highlight  the  distinction  between  academic  and

commercial  logic  as  two  separate  institutional  frameworks  that  can  complicate

collaborative  efforts  (Bjerregaard,  2010;  Dasgupta  and  David,  1994;  Sauermann  and

Stephan, 2013). A lack of alignment in research orientations hinders collaboration, as it

generates  contradictory  objectives  and  expectations  on  various  aspects  of  collaborative

research  projects: How  can  common research  questions  be shared? How  can  the  parties

agree on a common research program? Should the project be limited to a specific research

domain? Which research methodologies should be chosen? (Bruneel et al., 2010; Hemmert

investigate  a  topic  in  a  preliminary  and  open-ended  manner  to  explore  and  generate

insights and hypotheses rather than to test specific hypotheses or theories. An exploratory

research approach is valuable for identifying trends, patterns, or relationships that may

Cifre PhD programme description

In  this  study,  we  decide  to  focus  on  a  French  PhD  collaborative  programme,  the  Cifre

PhD  programme;  its  name  refers  to  the  French  Industrial  Convention  for  Research

Training. This programme is supported by the ANRT, a not-for-profit organization that

acts  on  behalf  of  the French  Ministry  of  Higher  Education,  Research,  and  Innovation.

Founded  in  1953,  the  ANRT  has  a  mission  to  bring  together  all  private  and  public

stakeholders in French research in all sectors of activity and all scientific disciplines. In

2020,  the  ANRT  had  354  members  and  partners  (companies  and  public  institutions),

representing  nearly  three-quarters  of  national  R&D  efforts.  Since  1981,  the  French

Ministry of Higher Education, Research and Innovation (MESRI) has entrusted the ANRT

with the implementation of the Cifre PhD programme (Industrial Agreement of Training

through  Research).  The  Cifre  PhD  programme  provides  financial  assistance  to  French

firms  that  recruit  a  PhD  student  to  work  on  a  research  project  for  3  years.  The  PhD

programme  at  a  public  university.  He  or  she  conducts  his  or  her  research  through  a

university–industry collaboration under the supervision of a PhD supervisor attached to a

public  research  laboratory  and  a  scientific  manager  within  the  company.  The  doctoral

student devotes 100% of his or her time, shared between the university and the company,

to his or her research work.

Cifre  PhD  programme.  Each  year,  the  Cifre  PhD  programme  represents  approximately

DISCUSSION

This  study  aimed  to  explore  the  nature  of  research  orientations  in  university–industry

collaborations,  specifically  in  the  context  of  the  Cifre  PhD  programme.  Following  the

literature review, we aimed at answering the following research questions: (1) What is the

nature of research orientations in university–industry collaborations focused on knowledge

creation? (2) What is the influence of context on research orientations? (3) What are the

relationships  between  research  orientations  and  forms  of  collaboration?    Based  on  an

analysis of 631 partnership agreements, we categorized the research orientations as basic

research, applied research, and use-inspired basic research, we examined the influence of

context on research orientations and the influence of the forms of collaborations on research

orientations.  We  have  shown  that  more  than  half  of  the  projects  (61%)  focus  on  basic

research, highlighting a strong commitment by industry to fundamental scientific inquiry.

While  industry  sponsors  joint  research  projects,  these  findings  indicate  that  research

collaborations  mainly  embrace  academic  research  conventions,  the  university  logic  and

prioritize orientations focused on basic research. This trend was consistent across different

scientific  disciplines,  university  types,  and  firm  sizes,  thus  challenging  the  claim  that

industry-driven  projects  predominantly  focus  on  applied  research  (Bozeman,  2000;  Lee,

1996; O’Shea et al., 2005). These findings highlight the strategic value of basic research in

industry-sponsored  projects  as  well  as  the  importance  of  developed  collaborations.  Our

results  provide  a  comprehensive  overview  of  the  diversity  and  dynamics  of  these

Theoretical and empirical contributions

Our  study  contributes  to  the  literature  on  university–industry  collaboration  in  several

ways. First, we emphasize the significance of focusing on research orientations during the

negotiation  phase  of  collaborative  projects.  Existing  studies  on  university‒industry

collaboration often concentrate on the performance and success of such projects (Bikard

et  al.,  2019;  Gulbrandsen  &  Smeby,  2005;  Perkmann  &  Walsh,  2009;  Perkmann  et  al.,

2021). Conversely, our study focuses on the preliminary phase of the collaboration process,

specifically  the  decisions  and  motivations  that  precede  the  initiation  of  various

collaborative  research  projects  on  the  basis  of  knowledge  creation  (Hottenrott,  2012;

Ruoslahti,  2020).  Our  study  indicates  that  those  collaborations  are  oriented  mainly

towards research orientations focusing on the creation of new scientific knowledge without

considering potential applications and uses. Such cases are not only rare events where both

parties managed to align their goals and expectations toward fundamental inquiries; on

the contrary, they constitute the majority of the studied projects. In other words, despite

the industry's bargaining power, projects are not focused mainly on developing knowledge

for short-term integration in the new product development process. Instead, companies are

more  willing  to  collaborate  with  universities  to  nourish  their  knowledge  base  with

knowledge that can help them sustain their R&D process over the long run.

Second, our study contributes to understanding university–industry collaborations

by revealing that the traditional influence of contextual factors, such as scientific discipline,

university type, and firm characteristics, on research orientations is not as significant as

expected. Despite the wide range of scientific disciplines involved in these collaborations,

most  projects,  including  those  in  traditionally  applied  fields  such  as  engineering  and

Practical implications

Our findings have implications for the management of university–industry collaborations

as well as for research and innovation policies.

The apparent distinction between academic logic and commercial logic suggests that

collaboration  on  the  basis  of  knowledge  creation  is  not  easy.  The  lack  of  alignment  in

research  orientations  can  potentially  generate  contradictory  objectives  and  expectations

regarding various aspects of such a collaborative research project. First, our findings reveal

that  companies  and  universities  can  collaborate  by  codesigning  a  common  research

programme  and  agreeing  on  a  shared  research  orientation,  thus  indicating  that

collaborations based on knowledge creation are complementary to collaborations based on

knowledge transfer. However, participants in collaborative projects should concentrate on

joint endeavours grounded in basic research. Our results indicate that collaborations often

adhere to the practices of academic research and prioritize research orientations centred

on  basic  research. Furthermore,  the results  highlight  different  basic  research  objectives,

innovation  is  not  a  linear,  deterministic  process  that  is  composed  of  sequences  of

independent  steps,  with  no  possible  feedback  among  basic  research,  applied  research,

technological  development,  and  product  commercialization  (Clarke,  2010;  Henard  &

McFadyen, 2005; Jansen, 1995; Walsh, 1984).

Limitations and future research directions

Because of the exploratory nature of this study, several limitations should be considered.

First,  due  to  the  typically  small  and  nonrepresentative  samples  used  in  exploratory

research, the findings may not be generalizable to larger populations or contexts. Indeed,

we decide to focus on university‒industry collaborations on the basis of PhD collaborative

programmes, also called industrial PhDs (Abu Sa’a & Yström, 2024; Grimm, 2018; Salimi

et al., 2016). In particular, we study a French PhD collaborative programme—the Cifre

PhD programme—and focus on 631 partnership agreements associated with the same year.

Hence, it would be preferable to supplement this study with data concerning other PhD

programmes in diverse countries. Second, this research may be affected by several biases

due to subjective interpretation or selective attention to the data, particularly because it

is a qualitative exploratory study. Additional empirical research could enhance and expand

upon this initial approach to the task of analysing knowledge creation in the context of

university–industry  collaborations.  Third,  this  exploratory  research

focuses  on

understanding relationships and patterns and does not establish causality among variables.

Our  findings  suggest  that  research  orientations  are  not  affected  by  scientific  discipline,

university type or the nature of firms but cannot determine cause-and-effect relationships.
