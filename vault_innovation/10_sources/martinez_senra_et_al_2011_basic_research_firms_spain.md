IS BASIC RESEARCH IN FIRMS USEFUL?
EVIDENCE FROM PANEL DATA FOR SPAIN

Ana I. Martínez-Senra
María A. Quintás
Xosé H. Vázquez1

Department of Business Organization and Marketing
Universidade de Vigo

Abstract

We  propose  a  model  that  associates  basic  research  with  product  innovation  through  the  absorptive

capacity it generates, moderated also by the level of industry appropriability. To test these relations,

we used a sample of 8,861 firms taken from a panel (PITEC). Our data suggest that basic research in

firms increases their knowledge stock and flows, improving their capacity to identify, assimilate and

exploit  external  knowledge  and,  therefore,  their  innovative  product  performance.  By  using  lags  we

were  able  to  find  that  this  result  can  last  for  up  to  three  years.  In  addition,  we  see  that  weak

appropriability  regimes  can  have  a  positive  effect  by  increasing  the  influence  of  basic  research  on

absorptive  capacity.  This  evidence  not  only  questions  the  disdain  frequently  shown  by  managers

towards basic research, but also calls for open reflection on both the net effect of appropriability on

innovative performance and the advisability of allocating public resources to stages and areas of the

innovation process in which there are seldom ‘market failures’.

Key words:

Product innovation, basic research, absorptive capacity, appropriability.

1. INTRODUCTION

Practically  throughout  the  20th  century  science  mostly  took  place  in  universities,  while  the

responsibility  for  taking  new  or  improved  products  and  processes  to  the  market  was  left  almost
exclusively  to  firms.  However,  this  trend  might  have  started  to  change  in  the  early years  of  the  21st

century (Pisano, 2010). The most recent data for Europe (2005) and for the USA (2007) suggest that

the gap between private and university basic research is narrowing (Eurostat, 2011). The same source

also confirms that in Japan basic research in firms is now even exceeding university research (42.7 € /

inhabitant vs. 39.1 € respectively in 2007).

This  change has  undoubtedly  arisen  in part  because  universities  have  changed their  budgetary  focus

from  the  ‘creation  of  science’  to  the  ‘business  of  science’,  making  greater  efforts  to  utilize  their

intellectual property through licenses and spinoffs (Mowery et al., 2001; Gregorio and Shane, 2003;

Sampat,  2006).  However,  the  slowly-growing  conviction  among  business  managers  about  the

profitability of investments in basic research has probably had a greater influence. This is something

that  has  been  recommended  in  academic  circles  for  decades  (Griliches,  1980;  Mansfield,  1981;

Rosenberg,  1990),  and  has  given  rise  to  empirical  studies  on  its  effects  on  productivity  (Griliches,

1986),  absorptive  capacity  (De  Marchi  and  Rocchi,  2000),  patents  (Lim,  2004)  and  business  profits

(Henard and McFadyen, 2005).

In this context, the lack of empirical evidence to explain the link between basic research and product

innovation  is  surprising.  Take,  for  example,  the  most  widely-quoted  studies  on  new  product

development (Durisin et al., 2010) which make no reference at all to the influence of basic research, or

the  fullest  meta-analysis  on  this  subject  (Henard  and  Szymanski,  2001)  in  which,  out  of  the  24

variables  for  which  sufficient  correlations  are  found  in  the  literature  for  them  to  be  included  in  the

study  (n  >  10  correlations),  not  one  refers  explicitly  to  investment  in  basic  research.  To  cover  this

niche is, therefore, the basic aim of this paper.

This analysis also includes two additional contributions. Firstly, although the literature has emphasized

the role of absorptive capacity in innovation processes, most studies focus on either its determinants

(Jansen et al., 2005; Schmidt, 2010) or the moderating role played by certain variables (Oltra and Flor,

2003; Lim, 2004; Nieto and Quevedo, 2005; Tsai, 2009; Escribano et al., 2009). This moderating role

traditionally  granted  to  absorptive  capacity  suggests  that  it  affects  the  sign  and  strength  of  relations

between any input and the firm’s result in innovation. Lim (2004) suggests, for example, that general

research activities are directly related to patent generation and that this relation becomes stronger with

greater  absorptive  capacity.  However,  absorptive  capacity  might  have  an  effect  that  conceptually

would be even more relevant if its influence were not just over the sign and strength of the relation but

over its actual existence. This is what we call the mediating effect of absorptive capacity like the effect

identified by Liao et al. (2010) between the acquisition of knowledge and global innovation capacity.

Along  the  same  lines,  our  study  proposes  a  mediating  role  for  absorptive  capacity  between  basic

research and product innovation.

Moreover, since the influence of appropriability on the background and results of absorptive capacity

are still a matter of controversy (Todorova and Durisin, 2007), we include this variable in the model.

In our paper we analyze the effect of appropriability, firstly, on the relation between basic research and

absorptive capacity and, secondly, on the relation between absorptive capacity and product innovation.

Our analysis suggests, and corroborates, that the absence of total appropriability of the results does not

prevent the firms with most basic research from obtaining better results in product innovation.

Finally,  from  a  strictly  empirical  viewpoint,  the  analysis  has  three  strengths.  Firstly,  of  special

relevance is the use of lags in the relation between basic research and absorptive capacity. This allows

us  to  test  to  what  extent  the  basic  research  carried  out  in  a  given  year  affects  the  simultaneous  or

subsequent  capacity  for  assimilating  and  exploiting  the  knowledge  that  becomes  available.  It  can  in

fact  be  expected  that,  with  today’s  fast  technological  change,  the  knowledge  generated  by  basic

research  will  soon  be  outdated.  We  therefore  aim  to  identify  how  long  the  positive  effect  of  basic

research on future absorptive capacity can last. Secondly, in order to reduce any possible data bias and

back up the widespread application of our results, we do not limit the sample to any specific sector.

This  is  a  relevant  contribution  because  most  studies  on  basic  research  focus  on  a  specific  sector,

normally  on  knowledge-intensive  sectors  in  which  this  type  of  research  has  traditionally  been

important,  such  as  pharmaceutics  or  biotechnology  (Gambardella,  1992;  Cockburn  and  Henderson,

1998; Lim, 2004). Finally, we build a multi-item index to measure absorptive capacity. By doing this

we aim to reduce any measurement errors associated with the use of excessively simplifying proxies

which, as with expenditure on R&D, do not reflect the multidimensional nature of the construct.

The paper is structured as follows: in section two we present the state of the art regarding the relations

between  basic  research,  absorptive  capacity  and  appropriability  in  product  innovation.  Three

hypotheses  are  drawn  on  which  we  build  a  model  (Figure  1)  to  be  tested  in  section  four.  In  section

three, we describe the sample of firms, the variables and the methodology used. In section 4 we cover

the econometric analysis and discuss the results. The conclusions give our main findings and certain

implications for product innovation theory, as well as recommendations for business management and

for public policy.

2. THEORETICAL BACKGROUND AND HYPOTHESES

The study of product innovation determinants has occupied much of the research agenda over recent

years, but there is still no consensus on the explanatory factors. The first studies were in the field of

Industrial  Economics  and  took  their  inspiration  from  Schumpeter,  leaving  a  legacy  that  clarified

matters significantly. So, although the evidence is ambiguous regarding the effect of firm size, there is

a  degree  of  consensus  that  certain  characteristics  of  the  sector  such  as  technological  opportunities

(Levin et al., 1985; Geroski, 1990), market opportunities (Schmookler, 1966; Levin, 1981; Dougherty,

1990) or the conditions for appropriability of innovations (Mansfield, 1981, 1986; Levin et al., 1987)

may influence product innovation differences between firms.

In parallel, studies carried out in the management field shared with Industrial Economics the emphasis

on  market  characteristics  (growth  potential,  rivalry,  uncertainty)  but  they  focused  above  all  on

stressing certain internal characteristics of organization (Vega-Jurado et al., 2008). They often adopted

the  Resource-based  view  of  the  firm  (Wernerfelt,  1984),  stressing  the  importance  of  generating

distinctive  competencies  (Leonard-Barton,  1992;  Tidd,  2000)  for  innovation  strategy  (Henard  and

Szymanski,  2001),  technology  (Love  and  Roper,  1999;  Bhattacharya  and  Bloch,  2004),  human

resources  (Hoffman  et  al.,  1998;  Song  et  al.,  2003),  organizational  routines  associated  with

management  philosophies  (Cooper,  1990;  Rothwell,  1992;  Souitaris,  2002;  Webster,  2004)  and  the

firm’s absorptive capacity (Stock et al., 2001; Fosfuri and Tribó, 2008).

The  role  of  basic  research  has  been  almost  residual  throughout  the  literature.  Although  there  were

some  prior  studies  on  the general  effects  of  basic  research  (Mansfiel,  1980;  Griliches,  1986),  it  was

probably  Rosenberg  (1990)  who  aroused  interest  amongst  academics  in  the  link  between  basic

research and product innovation. Rosenberg suggested in this paper that basic research is essential for

making  strategic  decisions  on  future  product  lines  and  the  process  technologies  to  be  adopted.  His

argument was that the output of basic research is new knowledge which may be used for new product

development. One year later, Pavitt (1991) completed this by pointing to the benefits of private basic

research – the development of certain skills, methods and networks of professional contacts allowing

firms to better exploit external scientific conclusions. Implicitly, they both suggested, therefore, that

basic research improves firms’ absorptive capacity, that is, their capacity for identifying, assimilating

and exploiting knowledge in their environment.

Perhaps because in 1990 the concept of absorptive capacity (Cohen and Levinthal, 1989) was not yet

well-known,  Rosenberg’s  argument  was  initially  tested  in  some  cases  as  a  direct  relation  between

basic research and patents (Gambardella, 1992). However, given the abstraction inherent in the results

of  basic  research  and  the  increasing  importance  of  the  absorptive  capacity  concept,  it  was  not  long

before studies on basic research or product innovation started to consider the influence of absorptive

capacity in one way or another. Generally speaking, we could classify these studies in two groups –

those which study the influence of basic research on absorptive capacity, and those which focus on the

relation between absorptive capacity and innovative performance.

The first group of studies suggests that firms that carry out their own R&D – either basic or applied –

are in a better position to use any available external and internal information (Rocha, 1999; Kamien

and  Zang,  2000;  Mancusi,  2004;  Escribano  et  al.,  2009).  In  particular,  strictly  with  regard  to  basic

research,  several  studies  show  a  close  relation  with  absorptive  capacity  (Lane  and  Lubatkin,  1998;

Dyer and Singh, 1998; De Marchi and Rocchi, 2000), giving arguments that tie in exactly with what

was  stated  by  Rosenberg  (1990)  and  Pavitt  (1991)  regarding  the  stock  and  flows  of  knowledge.  So

basic research was conceived as a learning process which, although not directly linked to the market,

increases the depth and breadth of the stock of stored knowledge (Hernard and McFadyen, 2005) and

consequently  allows  more  to  be  learnt  from  any  freely  available  internal  and  external  information

(Cassiman et al., 2002). In fact, the link between basic research and absorptive capacity is so close that

even  authors  such  as  Cassiman  and  Veugelers  (2006)  use  basic  research  as  a  measure  of  absorptive

capacity.

Moreover,  the  literature  on  innovation  performance  emphasizes  the  capacity  for  using  external

knowledge  sources  as  a  key  factor  for  innovation  activities  (Von  Hippel,  1988;  Tripsas,  1997;

Cockburn  and  Henderson,  1998;  Cohen  et  al.,  2002;  Chesbrough,  2003;  Caloghirou  et  al.,  2004;

Rothaermel  and  Hess,  2007;  Liao  et  al.,  2010).  In  particular,  since  new  product  development  is  a

knowledge-intensive  activity  that  benefits  from  the  acquisition  and  use  of  external  scientific,

technological or market information, greater absorptive capacity can be expected to improve product

innovation (Stock et al., 2001). In fact, there is empirical evidence suggesting that firms with higher

levels  of  absorptive  capacity  are  more  product  innovative.  For  example,  Stock  et  al.  (2001)  suggest

that  this  relation  has  an  inverted  U  shape,  whereas  Fosfuri  and  Tribó  (2008)  verify  that  firms  with

greater absorptive capacity systematically obtain greater percentages of sales of new, or substantially

improved, products.

Clearly,  all  these  studies  have  helped  us  to  understand  the  close  link  between  basic  research,

absorptive capacity and product innovation performance. It is surprising, however, that no studies have

4

aimed to check these relations as a whole, empirically exploring whether basic research has a positive

effect on product innovation through absorptive capacity. The study which comes closest to this idea is

by  Lim  (2004),  who  verifies  empirically  that  absorptive  capacity  moderates  the  relation  between

research activities (both basic and applied) and invention capacity (number of patents). However, as

the life of Benjamin Franklin illustrates clearly, capacity for invention is not the same as capacity for

innovation.  Moreover,  granting  a  moderating  role  to  absorptive  capacity  requires  the  implicit

assumption  that  there  is  a  direct  link  between  basic  research  and  product  innovation,  in  which

absorptive  capacity  only  affects  the  strength  of  this  link  but  does  not  explain  why  it  exists.  And

assuming  a  direct  link  between  basic  research  and  product  innovation  is  still  surprising  if  we

remember that basic research is defined as research that aims to further scientific knowledge without

finding  any  specific  marketable  application.  That  is,  obviously  a  firm  may  randomly  generate  a

product innovation stemming from its basic research, but the proposal of a direct link between the two

variables cannot arise randomly. Our proposal conceives absorptive capacity as a mediating variable

between basic research and product innovation, because we consider this explains how and why such a

link  exists;  that  is,  if  there  is  no  mediation  by  absorptive  capacity,  the  link  between  these  variables

cannot be explained.

So, capacity for product innovation depends on the firm’s capacity for recognizing the value of new,

external information, assimilating it and applying it to commercial purposes (absorptive capacity), and

this  depends  on  the  firm’s  level  of  prior  knowledge  –  at  least  in  part  –  stemming  from  the  level  of

investment in basic research. Against this background, we pose the following hypothesis:

H1: Firms’ basic research increases product innovation by improving their absorptive capacity.

But,  however  strong  the  link  between  basic  research,  absorptive  capacity  and  product  innovation  is

statistically,  considerations  regarding  the  appropriability  of  the  research  findings  may  affect  the

strength of the link. In fact, the appropriability of innovations may have two basic effects on the initial

model proposed: a first effect on the link between basic research and absorptive capacity, and a second

effect  on  the  link  between  the  latter  and  the  innovation  result.  In  both  cases,  the  literature  points  to

clear areas of controversy that should be clarified (Todora and Durisin, 2007).

So, on the one hand, the more difficult it is for firms in a sector to appropriate new knowledge that

they  generate  themselves,  the  greater  the  spillovers  of  knowledge  becoming  available  for  their

competitors. The regime of industry appropriability will not only determine incentives for investing in

the  determinants  of  absorptive  capacity  (Cohen  and Levinthal,  1990),  but  may  also  affect  the  actual

strength  of  the  relation  between  such  determinants  –  in  our  case,  basic  research  –  and  absorptive

capacity.  If  the  argument  explaining  this  relation  is  based  on  the  stock  and  flows  of  knowledge

generated by basic research (Rosenberg, 1990; Pavitt, 1991), in weak appropriability regimes in which

more, and better, external information is available, then firms are likely to have greater possibilities for

improving  their  absorptive  capacity.  Firstly,  because  a  higher  level  of  available  knowledge

undoubtedly  promotes  the  formation  of  human  capital  for  the  same  level  of  investment  in  basic

5

research; and, secondly, because the additional interaction with other agents, which might arise from

lower  appropriability,  will  facilitate  the  additional  identification  of  scientific  and  technological

opportunities  as  well  as  the  capacity  for  assimilating  new  knowledge  that  is  scientifically  far  from

what  the  firm  had  previously.  We  therefore  expect  that,  the  weaker  the  appropriability  conditions,

firms  will  be  able  to  benefit  more  from  a  given  level  of  basic  research  for  generating  absorptive

capacity.  When  appropriability  is  strong,  it  will  be  the  other  way  round.  We  therefore  pose  the

following hypothesis:

H2:  Stronger  appropriability  regimes  negatively  moderate  the  relation  between  basic  research  and

absorptive capacity.

On  the  other  hand,  the  level  of  appropriability  of  innovations  may  also  affect  the  relation  between

absorptive  capacity  and  product  innovation.  In  this  case,  however,  there  are  two  opposing  forces

which  cast  doubt  on  the  net  influence  of  appropriability.  Zahra  and  George  (2002)  suggest  that,  in

weak appropriability regimes, firms obtain less benefit from absorbed knowledge because other firms

with complementary assets may gain at least part of the return from their innovations (Teece, 1986).

This could be the interpretation of the empirical evidence presented by  Escribano et al. (2009), who

argue  that  the  role  of  absorptive  capacity  is  more  relevant  in  environments  characterized  by  strong

protection  of  intellectual  property  rights.  However,  exactly  the  same  argument  could  be  used  to

explain why low appropriability of results may intensify the effect of absorptive capacity in product

innovation.  After  all,  just  as  a  firm  may  lose  returns  from  its  absorptive  capacity  because  of  low

appropriability of its results, the same firms could also be considered to be utilising their absorptive

capacity  even  better  to  develop  new  products  because  they  would  be  able  to  benefit  from  the

knowledge  generated  by  other  firms.  When  the  appropriability  regime  is  strong,  the  opposite  would

happen. So, since we have diverging positions, we pose a hypothesis with two alternatives:

H3a:  The  strongest  appropriability  regimes  negatively  moderate  the  relation  between  absorptive

capacity and product innovation.

H3b:  The  strongest  appropriability  regimes  positively  moderate  the  relation  between  absorptive

capacity and product innovation.

3. DATA AND EMPIRICAL RESEARCH

3.1 Sample and measurement of variables

As  our  data  base,  we  use  the  Technological  Innovation  Panel  (PITEC),  based  on  the  Community

Innovation Survey carried out in Spain under the leadership of the Spanish National Statistics Institute

(INE).  The  PITEC  has  been  monitoring  technological  activities  in  Spanish  firms  since  2004.  It  is

therefore  the  result  of  stratified  sampling  in  which  four  groups  of  firms  are  represented:  firms  with

over  200  workers,  firms  with  internal  R&D  expenditure,  firms  with  less  than  200  workers  but  with

external R&D expenditure, and firms with less than 200 workers with no expenditure on innovation.

Table 2 describes the variables used in the study. In order to measure product innovation, we built a

variable  combining  the  importance  of  three  effects  that  innovative  activities  may  have  on  firms’

products: a wider range of products and services, greater market penetration and higher-quality goods

or services. Firms ranked these on a scale from 1 to 4, with 1 if the effect is slight and 4 if it is marked.

This variable has been used in other studies, for example, Murovec and Prodan (2009).

For absorptive capacity, although the literature suggests different measurement methods, most studies

use  single-dimensional  measures  such  as  the  firm’s  R&D  activity  (Cassiman  and  Veugelers,  2002;

Oltra and Flor, 2003; Schoenmakers and Duysters, 2006; Nieto and Santamaría, 2007), the number of

highly-trained workers (Caloghirou et al., 2004) or the importance of external sources of knowledge

(Murovec and Prodan, 2009; Schmidt, 2010). However, these single-dimensional measures have been

much criticized because they do not reflect the richness of the construct. In the process of transforming

external  knowledge  flows  into  innovation  results,  the  role  played  by  absorptive  capacity  changes

constantly  and  affects  different  capacities  and  routines  at  different  times  (Fosfuri  and  Tribó,  2008).

Mangematin and Nesta (1999), Wong et al. (1999), Lane et al. (2001), Jansen et al., (2005), Nieto and

Quevedo  (2005),    Vinding  (2006),  Tu  et  al.  (2006),  Liao  et  al.  (2007),  Escribano  et  al.  (2009)  and

Camisón and Fores (2010) study absorptive capacity in greater depth and propose multi-dimensional

operationalization of the construct.

In  line  with  these  authors,  we  propose  multi-dimensional  operationalization  of  the  construct  by

bringing  5  variables  together  in  an  index  generated  by  multiple  correspondence  analysis.  The

7

synthesizing capacity of the resulting index is 72.8% of the variance and Cronbach’s alpha is 0.907.

The five variables covered by this index are the following:

1)  Internal expenditure on R&D, because this expenditure not only generates new knowledge but

also  increases  the  firm’s  capacity  for  recognizing,  assimilating  and  exploiting  existing

information,  thus  contributing  to  its  absorptive  capacity  (Cohen  and  Levinthal,  1989).  To

measure this variable, we use firms’ internal expenditure in 2007.

2)  Systematic  internal  R&D  activities,  because  firms  which  are  constantly  involved  in  such

activities will have developed the necessary skills and experience to adapt external knowledge

in  their  specific  research  fields  so  will  have  greater  absorptive  capacity  (Becker  and  Peter,

2000; Oltra and Flor, 2003; Schmidt, 2010) than other firms which only occasionally carry out

such activities. We measure this variable using a dummy taking value 1 if the firm carried out

internal R&D in 2005, 2006 and 2007, and zero otherwise.

3)  Workers’ education and training, as this has a positive influence on firms’ absorptive capacity

(Rothwell and Dogson, 1991; Frenz et al., 2004). We measure this variable using the quotient

between university-trained personnel in R&D and the total number of employees.

4)  Training effort, because this also has a positive influence on employees’ skill at assimilating

external information (Frenz et al., 2004). To measure this variable, we use an ordinal variable

taking 10 values depending on the percentage of sales allocated to training during the period

2005-2007.

5)  The importance of external sources for innovation activities, because absorptive capacity can

also be developed through links with external knowledge sources such as customers, suppliers,

competitors, universities (Henderson and Cockburn, 1998) or research institutes (Mangematin

and  Nesta,  1999).  This  variable  is  measured  using  an  index  covering  the  importance  of  ten

external  sources  (suppliers,  customers,  competitors,  consultants, universities,  public  research

bodies,  technology  centers,  conferences,  scientific  journals  and  business  associations)  for
innovation activities2.

In addition, in order to quantify the annual basic research variable, we used the percentage of current

internal expenditure on R&D that firms in our sample devote to this activity according to the PITEC

panel for 2007, 2006, 2005 and 2004.

Finally, we included two control variables: firm size, because prior research has shown that innovation

results might benefit from economies of scale and scope (Henderson and Cockburn, 1994; Escribano

et al., 2009), measured here by the firm’s turnover; and sector of activity, because this might determine

the ease of producing innovations in terms of time and cost. In fact, it has been shown that relevant

5. CONCLUSIONS

Our  results  confirm  that  basic  research  improves  product  innovation  through  the  effect  it  has  on

absorptive capacity. This happens because, even though basic research by definition does not seek a

specific  marketable application, it does increase the  stock and flow of knowledge needed to extract,

assimilate  and  exploit  under  the  best  conditions  the  latest  scientific  and  technological  developments

which may turn into new or improved products.

Moreover, it should be stressed that the effect of basic research on absorptive capacity and, therefore,

on product innovation has a time limit. That is, however much a firm invests in basic research during

any given year, the effect on its absorptive capacity will have a limited duration unless it perseveres. If

its  effort  is  not  systematic,  fast-moving  scientific  and  technological  change  will  lead  the  knowledge

generated by the investment in basic research to quickly become outdated. We have seen in this study

that the positive effect of investing in basic research might last up to three years.

We also verified that the effect of basic research on absorptive capacity is greater in firms that belong

to sectors with weaker appropriability regimes. This might be due not only to the fact that a greater

level  of  spillovers  in  the  environment  would  stimulate  the  formation  of  human  capital  for  the  same

level  of  investment  in  basic  research,  but  there  might  also  be  an  additional  interaction  in  that

environment  between  different  agents  that  can  improve  the  identification  of  new  scientific  and

technological opportunities.

We have not, however, been able to test empirically whether the level of appropriability affects in any

way  the  relation  between  absorptive  capacity  and  product  innovation.  A  possible  future  line  of

research could study whether this result indicates that comparing these arguments would require more

information on the firm’s innovation strategy. In weak appropriability environments, it may be that the

effect  of  absorptive  capacity  on  product  innovation  in  firms  with  offensive  innovation  strategies  is

limited because other firms take advantage of their effort. Similarly, perhaps other firms with imitative

innovation strategies might be able to take advantage of exactly this, thus making the effect of their

absorptive capacity on their product innovation even more powerful.

Our results have certain implications for business management and for public policy. On the one hand,

managers  should  ignore  passing  fads  of  little  intellectual  depth  to  question  conventional  thinking

regarding  basic  research  and  product  innovation.  Short-sightedness  focusing  exclusively  on  market-

based R&D, neglecting the generation of human capital amongst their technical staff can only lead to a

gradual  loss  of  capacity  to  support  substantial  product  innovations.  In  particular,  regarding  basic

research as a source of advantages associated with the stock and flow of knowledge, our results should

qualify some of the most  important drawbacks faced by managers when investing in basic research,

mainly because the desired long-term effects of basic research in fact do not last so long. We have in

fact found that the investment in basic research carried out by firms in 2005, 2006 and 2007 affects

absorptive  capacity  and  product  innovation  during  that  same  period.  Secondly,  our  analysis,  carried

out  on  all  types  of  firms  from  all  sectors  and  of  all  sizes,  generates  results  that  are  very  predictable

16

regarding  product  innovation  capacity.  By  predictability  we  are  not  referring  to  the  specific

improvements  they  will  make  but  to  the  fact  that  we  have  found  a  robust  relation  between  basic

research  and  improvements  of  various  types  in  firms’  products.  Finally,  the  low  appropriability  of

basic research (which has nothing to do with the above-mentioned industry appropriability regimes) is

the  third  main  challenge  for  business  management.  Our  results  indicate  two  things.  Firstly,  the

arguments used here to justify basic research in firms have little to with its appropriability, that is, its

specific,  immediate,  exclusive,  return.  Once  its  importance  has  been  justified  by  its  effects  on

absorptive capacity (a tacit dynamic capacity), arguments on the difficulty of converting basic research

into specific market innovations are of little use. Moreover, if what managers are concerned about is

that  the  results  of  basic  research  might  generate  spillovers  to  other  firms,  then  they  might  wish  to

resort  to  industrial  secrecy,  as  is  being  done  by  a  growing  number  of  firms  to  protect  the  results  of

their  applied  research  (Pissano  and  Teece,  2007;  Ottoz  and  Cugno,  2008).  But,  anyway,  firms’

researchers do not need academic publications to gain promotion.

Regarding  public  policy,  our  results  have  two  possible  implications.  Firstly,  since  public  funding  is

limited,  financial  aid  should  not  focus  on  projects  that  are  so  close  to  the  market  that  public

stimulation is practically irrelevant for implementing them. Such projects generate interest per se and

hence it is more difficult to justify public intervention (Arrow, 1962), which under these circumstances

often  ends  only  reducing  costs  at  the  cost  of  distorting  competition.  Things  are  different,  however,

with projects where there is research, particularly basic research (Nelson, 1959). In this case there are

clear  market  failures  which  the  designers  of  public  policies  can  try  to  resolve  by  supporting

knowledge-intensive projects.

We  are  not  of  course  suggesting  that  public  policies  should  aim  to  only  promote  basic  research  in

firms but, just as administrations appreciate universities coming closer to the market, the designers of

public  policies  should  do  everything  possible  to  ensure  that  firms  take  care  to  generate  knowledge

stock  and  flows  amongst  their  technical  staff.  Otherwise,  not  only  will  regions  find  that  their

technologists become increasingly good in increasingly obsolescent areas, techniques and procedures,

but they will also lose opportunities to generate intra and inter-sector spillovers by providing the only

human capital that can solve problems at the forefront of knowledge (Zellner, 2003).

Finally, a second implication of our study for public policy relates to the appropriability of innovation

results. Traditionally, it has been suggested that strong systems to protect results encourage investment

in research and development, so regulators should strengthen industrial and intellectual property rights.

One intriguing result of our study, however, indicates that strong systems of industrial and intellectual

property  also  lead  to  a  smaller  effect  of  basic  research  on  firms’  absorptive  capacity.  This  might

indicate that needs differ depending on the country’s technological capacity. So, international pressure

to  design  strong  appropriability  systems  might  benefit  only  the  most  technologically-advanced

countries.  On  the  other  hand,  the  countries  with  the  greatest  difficulties  from  the  point  of  view  of

technology might require their industrial property rights to be strengthened at a different rate; perhaps

17

they  should  first  strengthen  their  firms’  absorptive  capacity  and,  only  when  investment  in  R&D  is

systematic and guaranteed, should legislators focus their attention on strengthening the appropriability

of innovations.
