## Detailed summary

This policy paper asks whether a comeback of German industry is possible after it has lost technological leadership in its key sectors (automotive, machinery) to China. The paper analyzes three historical case studies of formerly dominant industries that experienced a decline and attempted a turnaround: South Korean shipbuilding, Swedish steel, and the US automotive industry.

The central finding is that **full turnarounds after loss of technological leadership essentially never occur historically**. The realistic outcomes are: (1) a partial turnaround or "Gesundschrumpfung" (healthy downsizing) to a profitable niche level, or (2) failed turnarounds leading to sunset industry status.

The paper is grounded in the sectoral systems framework (Lee & Malerba 2017) and the concept of the **Incumbent Drag / Incumbent Trap** (Chandy & Tellis 2000): established market leaders face structurally higher opportunity costs when switching to new technologies than do new entrants. This creates an NPV asymmetry — the net present value of adopting a disruptive technology is systematically lower for an incumbent than for a new competitor because the incumbent must cannibalize its own existing business model. Even when the incumbent develops the disruptive technology first, this NPV conflict persists and incumbents often have a "gatekeeping incentive" (Pandoras-Box-Logik) to secure knowledge through strategic patents and prevent competitors from scaling.

## Research question

Under what conditions can a country achieve an industrial turnaround after losing technological leadership, and what role does the state play?

## Core argument or contribution

1. **Full turnarounds are historically near-impossible** after another country has become the technology leader in a sector.
2. **Incumbent Drag is the core mechanism**: Incumbents face higher opportunity costs of switching to new technologies, which makes their R&D investment rational in the short run but path-dependent in the long run. This is the micro-level foundation for why the innovation paradox persists and why comebacks are so hard.
3. **Gesundschrumpfung is the most likely outcome**: Downsizing to a profitable level through consolidation, productivity growth, and specialization in higher-value niches.
4. **State intervention is necessary but must be conditional**: Unconditional subsidies ("Überlebenssubventionen") cannot solve the structural problem. Turnarounds require conditionality — state support tied to restructuring, technological redirection, and long-term profitability targets.
5. **The China shock 2.0 differs structurally** from the early-2000s situation: China now exports to Germany rather than being a customer. Supply-side cost reforms (Bürokratieabbau, Lohnstückkosten) are insufficient because the competitive disadvantage is technological, not primarily cost-based.

## Methodology

Qualitative comparative case study analysis. Three cases selected on the criterion that the industry was once dominant and experienced a competitive crisis:
- South Korean shipbuilding (2009–2024): technological specialization in LNG carriers and green ships; state-directed consolidation; partial success.
- Swedish steel (1976–today): two-phase intervention; SSAB creation; HYBRIT initiative; specialization in premium and green steel.
- US automotive (2008–2009): TARP-based "managed bankruptcy"; auto task force; rapid restructuring; partial success without securing long-term sustainability.

## Datasets/materials used

Secondary sources, policy documents, Comtrade trade data, OECD data, industry statistics. No original micro-data analysis.

## Key findings

| Case | State action | Outcome |
|------|-------------|---------|
| South Korea (shipbuilding) | Direct equity + restructuring plan + ECA financing + TRL-conditioned R&D for green ships | Partial turnaround; world-leading productivity; still China-dependent on volume |
| Sweden (steel) | SSAB merger/nationalization + HYBRIT hydrogen steel initiative + Fossil Free Sweden governance | Partial turnaround; premium niche dominant; employment permanently reduced; new market window from green transition |
| USA (automotive) | TARP + managed bankruptcy + auto task force + domestic market stimulus | Partial turnaround; returned to profitability; structural long-term competitiveness remains fragile vs. China |

**Key cross-case conclusions:**
- Conditionality matters: open-ended subsidies failed; performance-linked restructuring succeeded.
- Diversification into adjacent higher-value markets is the route to employment stabilization (not recovery).
- Market opportunity must exist: no turnaround worked without a favorable external market window.
- The state cannot compensate for absent market windows through funding alone.

## Limitations

- Three case studies; no systematic quantitative analysis of success conditions across a broader sample.
- German situation may differ structurally from all three cases (size of the economy, EU embedding, geopolitical context).
- Case selection criteria (formerly dominant industries) explicitly excludes lessons from emerging-industry catch-up stories, which limits external validity for new sector strategies.
- Forward-looking conclusions depend on assumptions about China's continued dominance and market conditions.

## Important concepts discussed

- **Incumbent Drag / Incumbent Trap**: Incumbents face higher NPV costs of switching to disruptive technologies → defensive R&D investment is rational short-term → path dependence → loss of leadership.
- **Pandoras-Box-Logik / Gatekeeping Incentive**: Incumbents may actively suppress new technologies via strategic patents to protect existing business models.
- **Catch-up Cycle / Sectoral Systems** (Lee & Malerba 2017): Technology windows open via disruption, demand shifts, or regulatory change.
- **Gesundschrumpfung**: Profitable downsizing — the most realistic outcome for German Leitindustrien.
- **Sunset Industry**: Failed turnaround leading to industry dissolution.
- **China Shock 2.0**: Structural shift from China as export market to China as export competitor in core German sectors.

## Methods discussed

Comparative case study based on Lee & Malerba (2017) sectoral systems framework. Three-scenario typology: full turnaround, partial turnaround (Gesundschrumpfung), failed turnaround.

## Datasets discussed

No primary datasets. Uses publicly available trade data (UN Comtrade), EIB Investment Survey, Macrobond, industry statistics.

## Why incumbent R&D in incremental improvement makes comebacks so hard

The innovation-paradox argument (rising R&D → weak innovation output → resources flowing to incumbents for incremental improvement) maps directly onto Paleschke's explanation of comeback difficulty:

1. **The NPV asymmetry**: An incumbent auto company that has invested decades in combustion-engine optimization faces a lower NPV on battery investment than a greenfield entrant. Every rational incremental investment deepens this asymmetry.
2. **Gatekeeping**: Even when incumbents develop disruptive technologies, the NPV conflict gives them an incentive to protect existing business models via strategic patents rather than scaling the disruption.
3. **Historical result**: No country in Paleschke's case survey regained full technological leadership after incumbents locked into an obsolete trajectory. The best outcome is profitable downsizing.
4. **Policy implication for the Foerderkatalog**: If public funding flows predominantly to incumbents for high-TRL incremental projects, it risks reproducing the same lock-in. This strengthens the case for examining whether German federal project funding systematically supports defensive incumbent R&D rather than frontier entry and disruption.
