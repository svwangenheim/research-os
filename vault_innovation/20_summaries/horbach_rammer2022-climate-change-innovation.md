## Detailed summary

Horbach and Rammer study whether climate change affects firms' innovation behavior. Their starting point is that climate change can shape firm activity through multiple channels: direct extreme-weather impacts, higher costs of adapting to climate consequences, public policy measures such as regulation or carbon pricing, and customer demand for climate-friendly products. The paper asks whether these channels are merely background conditions or whether they are empirically associated with eco-innovation and other innovation activities.

The core empirical innovation is the use of a new climate-change affectedness measure from the German part of the Community Innovation Survey 2020. The CIS question asks firms how important four climate-change-related factors were for their business during 2018 to 2020: extreme weather, increasing costs or input prices, government climate policies or measures, and customer demand for products that mitigate or adapt to climate change. This measure is available for innovative and non-innovative firms, which lets the authors compare affectedness across innovation types.

Descriptively, the paper shows that climate change was already relevant for a majority of firms in Germany by 2020. In the German CIS sample of 8,462 observations, 28.2 percent of firms reported high importance for at least one of the four climate-change factors, and another 32.6 percent reported at least medium importance without any high-importance factor. The most widespread high-importance channel was increasing costs or input prices at 18.1 percent, followed by government policy or measures at 11.9 percent, customer demand at 7.4 percent, and extreme weather at 7.3 percent. The source reports weighted results for firms with at least five employees in goods-producing industries and business-oriented services.

The econometric analysis uses probit models and propensity-score matching/treatment-effect models. Dependent variables include whether a firm had eco-innovation, whether it had any innovation, and whether it had non-eco product or process innovation after excluding eco-innovators. Eco-innovation is further divided into eco-product and eco-process subtypes. The climate-change affectedness variables are based on high-importance responses for the four climate channels. Controls include firm size, age, R&D, subsidies, cooperation, competition intensity, and sector dummies.

The main result is that all four climate-change affectedness indicators are positively correlated with eco-innovation, and the marginal effects are larger for eco-innovation than for innovation overall. The treatment-effect models support the same pattern. The authors also find effects beyond explicitly environmental innovation. Among firms without eco-innovation, demand for climate-neutral products is associated with non-eco product innovation, while climate-related costs are associated with non-eco process innovation. Climate regulation and weather conditions are not significant drivers of these non-eco innovation categories in that restricted analysis.

For eco-innovation subtypes, the demand channel matters strongly for eco-product innovations, especially products that lower energy consumption. Regulation also helps shift activity toward eco-product innovation. For eco-process innovation, climate policy and demand for climate-neutral products are positively associated with innovations that reduce CO2 emissions or replace fossil energy with renewables.

## Research question

The paper asks how different dimensions of climate-change affectedness are related to firm innovation: do climate policy, climate-related costs, customer demand for climate-friendly products, and extreme weather trigger eco-innovation and do they also affect non-environmental product and process innovation?

## Core argument or contribution

The contribution is to connect a newly available CIS 2020 firm-level affectedness measure to innovation outcomes. The paper argues that climate change is not only a regulatory or environmental policy issue; it creates cost, demand, and physical-risk pressures that are associated with both eco-innovation and broader innovation behavior.

## Methodology

- Descriptive analysis of the German CIS 2020 climate-change affectedness question.
- Probit regressions with binary innovation outcomes.
- Propensity-score matching/treatment-effect models, treating high affectedness by each climate channel as the treatment.
- Separate models for eco-innovation, all innovation, non-eco product innovation, non-eco process innovation, and eco-innovation subtypes.

## Datasets/materials used

- German Community Innovation Survey 2020, corresponding to the German implementation of the CIS and closely related to the Mannheimer Innovationspanel infrastructure.
- The German CIS 2020 sample used for descriptive affectedness results contains 8,462 observations.
- The target population is firms with five or more employees in goods-producing industries and business-oriented services.

## Key findings

- Climate-change affectedness is common: 28.2 percent of firms report high importance for at least one affectedness channel and 32.6 percent report medium importance for at least one channel.
- Increasing costs or input prices are the most common high-importance channel at 18.1 percent.
- Government policy or climate measures are high-importance for 11.9 percent of firms.
- Customer demand for climate-friendly products and extreme weather are each high-importance for about 7 percent of firms.
- All four affectedness indicators are positively related to eco-innovation.
- The relationship with broader innovation is positive but weaker than for eco-innovation.
- Demand for climate-neutral products is significantly important for eco-product innovations.
- Among non-eco innovations, climate-related demand is associated with product innovation and climate-related costs with process innovation.
- The authors interpret these results as evidence that climate change can redirect firm innovation strategies beyond narrowly environmental innovation.

## Limitations

- The paper identifies correlations and treatment-effect estimates based on survey data; it should not be read as definitive causal proof that climate-change affectedness causes innovation.
- Climate-change affectedness is self-assessed by managers and may reflect subjective perception, awareness, or reporting differences.
- The source measures whether firms innovate, not the monetary size, novelty, quality, or emissions impact of the innovation.
- The analysis is based on German CIS 2020 data and the 2018-2020 period, so it may not capture later energy-price shocks or post-2020 policy changes.

## Implications for LLM research or the project domain

For project retrieval, this paper is useful when classifying innovation-policy projects connected to climate, energy, demand-pull, regulation, or firm adaptation. It suggests that project coding should distinguish between climate-policy pressure, climate-cost pressure, demand for climate-friendly products, and physical climate impacts rather than treating all "green innovation" as one channel. Any TRL or funding-allocation interpretation is a project-level inference and is not directly estimated in the paper.
