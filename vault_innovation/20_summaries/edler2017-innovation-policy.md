## Detailed summary

Edler and Fagerberg review the expanding field of public innovation policy. The paper is organized around three questions: what innovation policy is, why governments intervene in innovation, and how innovation policy is designed, implemented, evaluated, and governed. It treats innovation policy broadly, as public policies that affect innovation, not only policies with the word "innovation" in their label.

The paper first defines innovation more broadly than invention. Following the innovation-studies tradition, innovation involves implementing new solutions in response to problems, challenges, or opportunities. Novelty can be new to the world or new to the adopting context, and it can be radical or incremental. The authors emphasize that economic and societal effects depend not only on invention but also on adoption, diffusion, feedback, and subsequent improvement.

The paper distinguishes three types of innovation policy. Mission-oriented policies seek solutions to specific political or societal challenges and must consider the full innovation cycle because success requires practical implementation. Invention-oriented policies focus more narrowly on R&D and invention, often leaving exploitation and diffusion to markets. System-oriented policies focus on system-level features such as interactions, capabilities, complementary resources, bottlenecks, and institutional conditions in innovation systems.

The rationale section contrasts the market-failure approach and the innovation-system approach. The market-failure rationale focuses on underinvestment in knowledge creation caused by public-good characteristics, appropriability problems, and incomplete incentives. It supports public research funding, R&D subsidies, and intellectual-property protection. However, the authors argue that this rationale is too narrow for innovation policy as a whole because it tends to focus on early knowledge creation and gives limited guidance on policy design, implementation, governance failure, diffusion, and systemic bottlenecks.

The innovation-system approach broadens the rationale for intervention. Innovation depends on distributed knowledge, skills, finance, demand, user-producer interaction, networks, institutions, and complementary capabilities. If a system does not provide these functions sufficiently, innovation can be hampered by system failures. This implies a more holistic policy role: governments may need to improve interactions, capabilities, demand conditions, finance, and coordination across policy areas rather than only subsidize R&D or fund basic research.

The policy-instrument section reviews how innovation-policy instruments have evolved. The authors distinguish instruments that affect the supply of innovation from those that affect demand. Supply-side tools include public R&D funding, fiscal incentives, capability-building, skill formation, knowledge transfer, networks, and cluster support. Demand-side tools include public procurement, regulation, standards, and demand articulation. A central lesson is that instrument choice should follow the diagnosed systemic bottleneck, not a generic preference for one instrument type.

The paper also stresses evaluation and governance. Innovation policy must be designed, implemented, evaluated, revised, and coordinated across ministries and levels of government. Because challenge-driven and systemic policies require long time horizons and often involve uncertainty and setbacks, policy capabilities matter. The authors argue that effective innovation policy requires deep contextual understanding of the relevant innovation system and the capacity to learn from implementation.

For the wiki, this is a foundational concept and governance source. It should be used to frame policy rationales, instrument mixes, and evaluation questions. It should not be cited as evidence that a specific German program caused an outcome, that incumbents captured a specific policy, or that TRL stages map directly to policy rationales.

## Research question

What is innovation policy, what rationales justify public intervention in innovation, and how should innovation policy be designed, implemented, evaluated, and governed?

## Core argument or contribution

The paper contributes a structured review of innovation-policy definitions, rationales, instruments, and governance. It argues that innovation policy has moved beyond a narrow market-failure/R&D-subsidy frame toward broader system-oriented and challenge-oriented approaches.

## Methodology

- Conceptual review and policy synthesis.
- Review of innovation-studies literature, policy rationales, and policy-instrument typologies.
- Historical discussion of how innovation policy labels and rationales evolved.

No formal dataset is used. The article reviews academic literature, policy frameworks, and instrument typologies.

## Key findings

- Innovation policy can mean policies explicitly labelled as innovation policy, but also broader public policies that significantly affect innovation.
- Innovation requires implementation and diffusion, not just invention.
- Mission-oriented, invention-oriented, and system-oriented policies have different goals and design requirements.
- Market-failure theory helps justify public funding for knowledge creation, R&D subsidies, and intellectual-property protection, but is too narrow for many innovation-policy problems.
- Innovation-system rationales focus on interactions, capabilities, finance, demand, institutions, and systemic bottlenecks.
- Policy instruments should be selected according to the specific innovation-system problem being addressed.
- Demand-side instruments such as public procurement, standards, and regulation deserve attention alongside supply-side R&D support.
- Evaluation, policy learning, coordination, and policy-maker capabilities are central to effective innovation policy.
- Challenge-driven innovation policy requires long time horizons and tolerance for uncertainty and failure.

## Limitations

- The paper is a conceptual review, not an empirical impact evaluation.
- It does not provide program-level evidence on German federal funding.
- It does not define TRL levels or project-level coding rules.
- It does not prove that any particular policy instrument is superior in all contexts.
- Applying its framework requires diagnosis of the specific innovation system, bottleneck, and policy context.

## Implications for LLM research or the project domain

For the Bundesinnovationshaushalt/TRL project, this source provides the conceptual map for distinguishing funding rationales and instruments. It helps separate questions such as "is there an R&D market failure?", "is there a system bottleneck?", "is demand missing?", and "is the policy instrument matched to the problem?" It supports a richer evaluation frame than counting project spending alone, but it does not itself determine whether a German project is early-stage, late-stage, radical, incremental, or effective.
