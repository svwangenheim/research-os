## Detailed summary

Falck and Pfaffl study the structural transformation of German manufacturing by adapting the Boston Consulting Group Growth-Share Matrix from product-level business strategy to sector-level industrial analysis. Instead of asking whether an individual firm has "stars," "cash cows," or declining products, they ask whether German manufacturing sectors combine current economic weight with future-oriented product portfolios. The vertical dimension is each two-digit manufacturing sector's share of total manufacturing gross value added in 2023. The horizontal dimension is a product-growth balance: for each sector, the authors compare the production-value share of strongly growing product groups with the production-value share of strongly shrinking product groups over 2019-2024. Strongly growing and strongly shrinking products are identified at the 9-digit product level using the upper and lower quartiles of production-value change across product groups. The resulting four groups classify sectors as economically important but shrinking, economically important and growing, less important and shrinking, or less important but growing. The paper's headline finding is cautiously optimistic: around 76 percent of German manufacturing gross value added is in sectors where strongly growing products outweigh strongly shrinking products. This suggests that many parts of German manufacturing have already begun to adjust their product portfolios. The optimism is qualified by a sectoral warning. Germany's industrial strength remains concentrated in mid-technology sectors such as machinery, automotive, and chemicals. These sectors are characterized by incremental innovation that improves existing product portfolios rather than generating disruptive technologies or new business models. The authors explicitly connect this to the "Mitteltechnologie-Falle": institutional framework conditions and public innovation programs have adapted to this incremental system, but this is insufficient in a period of deep structural change. The automotive sector is the clearest risk case. It remains the largest manufacturing sector by gross value added share, but its product portfolio is classified as dominated by shrinking products. Electric vehicles have grown very strongly in production value, but from a still limited base within the sector, and their higher use of external inputs can reduce domestic value-added depth. By contrast, pharmaceuticals and data-processing/electronic/optical equipment appear as growing high-technology fields with comparatively lower current weight but stronger product-growth balances.

## Research question

Which German manufacturing sectors combine current economic importance with future-oriented product portfolios, and which sectors risk losing long-term economic importance because their product portfolios are dominated by shrinking products?

## Core argument or contribution

The paper contributes a sectoral portfolio diagnostic for German manufacturing. It shows that Germany's industrial transformation problem is not a uniform decline of all manufacturing, but a portfolio problem: many sectors have growing products, while central incumbent sectors, especially automotive, face a negative product-growth balance. The policy contribution is to connect this diagnosis to a warning against structurally preserving existing mid-technology strengths at the expense of disruptive innovation and entrepreneurial renewal.

## Methodology

- Descriptive sectoral portfolio analysis, not causal identification.
- Adaptation of the BCG Growth-Share Matrix to two-digit German manufacturing sectors.
- Vertical axis: nominal gross value added share of each sector in total manufacturing gross value added in 2023.
- Horizontal axis: product-growth balance from 2019 to 2024, calculated as the production-value share of strongly growing product groups minus the share of strongly shrinking product groups.
- Product-level growth classification: 9-digit GP 2019 product groups; top quartile classified as strongly growing, bottom quartile as strongly shrinking.
- Sector classification: four groups based on current economic weight and product-growth balance.
- Technology classification: Eurostat technology classification using R&D expenditure and patents.

## Key findings

- Around 76 percent of manufacturing gross value added is in sectors with a positive product-growth balance.
- The automotive sector is economically central but classified as high-importance/shrinking; this reflects the depth of its transformation challenge.
- Electric vehicles have grown very strongly in production value since 2019, but still represent too small a share of automotive production to offset the broader shrinking-product balance.
- Machinery remains economically important and has a more positive product portfolio, partly because of specialized niches such as machinery for semiconductor manufacturing.
- Pharmaceuticals and data-processing/electronic/optical equipment show strong positive product-growth balances but still have lower current gross-value-added weight.
- The paper frames Germany's strength in mid-technology sectors as a risk when incremental innovation and structurally preserving policy crowd out disruptive innovation.
- Policy should support transition, entrepreneurship, disruptive innovation, capital-market conditions, tax and regulatory settings for start-ups, and labor-market flexibility rather than only preserving existing structures.

## Limitations

- The analysis is descriptive and does not estimate causal effects of policy, R&D funding, or sectoral structure.
- The product-growth balance uses nominal production values, so price effects can influence classification.
- The 2019-2024 period includes major shocks, including pandemic and energy-price effects.
- The sector-level matrix abstracts from within-sector firm heterogeneity and from global value-chain/value-added depth.
- The paper discusses public innovation programs at a strategic level but does not evaluate individual programs or funding datasets.

## Implications for LLM research or the project domain

For [[70_projects/bundesinnovationshaushalt-trl.md]], the paper provides a sector-level plausibility check for the project's TRL and funding-allocation hypotheses. If federal funding is concentrated in high-TRL projects and incumbent mid-technology sectors, it may reinforce the Mitteltechnologie-Falle rather than shift resources toward disruptive innovation. The paper should not be used as direct evidence on Förderkatalog project effects, but it is highly relevant for framing sector classifications, interpreting automotive and machinery funding, and explaining why innovation policy should distinguish incremental transformation from disruptive renewal.
