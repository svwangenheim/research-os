## Detailed summary

Rothgang and Lageman analyze why Germany struggles to translate scientific research into high-tech commercial success. The paper is motivated by the 2025 coalition agreement and federal high-tech agenda, which aim to strengthen Germany as an industrial, AI, and startup location. The authors argue that these goals require a much stronger transfer of applied basic research from universities and research organizations into firms, startups, and marketable innovations.

The source defines innovation transfer as the translation of applied basic-research results into concrete innovations. It lists several transfer mechanisms: spin-offs and new firms, patent and rights exploitation, university-industry research cooperation, contract research, licensing and commercialization of protected knowledge, personnel mobility, and broader knowledge exchange. The paper's concern is not that Germany has no transfer system, but that its system works better for incremental and mid-tech improvement than for high-tech scale-up.

The central diagnosis is a mid-tech/high-tech imbalance. Germany has a strong innovation system in established sectors such as automotive, mechanical engineering, chemicals, and mid-tech electronics. These sectors benefit from long-term relationships, applied engineering strength, and incremental improvement. The same institutional strengths do not automatically produce fast high-tech transfer in fields such as artificial intelligence, biotechnology, and quantum technology. The authors argue that Germany has excellent research and many transfer activities, but too few successful high-tech spin-offs and too little dynamism in turning research into globally competitive new firms.

The comparison countries are the USA, Switzerland, Sweden, and the United Kingdom. They were chosen because they rank ahead of Germany in the Global Innovation Ranking and share enough institutional or cultural similarity to make comparison meaningful. The authors use the comparison to emphasize that transfer performance is not mainly the result of one single program. Successful systems combine long-run institutional development, strong universities or research organizations, startup finance, entrepreneurial culture, professional transfer organizations, and public-private coordination.

The USA is presented as especially strong in high-tech entrepreneurship, venture-capital ecosystems, federal laboratory and university transfer, and SBIR/STTR-style programs. Switzerland is relevant because ETH Zurich, EPFL, Innosuisse, and the swiTT technology-transfer association form a coherent, professionally visible transfer environment despite federalism. Sweden is discussed as a coordinated innovation system with strong university-industry cooperation, Vinnova, research institutes, SNITTS, and a culture of collaboration around industrial transformation. The UK is highlighted for Knowledge Transfer Partnerships, Technology Transfer Offices, and venture-capital/equity-gap instruments.

The study's recommendations follow from this systemic diagnosis. For policy, it stresses stronger high-tech transfer strategies, reduced bureaucracy in funding procedures, better access to research and innovation data, stronger venture-capital and startup financing, and more professional transfer organizations. For science and research institutions, it emphasizes entrepreneurial culture, professional transfer capacity, clearer incentives for spin-offs and cooperation, and better use of international best practices without copying institutions mechanically.

## Research question

What explains the stronger innovation-transfer performance of selected comparison countries, and what can Germany learn from their systems to improve high-tech transfer from science to business?

## Core argument or contribution

The paper argues that Germany's transfer problem is systemic. Germany's established mid-tech model supports incremental innovation well, but high-tech transfer requires a different environment: entrepreneurial culture, professional transfer organizations, risk capital, data access, low-bureaucracy funding, and coordinated science-business-state interaction.

## Methodology

This is a policy and comparative innovation-systems study. It synthesizes secondary indicators, literature, policy documents, country cases, and selected best-practice examples from the USA, Switzerland, Sweden, and the UK. It is not an econometric evaluation and does not estimate causal effects of individual transfer programs.

## Datasets/materials used

- WIPO Global Innovation Index 2025 and related innovation-system indicators.
- OECD and other secondary innovation-policy sources cited in the report.
- Country and institutional material on the USA, Switzerland, Sweden, and the UK.
- Examples including SBIR/STTR, Knowledge Transfer Partnerships, Technology Transfer Offices, Innosuisse, swiTT, SNITTS, Vinnova, ETH Zurich, and EPFL.

## Key findings

- Germany has strong mid-tech transfer and incremental innovation capabilities but weaker high-tech transfer dynamics.
- The main high-tech gaps concern fields such as AI, biotechnology, and quantum technologies.
- Successful transfer systems are built over time and depend on interaction between science, firms, finance, culture, and public institutions.
- Entrepreneurship culture and positive attitudes toward technology are treated as central enabling conditions.
- Venture capital and startup ecosystems are key for high-tech commercialization; Germany is portrayed as having catch-up needs.
- Bureaucracy and weak data access are barriers to transfer.
- The comparison countries offer useful examples, but the source cautions implicitly through its systemic framing that Germany cannot simply copy isolated programs without adapting the broader system.

## Limitations

- The study is a policy synthesis and comparative diagnosis, not a causal impact evaluation.
- Best-practice transferability is uncertain because the source emphasizes historically grown national systems.
- The report relies on secondary indicators and examples rather than a unified quantitative model of transfer performance.
- Its recommendations are oriented toward German high-tech transfer and should not be generalized to all innovation-policy domains without checking fit.
