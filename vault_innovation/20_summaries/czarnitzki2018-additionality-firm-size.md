## Detailed summary

Czarnitzki and Hussinger (2018) provide a German firm-level analysis of whether R&D subsidies generate input additionality (increased total R&D spending) or crowd out private spending, and whether they produce output additionality (increased patents or innovation). Using matched firm data from German innovation surveys merged with subsidy receipt records, they find positive additionality on average but with firm-size heterogeneity.

**Key finding for the project:** Small firms show stronger additionality effects than large firms. This firm-size heterogeneity has a direct implication: if large firms receive a disproportionate share of German grants (as documented by Aschhoff 2010), the realized additionality of that allocation is lower than what could be achieved with better-targeted allocation to smaller firms.

The trl_research_briefing uses this paper to support two arguments:

1. **Self-selection confirmation:** Firms already successful at innovation are more likely to win grants, reinforcing incumbent advantage in the German grant system.

2. **Firm-size × TRL interaction:** Combined with Aschhoff (2010), the firm-size additionality heterogeneity implies that the German grant portfolio — which Aschhoff shows is skewed toward large firms — produces below-potential social returns. If this large-firm bias is also concentrated at high TRL (which the Förderprojekte classification will test), the three-pillar innovation paradox is confirmed: (a) large firms win, (b) non-high-tech sectors dominate, (c) near-market activities are funded — all three conditions reducing the social return to public investment.

## Research question

Do German R&D subsidies generate input additionality and output additionality, and is additionality heterogeneous by firm size?

## Core argument or contribution

German R&D subsidies produce positive additionality on average, but additionality is stronger for small firms than for large firms. This means the observed large-firm bias in grant allocation depletes realized social returns and amplifies the innovation paradox.

## Key findings

- Positive input additionality on average: R&D subsidies increase total R&D spending
- Firm-size heterogeneity: **small firms show stronger additionality** than large firms
- Output additionality confirmed for patents, with size variation
- Self-selection problem: firms already successful at innovation are more likely to receive grants
- Implication: large-firm dominance in grant receipt depletes realized additionality below its potential

## Limitations
