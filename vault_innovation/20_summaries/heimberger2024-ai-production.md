## Detailed summary

Heimberger, Jaeger and Maloca examine the state of artificial intelligence use in German manufacturing production. The paper starts from the claim that AI can improve production tasks where digital data and self-learning algorithms can identify patterns, anomalies, recommendations, or optimization opportunities. The relevant production applications include quality control, maintenance, internal logistics, production-process control and optimization, energy management, and continuous process improvement.

The empirical contribution is descriptive. Using the 2022 wave of the Fraunhofer ISI survey Modernisierung der Produktion, the authors show that AI has entered German manufacturing but remains far from universal. Sixteen percent of surveyed production sites use at least one AI application in production. Around one tenth of firms report plans to implement AI by 2025; the source distinguishes this from first-time adoption and states that 8 percent of firms without AI plan to introduce it by 2025. Existing AI users are more likely to expand their use: about 20 percent of current users plan additional AI applications, whereas just under 10 percent of non-users plan first adoption.

Adoption is uneven across firm types and production contexts. Large firms with at least 500 employees are the most active users: 29 percent already use AI in production and another 13 percent plan first adoption by 2025. Smaller and medium-sized firms use AI less often, with 13 percent adoption among firms with fewer than 50 employees, 18 percent among firms with 50 to 99 employees, and 16 percent among firms with 100 to 499 employees. Planned first adoption is also lower among smaller firms, especially the below-50-employees group at 5 percent. The paper concludes that the gap between large firms and smaller firms is likely to widen in the near term.

The industry pattern is also uneven. Vehicle construction is the current leader, with roughly every third firm using AI in production. Chemicals and pharmaceuticals report much lower current use, at 8 percent. Other industries cluster between 13 and 18 percent. Planned first adoption suggests that the distribution may change: the electrical industry shows particularly high planned entry at 19 percent, while chemicals, textiles/clothing, and mechanical engineering also report noticeable planned adoption.

Production characteristics matter. AI adoption is higher among mass and large-series producers than among small/medium-series or single-series producers. Firms producing complex products use AI more often than firms producing medium-complexity or simple products. Parts and system suppliers also show higher AI use than end-product manufacturers or contract manufacturers. The source interprets these patterns as evidence that AI is more common where firms have production settings in which data-rich, repeated, or complex processes make AI applications easier to integrate.

The paper's strongest interpretive point is that AI depends on prior digitalization and skills. Firms classified as more advanced in Fraunhofer's Industry 4.0 readiness index use AI more often than basic users or non-users. The paper also gives application-specific evidence: firms already using digital internal-logistics automation are much more likely to use AI in internal logistics, and firms with real-time production-control systems are more likely to use AI for energy management. Training patterns reinforce this view. Firms that use AI, and firms that plan to introduce it, provide production workers with training more often than firms without AI, including training on digital systems, data security and compliance, creativity and innovation, and cross-functional skills.

## Research question

The source asks how widely AI applications are already used or planned in German manufacturing production, which types of firms and production environments adopt them, which production applications are most common, whether AI adoption is linked to digitalized production systems, and what role workforce training plays.

## Core argument or contribution

The paper contributes a descriptive map of production-AI adoption in German manufacturing. Its core argument is that AI use is structurally conditioned: adoption is more likely where firms have scale, complex or repeated production processes, digital process data, Industry 4.0 capabilities, and training practices that prepare workers for new digital systems.

## Methodology

The paper uses descriptive analysis of the 2022 Fraunhofer ISI survey Modernisierung der Produktion. The source reports adoption shares and planned-adoption shares by firm size, industry, production mode, product complexity, value-chain position, application area, Industry 4.0 readiness, and training provision. It does not present a causal identification strategy in the converted source.

## Datasets/materials used

- Erhebung Modernisierung der Produktion 2022, Fraunhofer ISI.
- The source states that 14,045 German manufacturing establishments were contacted and 1,334 usable questionnaires were returned by September 2022, a response rate of 9 percent.
- The responding firms cover major manufacturing branches, including metalworking, mechanical engineering, electrical/electronics, rubber/plastics, food, and chemicals, and are mostly small and medium-sized establishments.

## Key findings

- AI is used in production by 16 percent of German manufacturing establishments in the survey.
- Eight percent of firms without AI plan first adoption by 2025; current users are especially likely to expand AI use.
- Large firms lead adoption, with 29 percent current use among firms with at least 500 employees and 13 percent planned first adoption by 2025.
- Smaller firms are less dynamic: firms with fewer than 50 employees show 13 percent current AI use and 5 percent planned first adoption.
- Vehicle construction is the current sectoral leader; chemicals/pharmaceuticals have low current AI use, while the electrical industry has high planned first adoption.
- AI use is higher in mass or large-series production, complex-product production, and parts/system supplier settings.
- The most discussed application areas include production-process control, quality control, internal logistics, maintenance, energy management, and continuous process improvement.
- Firms with stronger Industry 4.0 readiness and more digital production systems use AI more often.
- AI users and planned AI adopters report more workforce training for production employees than non-users.

## Limitations

- The evidence is descriptive and should not be used as causal evidence that digitalization or training causes AI adoption.
- Planned adoption to 2025 is self-reported and may not equal realized implementation.
- The source is limited to German manufacturing production and does not cover AI use in services or non-production business functions.
- The AI category is broad and self-reported; it groups multiple production applications rather than evaluating a single technical system.
- The survey response rate is 9 percent, so representativeness depends on the survey design and weighting choices not fully detailed in the converted source.
