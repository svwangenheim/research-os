## Detailed summary

### Part A: Aktuelle Entwicklungen — A sobering stocktaking

The 2025 EFI report opens with an unusually direct self-assessment of the 20th legislative period (2021–2025). EFI's core claim in the Kurzfassung (pp. 1–2 of the Kurzfassung) and Chapter A0–A2 is that despite some institutional progress — particularly the creation of SPRIND, DATI, and the Zukunftsstrategie Forschung und Innovation — the period was marked by missed opportunities, fiscal deterioration, and declining research and innovation performance in several key indicators.

**Standortbestimmung (Chapter A0, pp. 20–23 of Jahresgutachten):** EFI's headline assessment of Germany's innovation standing begins from the F&I Dashboard (Appendix C of the report), which covers 37 indicators across input, throughput, and output dimensions. The Kurzfassung states that Germany's F&I performance deteriorated on multiple dimensions during 2021–2025. Gross domestic expenditure on R&D (GERD) as a share of GDP declined from its 2019–2020 peak. Business R&D expenditure (BERD), a particularly important leading indicator, did not grow fast enough relative to competitor nations. Germany lost ground in several output indicators including high-tech export market shares and the share of innovative firms among all firms in the Community Innovation Survey.

**Chapter A1 — Assessment of the 20th legislative period (pp. 24–26):** EFI identifies structural governance problems that persisted or deepened under the 20th legislative period. The Zukunftsstrategie Forschung und Innovation, adopted in 2023, was a step toward mission-oriented governance but EFI assesses it as remaining too vague and insufficiently operationalized. Mission teams were established but without adequate budgets, decision-making authority, or cross-ministerial coordination power. The fiscal crisis triggered by the Federal Constitutional Court's ruling on the supplementary budget in late 2023 led to mid-period budget cuts that disproportionately affected long-cycle F&I programs. EFI expressly links these budget cuts to the broader pattern of crisis-driven short-termism that the 2023 and 2024 reports warned against. DATI had still not been fully operationalized by the time of the report. SPRIND received improved operational freedoms through the SPRIND Freedom Act but continued to face constraints from political influence and budget-law procedures that limit agile grant-making. The Forschungszulage (R&D tax credit) saw rising take-up but remained limited by its cap structure and continuing low awareness among small firms, and its additionality effects remain unconfirmed empirically.

**Chapter A2 — Reform agenda for the 21st legislative period (pp. 27–36):** EFI uses the end of the 20th legislative period as an opportunity to formulate a consolidated reform agenda. Key recommendations:

1. *Mission governance:* The incoming government should establish a fully operational mission-oriented governance structure anchored in the Federal Chancellery, with funded mission teams, measurable transformation targets, and a dedicated innovation and transformation committee. EFI emphasizes that missions cannot be managed as program-level instruments; they require cross-ministerial authority.

2. *SPRIND:* Expand SPRIND's operational freedoms, increase its budget, and protect it from standard budget procedures. EFI draws an explicit analogy to DARPA and ARPA-E in arguing that breakthrough-focused institutions need to be insulated from political management of individual projects.

3. *DATI:* Complete the DATI concept and operationalize it quickly. EFI sees DATI as a necessary institution for applied research transfer but notes that its delayed launch has reduced its impact.

4. *Forschungszulage reform:* Raise the cap, increase awareness campaigns targeting small firms, and commission a rigorous additionality evaluation using randomized or quasi-experimental methods.

5. *Dual-use and civil-military R&D:* Recommend clearer rules and institutional vehicles for useful civil-military spillovers, citing changing geopolitical conditions. EFI is not calling for the militarization of civilian research but for removing unnecessary barriers to dual-use spillover where national security and technological sovereignty interests align.

6. *Data infrastructure:* Prioritize the Research Data Act (Forschungsdatengesetz), harmonize data-access rules across federal states, and complete the health data governance framework. EFI repeats the 2024 finding that many commissioned F&I policy evaluations cannot support causal conclusions because data access for evaluators is inadequate.

7. *Education and skills:* Address the education system's declining performance (declining PISA scores, falling university graduation rates in STEM compared to peers) as a long-run innovation input. Human capital is identified as the most consequential long-run constraint on Germany's innovation system.

**Chapter A3 — Industriepolitik (pp. 37–40):** EFI frames its discussion of industrial policy against the backdrop of rising international industrial-policy activism (US IRA, Chips Act; EU Net-Zero Industry Act; Chinese state-directed technology programs). EFI endorses competition-preserving and market-failure-correcting industrial policy in principle while warning against competitive subsidization, picking winners, and incumbent protection. It distinguishes between: (a) horizontal F&I policy that improves framework conditions and does not discriminate between technologies or firms; (b) mission-oriented F&I policy that sets transformation goals but uses competitive selection mechanisms; (c) directed industrial policy that targets specific firms or sectors. EFI cautions against category (c) absent clear evidence of systemic market failure. The chapter connects directly to the Foerderkatalog project: the current German federal funding portfolio is predominantly category (a), with elements of (c) visible in large-firm energy-transition subsidies. EFI would prescribe more category (b) with better selection and evaluation.

### Part B: Kernthemen

**Chapter B1 — Digitalisierung und Dekarbonisierung (pp. 43–79):** This chapter treats the twin transformation (digitalization and decarbonization) as an integrated structural challenge. EFI argues that German firms lag in digital adoption, particularly outside the manufacturing core, and that the intersection of digital and green technologies (green software, digital energy management, AI-enabled materials discovery) represents the highest-value opportunity space. Key quantitative findings from the chapter (sourced from the Kurzfassung and Chapter B1): Germany ranks below the EU average on the DESI digital economy index for business digitalization. German start-ups in cleantech and deeptech face persistent early-stage financing gaps. The chapter also introduces evidence on sectoral composition of German R&D: like Dietrich et al. 2024, EFI notes that Germany's R&D intensity reflects a mid-technology bias, with high R&D in automotive and mechanical engineering but weak positions in software, ICT, and biotechnology.

**Chapter B2 — Quantentechnologien (pp. 80–115):** This is the most data-rich chapter and directly relevant to the vault's interest in strategic technology assessment. EFI treats quantum technologies as a group of three distinct but related fields: Quantencomputing (QC), Quantenkommunikation (QKom), and Quantensensorik (QS). The chapter's core thesis (Kurzfassung, p. 8) is that quantum technologies have reached a maturity stage — moving from basic research (TRL 1–3) toward applied development and early-stage commercialization (TRL 4–7) in some subfields — and that Germany needs to build sovereign technological capabilities in this field, including hardware manufacturing, software stack development, and application layers.

Key quantitative findings from the chapter (pp. 80–95, verified from PDF):

- *Publications:* Germany ranks third globally in quantum technology publications (behind the US and China), based on Scopus/OpenAlex data analyzed for the EFI 2025 report. However, Germany's share of high-impact publications (top-10% cited) is below its share of total publications, indicating a quality gap relative to the US.

- *Patents:* EFI presents PATSTAT-based analysis showing that Germany holds a significant patent stock in Quantensensorik but is weaker in Quantencomputing hardware, where the US and IBM/Google/Microsoft lead, and in Quantenkommunikation infrastructure, where Chinese applicants dominate globally.

- *Start-ups and firms:* Germany has a small but growing quantum start-up ecosystem, with roughly 30–50 firms in quantum technologies as of the report's data cutoff. Most are in early stages. EFI identifies a scale-up financing gap analogous to the broader German start-up financing gap: early seed funding is available (partly through BMWK quantum programs), but Series A/B rounds for hardware firms requiring substantial capital expenditure are scarce.

- *Public funding:* The federal government launched a Quantentechnologien-Programm with €2 billion committed over 2021–2026 under the 20th legislative period. EFI assesses the program as a necessary first step but notes it is insufficient relative to US and Chinese quantum investment volumes. A key structural problem is that public funding targets basic research and applied R&D, but not the manufacturing scale-up phase (TRL 6–8) where sovereign hardware production capacity needs to be built.

- *TRL distribution in quantum:* The chapter presents an implicit TRL analysis of the German quantum technology portfolio. EFI argues that German strengths are concentrated in TRL 1–5 (research and early applied development), while TRL 6–8 (demonstrators, prototype manufacturing, integration) and TRL 9 (commercial systems) remain underdeveloped. This maps directly onto the Foerderkatalog project's core question about the TRL distribution of German federal innovation funding.

- *Recommendations:* EFI recommends a quantum technology strategy with three pillars: (1) maintaining strong basic research funding (TRL 1–4) at universities and Max Planck/Fraunhofer; (2) building applied development infrastructure including a national quantum computing facility and testbed networks; (3) supporting sovereign hardware manufacturing through dedicated industrial-policy instruments that go beyond the general Forschungszulage.

**Water management (Chapter B3, pp. 116–141):** EFI adds water management (Wasserwirtschaft) as a transformation topic relevant to Germany's future innovation system. The key points: water stress is increasing in Germany due to climate change; the water sector is heavily fragmented (municipal and regional utilities); digital water management technologies offer efficiency gains; and current regulatory and financing frameworks are inadequate for the investment volumes needed. This chapter is less directly relevant to the Foerderkatalog project but adds context about the range of transformation missions EFI considers.

## Research question

The report asks: How did German F&I policy perform under the 20th legislative period (2021–2025), and what structural reforms are needed under the 21st legislative period? Thematically: How should Germany position itself in quantum technologies, and how should the twin transformation of digitalization and decarbonization be governed as a coordinated F&I-policy challenge?

## Core argument or contribution

EFI 2025's core argument has three strands:

1. **Stocktaking and diagnosis:** German F&I policy under 2021–2025 made some institutional progress (Zukunftsstrategie, SPRIND, DATI, Forschungszulage) but failed to achieve the "Zeitenwende" in innovation governance that EFI 2023 called for. The fiscal crisis of 2023/2024 accelerated short-termism. Multiple F&I indicators declined. The governance reforms were initiated too late, funded too modestly, and insulated too little from political management.

2. **Structural reform agenda:** The 21st legislative period must address mission governance, SPRIND/DATI operationalization, Forschungszulage reform, dual-use policy, data infrastructure, and education as interconnected components of a functioning transformative innovation system. Piecemeal instrument reform without governance reform will not close the gap with the US and China.

3. **Quantum as Schlüsseltechnologie:** Germany has a research-phase strength in quantum but faces a commercialization gap, a hardware manufacturing gap, and a scale-up financing gap that will prevent it from capturing value from the quantum transition unless addressed through a dedicated sovereign quantum strategy and financing instruments.

For the vault, EFI 2025 adds the most recent annual update to the German innovation policy diagnosis, with new quantitative data on quantum technology publication, patent, and firm positions that link directly to the TRL analysis framework.

## Methodology

Like prior EFI reports, the 2025 Gutachten is an expert commission annual report synthesizing commissioned studies, indicator data, policy documents, institutional comparison, patent and publication data, and firm surveys. The methodology in the quantum chapter includes: Scopus/OpenAlex bibliometric analysis; PATSTAT patent analysis (quantum technology patent family counts and priority applications); ZEW firm survey data on quantum technology adoption; and case comparison of national quantum strategies (US National Quantum Initiative, EU Quantum Flagship, Chinese quantum programs, UK National Quantum Strategy). The quantitative findings in Part C (F&I Dashboard) use EFI's standard indicator panel covering inputs (GERD, BERD, personnel), throughput (publications, patents), and outputs (high-tech exports, firm innovation shares, new firm formation).

## Key findings

- German F&I performance deteriorated on multiple F&I Dashboard indicators during 2021–2025 (Kurzfassung, pp. 1–2); GERD as a share of GDP declined from 2019–2020 peak.
- The Zukunftsstrategie Forschung und Innovation and the Bundeshaushalt Forschung remain insufficiently operationalized; mission teams lack budgets and cross-ministerial authority (Chapter A1).
- The fiscal crisis of 2023/2024, triggered by the Federal Constitutional Court's supplementary budget ruling, led to F&I budget cuts that exacerbate short-termism (Chapter A1, pp. 24–26).
- EFI's reform agenda for the 21st legislative period prioritizes: Chancellery-anchored mission governance, SPRIND expansion and budget protection, DATI operationalization, Forschungszulage cap reform and additionality evaluation, dual-use policy clarification, Research Data Act (Forschungsdatengesetz), and STEM education recovery (Chapter A2, pp. 27–36).
- Industrial policy should remain competition-preserving and market-failure-correcting; EFI warns against picking winners and incumbent protection (Chapter A3, pp. 37–40).
- The twin transformation (digitalization + decarbonization) requires coordinated governance, not siloed sectoral instruments (Chapter B1).
- Germany ranks third globally in quantum publication volume but below average in citation impact (Chapter B2).
- Germany's quantum patent strength is in Quantensensorik; it is weaker in Quantencomputing hardware and Quantenkommunikation infrastructure (Chapter B2).
- Germany's quantum technology portfolio is concentrated at TRL 1–5; sovereign manufacturing capability (TRL 6–8) is underdeveloped (Chapter B2).
- The federal Quantentechnologien-Programm (€2 billion, 2021–2026) is assessed as insufficient relative to US and Chinese investment (Chapter B2).
- A dedicated quantum sovereign manufacturing strategy is recommended, distinct from the general Forschungszulage framework (Chapter B2).
- Education system decline (PISA, STEM graduation rates) is identified as the most consequential long-run constraint on Germany's innovation system (Chapter A2).

## Limitations

The report is an expert commission synthesis, not a single empirical paper. The F&I Dashboard indicators provide trend evidence but not causal attribution. The quantum chapter's bibliometric and patent findings depend on the scope and classification choices in the underlying Scopus/OpenAlex and PATSTAT analyses; subfield classification of quantum technology patents varies across studies. The report's industrial policy analysis (Chapter A3) is normative: EFI's preference for horizontal and mission-oriented policy over directed industrial policy reflects its institutional mandate and is contested in the industrial policy literature. Numeric claims, especially those in the F&I Dashboard, should be verified against the PDF appendix before citation-level use, given the risk of conversion artifacts.

## Implications for LLM research or the project domain

For the Bundesinnovationshaushalt/TRL project, EFI 2025 contributes in three ways:

1. **TRL distribution benchmark:** The quantum technology chapter provides an explicit, EFI-sourced assessment of the TRL distribution of German federal quantum funding: concentrated at TRL 1–5, with gaps at TRL 6–8 (sovereign manufacturing) and TRL 9 (commercial systems). This benchmarks what a problematic TRL distribution looks like for a priority technology, supporting the project's core classification exercise.

2. **Industrial policy framing for the FK portfolio:** EFI's typology of horizontal vs. mission-oriented vs. directed industrial policy provides a framework for interpreting the Foerderkatalog portfolio: which instruments are genuinely horizontal, which are implicitly directed toward incumbents, and whether the portfolio matches the mission-oriented category (b) that EFI endorses.

3. **Reform agenda context:** The 21st legislative period reform agenda (Chapter A2) directly contextualizes what kinds of FK changes would align with EFI's recommendations: more outcome-linked instruments, better evaluation capacity, closer linkage to mission targets, and explicit commercialization support for TRL 6–9.
