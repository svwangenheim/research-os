## Detailed summary

This report evaluates the German Zentrales Innovationsprogramm Mittelstand (ZIM) under the 2015 guideline. It was conducted between July 2018 and May 2019 and focuses on program design and the first three years of implementation under the 2015 ZIM guideline. ZIM is described as one of the major German innovation-policy instruments for application-oriented R&D in SMEs. Since its launch in July 2008 through June 2018, the program approved around 40,500 R&D projects, with EUR 5.5 billion in grants and at least EUR 10.3 billion in total project volume. Firms received EUR 3.4 billion in grants and invested at least EUR 8.3 billion. More than 208,000 R&D staff were involved in ZIM projects over the period. The evaluation emphasizes that ZIM has a distinctive design. It combines three program pillars under one roof: single-firm R&D projects, R&D cooperation projects, and innovation networks. The report argues that this combination is unusual internationally because direct R&D projects and network-building are connected through a common program structure. ZIM is bottom-up, thematically open, focused on application-oriented R&D in SMEs, and relatively administratively lean. It is distinct from EU programs, which usually require larger projects and higher novelty thresholds, and from many state-level programs, which may overlap but differ by instrument, supported phases, cost categories, and administrative burden. The report's main empirical finding is positive but qualified. ZIM has measurable positive effects on R&D inputs and cooperation, but it works more "in depth" than "in breadth." The strongest additionality appears among already R&D-active or at least R&D-capable firms, where the program helps maintain or expand R&D intensity. The report estimates, using conditional difference-in-differences approaches where possible, that ZIM increases R&D-to-sales intensity by roughly 4 to 6 percentage points for a representative firm during the project period. Starting from an average R&D-to-sales intensity of about 4.3 percent, this is roughly a doubling. The report also finds positive effects on R&D spending, R&D employment, and R&D employment intensity, while warning that small sample sizes limit the robustness of some econometric estimates. At the same time, the evaluation identifies a key limitation for the innovation-paradox question: ZIM does not primarily draw many non-R&D firms into R&D. The report states that the effects are mainly at the level of additional R&D in firms that already conduct R&D regularly or irregularly. Under a narrow definition, ZIM induces entry into R&D for only about 3 percent of firms. This matters because Germany's aggregate R&D inputs can rise while the number of innovation-active firms declines. The report explicitly notes the broader context: Germany's R&D spending exceeded the 3 percent of GDP target in 2017, but the number of innovation-active firms had declined, and innovation activity had become more concentrated among fewer large firms and R&D-intensive SMEs. The report also shows that ZIM reaches first-time applicants at a meaningful rate. Under the 2015 guideline, first-time funding applicants accounted for 42 percent of submissions and 47 percent of firms, although this may be somewhat overstated due to firm demography, mergers, and restructurings. Single-firm projects contributed disproportionately to first-time participation. This supports the idea that ZIM remains open, but it does not fully offset the finding that the program's strongest impact is on firms already near the R&D margin rather than firms far outside formal R&D. Cooperation is a major positive mechanism. ZIM projects, especially cooperation projects and network-derived projects, often originate from sources outside the firm. For single-firm projects, about 10 percent of project ideas come from outside the firm; for cooperation projects between firms and research institutions, the share is above 50 percent; for projects from networks, it is nearly 75 percent. The report argues that ZIM increases cooperation, knowledge exchange, and follow-on collaborations among firms and research institutions. Research institutions also benefit through application-oriented knowledge, further R&D cooperation with firms, publications, and teaching. The evaluation distinguishes direct and indirect effects. Direct effects are assessed with control-group comparisons and survey evidence; indirect effects include technology and market spillovers. Positive spillovers appear especially in firm-firm cooperation projects and single-firm projects, including higher demand among customers and suppliers and effects on the state of the art in the relevant field or industry. The report also notes displacement risks for more than half of firms, with somewhat higher risk among medium-sized firms than smaller firms, but concludes that positive technology and market spillovers clearly exceed displacement effects overall. ZIM networks are treated as structurally important. Networks are distributed roughly evenly across East and West Germany, with relatively greater importance in East Germany because of structural and cultural factors: more smaller firms needing cooperation and a stronger tendency toward institutionalized cooperation. Networks function as platforms for exchange, partner search, cross-sector cooperation, project development, and future business relationships. Even when networks do not survive all funding phases as self-financed entities, they often create informal cooperation structures and new networks. The report's policy discussion places ZIM in the context of the then-planned German R&D tax incentive. It expects tax incentives to work more "in depth" among already R&D-active firms, while direct project funding could be steered more strongly toward firms less well served by tax incentives: firms in less research-intensive or knowledge-intensive sectors, firms with low R&D intensity, and firms wanting to begin R&D. The report suggests that direct project funding can provide additional value for these groups by improving innovation management, providing higher subsidy rates and liquidity, enabling projects, and increasing cooperation-related spillovers. For the Bundesinnovationshaushalt project, the report is important because it documents a policy instrument that is effective but not a complete solution to the innovation paradox. It supports the view that public R&D funding can create additional R&D and cooperation, but it also shows that program design can remain biased toward firms already capable of R&D project application. It therefore sits between [[30_concepts/rd-subsidy-additionality.md]], [[30_concepts/sme-innovation-participation.md]], and [[30_concepts/innovation-paradox.md]].

## Research question

The report asks whether ZIM under the 2015 guideline is appropriately designed, efficiently implemented, and effective in reaching its goals: increasing innovation capacity and competitiveness among SMEs, supporting application-oriented R&D, strengthening cooperation between firms and research institutions, improving innovation and cooperation management, and generating direct and indirect effects. It also asks how ZIM is positioned in the German funding portfolio, how it interacts with the emerging R&D tax incentive, and whether it reaches new firms and less R&D-experienced SMEs.

## Core argument or contribution

The core contribution is an evaluation of ZIM as a central German SME-oriented R&D funding instrument. The report finds that ZIM is well positioned and administratively efficient, with positive effects on R&D input intensity, cooperation, knowledge transfer, spillovers, and network formation. Its main limitation is breadth: it works mainly by deepening R&D among already R&D-active or R&D-near firms, rather than broadly bringing non-R&D firms into R&D. It also reaches fewer young firms over time.

## Methodology

The report uses a broad evaluation design:

- theory-based intervention and impact logic for the program and its pillars;
- administrative ZIM project data from 2008 to 2018;
- survey data from companies and research institutions;
- interviews, focus groups, and 25 case studies;
- control-group comparisons and conditional difference-in-differences analyses where data allow;
- analyses of direct effects, indirect effects, spillovers, program implementation, and positioning in the funding portfolio.

The report explicitly warns that some econometric results should be interpreted cautiously because sample sizes are limited. It triangulates quantitative estimates with surveys, interviews, case studies, and program-data analysis.

## Key findings

- ZIM approved around 40,500 R&D projects between July 2008 and June 2018, with EUR 5.5 billion in grants and at least EUR 10.3 billion in project volume.
- Firms received EUR 3.4 billion in grants and invested at least EUR 8.3 billion.
- ZIM is one of the major German application-oriented SME R&D instruments and is relatively well differentiated from EU and state-level programs.
- Control-group estimates suggest a positive effect on R&D-to-sales intensity of roughly 4 to 6 percentage points during the project period.
- ZIM increases R&D spending, R&D employment, and R&D employment intensity, though econometric robustness is limited by sample size.
- The program works more in depth than breadth: it mainly increases R&D among already R&D-active firms and induces entry into R&D for only about 3 percent of firms under a narrow definition.
- First-time applicant and first-time recipient rates remain meaningful, but the program reaches fewer young firms over time.
- Cooperation projects and networks generate knowledge transfer, follow-on cooperation, and technology spillovers.
- ZIM networks can create structural cooperation effects even when they do not continue as fully self-financed networks after funding.
- In a system with R&D tax incentives, ZIM could be steered more toward firms that tax incentives are less likely to reach.

## Limitations

- Some causal estimates are statistically fragile because of small sample sizes.
- The evaluation focuses on the 2015 guideline and early implementation years, so later program reforms and post-2020 conditions are not covered.
- TRL distribution is not directly measured; the report describes project phases and application-oriented R&D but does not classify projects into TRL stages.
- Many outcomes are based partly on survey evidence, which can be affected by self-reporting and selection.
- The report evaluates ZIM, not the full Bundesinnovationshaushalt or the Foerderkatalog population.

## Implications for LLM research or the project domain

For [[70_projects/bundesinnovationshaushalt-trl.md]], this source is a key bridge between program evaluation and portfolio classification. It suggests that German SME R&D funding can be additional and cooperative while still failing to solve the broad innovation-base problem. The project should therefore not treat "ZIM has positive effects" as sufficient evidence that the German innovation portfolio is well targeted. The more precise question is whether funding reaches young firms, first-time R&D performers, early-stage radical projects, and commercialization bottlenecks, or whether it mainly deepens activity among already R&D-capable firms. The report also supports adding recipient history, age, firm size, and R&D-experience variables to any Foerderkatalog or ZIM-linked analysis. TRL classification should be interpreted alongside recipient structure and additionality evidence.
