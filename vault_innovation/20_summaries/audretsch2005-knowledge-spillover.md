## Detailed summary

Audretsch and Lehmann ask whether the Knowledge Spillover Theory of Entrepreneurship (KSTE) applies not only across industries but also across regions. KSTE argues that entrepreneurship is a mechanism through which knowledge created in one organization, such as a university or incumbent firm, is commercialized by another organization, often a new firm. The spatial version of the theory predicts that knowledge-based startups should locate near knowledge sources because proximity lowers the cost of accessing, absorbing, and commercializing spillovers.

The paper links two literatures. One literature shows that small and new firms often produce more innovation than their formal R&D inputs would predict, suggesting that they draw on external knowledge. Another literature shows that knowledge spillovers are geographically bounded. Audretsch and Lehmann combine these arguments by testing whether young high-technology firms cluster around universities whose research output and human-capital production provide local spillover opportunities.

The empirical setting is Germany. The authors build a dataset of 281 firms that made an initial public offering in Germany between March 1997 and March 2002, using IPO prospectuses and public online sources including Deutsche Boerse information. They assign each firm to the closest university and combine the firm-location data with university-level measures collected for German public universities. University output is measured through students in natural and social sciences and publications in natural and social sciences. Regional knowledge capacity is measured with an index based on regional R&D and technological innovation activity.

The authors estimate negative binomial models in which the dependent variable is the number of technology startups located closest to a university. The source reports that the estimation dataset includes 281 firms and 54 universities. Controls include whether the university is a technical university, university age, nearby universities of applied sciences, West/East location, and other university/regional factors.

The core finding is that the number of knowledge-based startups near a university is positively influenced by both regional knowledge capacity and university knowledge output. Universities with more knowledge output and regions with stronger knowledge capacity tend to have more nearby high-technology startups. The evidence supports the spatial version of KSTE: entrepreneurial activity is partly an endogenous response to localized knowledge opportunities.

The paper also suggests that spillover mechanisms differ by type of knowledge and industry. Human capital embodied in students and research output in natural sciences are especially relevant for technology startups in some sectors. The policy implication is that universities can matter as local spillover sources through research, human capital, and collaboration, but the paper does not evaluate a funding program or estimate causal effects of a specific policy intervention.

## Research question

Does the Knowledge Spillover Theory of Entrepreneurship hold for regions, such that high-technology startups locate near universities and regions with stronger knowledge output and knowledge capacity?

## Core argument or contribution

The paper extends KSTE into a spatial/regional framework. It argues that universities and regional knowledge capacity generate localized entrepreneurial opportunities, and it provides German evidence that technology startups cluster near stronger university and regional knowledge sources.

## Methodology

- Spatial test of Knowledge Spillover Theory of Entrepreneurship.
- Firm-location dataset based on 281 German IPO firms from March 1997 to March 2002.
- University-level measures of students and publications in natural and social sciences.
- Regional knowledge-capacity index based on R&D and technological innovation.
- Assignment of firms to the closest university by geographic distance.
- Negative binomial regression of startup counts near universities.
- Controls for technical-university status, university age, universities of applied sciences, and regional/location factors.

## Datasets/materials used

- IPO prospectuses and public online sources for German firms.
- Deutsche Boerse information.
- University data for German public universities.
- Federal Statistical Office student counts, using 1997 as base year where needed.
- ISI publication data for university articles from 1993 to 2000.
- Regional knowledge-capacity index from Sternberg and Litzenberger.

## Key findings

- Knowledge-based startups are more numerous near universities with stronger knowledge output.
- Regional knowledge capacity has a positive association with nearby technology startup counts.
- The findings support the claim that KSTE holds for regions as well as industries.
- University spillovers operate through at least research output and human capital, though their importance can vary by field and sector.
- The paper supports a regional innovation-system view in which universities are local sources of entrepreneurial opportunities.

## Limitations

- The study analyzes startup location and regional spillovers, not public R&D subsidy allocation or treatment effects.
- The firm sample consists of IPO firms, which are not representative of all startups.
- The analysis is observational and spatial; it does not prove that universities causally created each nearby firm.
- The converted source has noisy OCR/table extraction, so exact coefficients and table values should be checked against the original PDF before citation.
- The paper does not analyze TRL levels, Foerderkatalog projects, ZIM, CORDIS, or German federal funding recipients.
