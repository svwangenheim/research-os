# The Decline of Science in Corporate R&D

- **Authors:** Ashish Arora (Duke), Sharon Belenzon (Duke), Andrea Patacconi (University of East Anglia)
- **Year:** 2018
- **Venue:** Strategic Management Journal
- **Keywords:** innovation, scientific research, development, patents, use of science in inventions

## Detailed summary

This paper documents a significant shift away from scientific research by large American corporations between 1980 and 2006. Using firm-level financial data combined with publications from the Web of Science and patent data, the authors analyze 4,068 publicly-traded, R&D-performing companies accounting for 452,297 firm publications.

**Key empirical findings:**
- Corporate publications by company scientists declined significantly over the 26-year period
- The decline is most evident in high-quality publications
- The value attributable to scientific research capabilities (measured by stock market valuations and M&A acquisition prices) declined
- By contrast, patenting by large American firms increased, and patent values remained stable
- There is no evidence that invention became less science-intensive, nor that the science used in inventions became older
- These patterns occur across most industries except biotechnology

## Research question

How has the nature and extent of scientific research in large American corporations changed since 1980? Has the economic value of research, and if so, is it private or social value that declined?

## Core argument or contribution

The authors establish a critical fact: large corporations are withdrawing from internal scientific research but not because science has become less valuable for innovation. Rather, they are "killing the golden goose" by reducing investments in internal scientific capabilities while still capturing "golden eggs" (patents and innovations) through external acquisition and collaboration. This reflects a shift away from the private benefits of internal research rather than a decline in the utility of science itself.

## Methodology

- **Sample:** 4,068 publicly-traded US companies with at least one patent (1980-2006)
- **Data sources:**
  - Web of Science scientific publications linked to firm affiliations
  - USPTO patent data
  - Stock market valuations (Compustat)
  - M&A deal data
- **Key indicators:**
  - Publication counts and quality (journal tier analysis)
  - Patent counts and values
  - Implied value of scientific capability via stock market premiums
  - Acquisition premiums for patents vs. scientific capabilities
- **Analysis:** Decomposition of publication decline into:
  - Entry effects (new firms without research)
  - Behavioral changes (existing firms reducing research)
  - Quality changes (shift toward lower-quality publications)

## Datasets/materials used

- **Web of Science** — comprehensive database of scientific publications with author affiliations
- **USPTO Patents** — complete patent records with citations and applicants
- **Compustat Financial Data** — firm-level financial and market valuations
- **M&A data** — acquisition prices and deal characteristics
- **Census data** — industrial classification and firm characteristics

## Key findings

1. **Publication decline is real, not artifactual:**
   - Significant fraction attributable to entry of non-publishing firms
   - But firms with established research programs also markedly decreased publications
   - Decline most evident in high-quality journals

2. **Declining value of scientific research:**
   - Stock market assigns lower premiums to scientific capability
   - M&A acquirers pay less for scientific publications
   - But patents continue to command premiums

3. **Science remains integral to innovation:**
   - Inventions did NOT become less science-intensive
   - Patents still cite recent scientific publications
   - Suggests external science (university, start-ups) substitutes for internal research

4. **Broad pattern across industries:**
   - Present in chemicals, electronics, computing, pharmaceuticals
   - Notably absent in biotechnology (which increased research)
   - Suggests industry-specific drivers

## Limitations

- **Geographic scope:** United States only (though accounts for ~50% of global R&D)
- **Time period:** Data only through 2006; may not reflect post-2008 financial crisis behavior
- **Publication sample:** Limited to "hard science" journals (engineering and natural sciences); excludes social sciences and applied journals
- **Affiliation matching:** Potential misclassification of authors, though likely biases toward underestimating publications
- **Valuation measures:** Stock market and M&A valuations are imperfect proxies for true value

## Implications for innovation policy and research

**For policy:**
- Explains the shift in innovation models toward ecosystems (universities, start-ups, collaborative networks)
- Suggests tax incentives alone may not restore internal research if private returns are low
- Highlights potential social welfare concerns if external science is not sufficient to sustain innovation

**For empirical research:**
- Publication-based indicators remain valid measures of firm science involvement, though declining absolute levels
- Patent analysis alone would miss the shift away from basic research
- Requires linking innovation metrics (patents) to underlying science (publications)

**For ongoing TRL work:**
- Firms may under-invest in early-stage (high-TRL) research if private returns are limited
- Federal support for TRL 1-3 research may become more important if private sector withdraws
- Technology maturation programs should account for heterogeneous private incentives across sectors
