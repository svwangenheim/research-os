## Detailed summary


Akcigit's Finance & Development article explains an apparent innovation paradox in the United States: R&D spending rose substantially as a share of GDP, but productivity growth and economic dynamism slowed. The article argues that the problem is not simply too little R&D, but the allocation of innovative resources. Large incumbent firms have absorbed a growing share of inventors and R&D resources, yet their incentives often favor defending market position over disruptive innovation.

The article links several empirical findings from Akcigit's research program. First, small businesses are more innovative relative to size, while large firms often shift from innovation toward market protection as they become dominant. Second, work with Nathan Goldschlag documents a reallocation of inventors toward older, large incumbent firms: roughly 48 percent of U.S. inventors worked for large incumbents at the start of the century, rising to 58 percent by 2015. Third, inventors who move to large incumbent firms become less innovative relative to inventors moving to young firms, consistent with "innovation-stifling hiring." Fourth, work with Salome Baslandze and Francesca Lotti shows that leading firms in Italian industries hire more politicians while patent production declines, a pattern Akcigit calls a leadership paradox.

The article's policy conclusion is that aggregate R&D spending is an incomplete target. Innovation policy should improve allocation: support smaller and younger firms, reduce entry barriers, preserve competition, and avoid subsidy designs such as broad R&D tax credits that are disproportionately captured by large incumbents.

## Research question


Why has rising U.S. R&D spending not translated into faster productivity growth, and how does the allocation of inventors, subsidies, and market power across firms help explain that paradox?

## Core argument or contribution


The contribution is a policy-facing synthesis of Akcigit's research on firm size, inventors, political connections, market concentration, and industrial policy. The article reframes the productivity puzzle as an allocation problem: R&D resources increasingly flow to incumbent firms that can use innovation inputs defensively, while younger and smaller firms are more likely to generate disruptive growth. This makes the structure of R&D support at least as important as the total amount of R&D spending.

## Methodology


This is a synthesis article rather than a standalone causal paper. It draws on several empirical studies: U.S. microdata on businesses, inventors, patenting, and R&D; work with Nathan Goldschlag on inventor allocation and innovation-stifling hiring; work with Sina Ates on declining business dynamism and market concentration; work with Baslandze and Lotti using Italian data on political connections, firm leadership, and patenting; and earlier Akcigit/Kerr work on heterogeneous innovation by firm size. The article itself summarizes those findings for policy readers and does not introduce a new estimation design.

## Datasets/materials used


The article discusses evidence from U.S. Census-linked business and inventor microdata, patent production, R&D spending measures, National Science Foundation R&D statistics, work on U.S. R&D tax credits, and Italian firm-level evidence on political hiring and patenting. It does not use the European Innovation Scoreboard and should not be linked to that dataset as a source-specific material.

## Key findings


- U.S. R&D spending increased from about 2.2 percent of GDP in the 1980s to about 3.4 percent, while productivity growth slowed rather than accelerated.
- Private business R&D rose from about 1.1 percent of GDP to about 2.5 percent, but aggregate R&D growth did not produce the expected productivity acceleration.
- The share of the population involved in patent production nearly doubled over two decades while productivity growth fell by half.
- Innovative resources shifted toward large incumbents: the share of inventors employed by old, large firms rose from roughly 48 percent at the beginning of the century to 58 percent by 2015.
- Inventors moving to established firms received a widening wage premium but became less innovative relative to peers who moved to young firms; the article reports a 20 percent widening of the wage premium and a 6 percent drop in innovativeness.
- Leading firms can engage in defensive strategies: Akcigit, Baslandze, and Lotti find that as firms enter the top 20 in their industry, they hire more politicians while patent production declines.
- Broad R&D tax credits and industrial-policy tools can unintentionally favor large incumbents, encouraging defensive R&D and innovation-stifling hiring rather than productivity-enhancing innovation.
- Policy should focus on the allocation of R&D support, competition, entry, and targeted support for young and small firms rather than only increasing aggregate R&D volume.

## Limitations


The article is a policy synthesis, not a new standalone empirical paper. It combines findings from several studies and datasets, so exact causal claims should be traced to the cited underlying papers. The evidence is mainly about the United States, with one Italian firm-politician study used as supporting evidence; applying the mechanism to Germany requires a separate empirical bridge. The article does not analyze the German Foerderkatalog, TRL distributions, European Innovation Scoreboard data, or German public R&D programs directly.

## Implications for LLM research or the project domain


For [[70_projects/bundesinnovationshaushalt-trl.md]], the article is useful as a mechanism hypothesis, not as direct German evidence. It suggests that a funding system can look innovation-friendly in aggregate while still underperforming if resources are captured by older, larger incumbents or used defensively. The relevant project implication is therefore testable: check whether German federal R&D support is concentrated among incumbents, high-TRL/incremental projects, or sectors with defensive transformation needs.
