## Detailed summary

Horbach and Rammer study firm-level reactions to the 2022 German energy-price shock. The source frames the shock as the sharp increase in energy prices following Russia's war against Ukraine and the disruption of Russian natural gas and oil supply to Germany. It notes that natural gas prices rose by 89 percent from 2021 to 2022, while fuel oil, heat, and electricity also increased strongly. Because natural gas was the largest industrial energy source in German manufacturing before the shock, the event created strong pressure for firms to reduce energy consumption, increase energy efficiency, change energy sources, or reduce energy-intensive activities.

The paper focuses on short-term responses rather than long-run transformation. It studies two kinds of firm reaction. The first is adoption of energy-related measures: substituting machinery, vehicles, or equipment with less energy-consuming alternatives; using digital applications such as smart metering to optimize energy use; introducing energy management systems; changing energy suppliers; implementing smaller energy-saving measures such as lower room temperature or reduced travel; and relocating or stopping energy-intensive activities. The second is change in the composition of energy use, especially whether firms reduce fossil-energy consumption or substitute fossil with renewable energy sources.

The empirical analysis uses the German Community Innovation Survey/Mannheim Innovation Panel. The source states that the MIP is a stratified random-sample panel survey and that the reporting-year 2022 sample contained 37,980 firms, of which 8,034 provided valid responses. It then links these 2022 data to the following survey year: 4,443 of those firms provided valid responses for 2023. Energy suppliers are excluded because their motives and energy-source choices differ from the rest of the sample.

The key explanatory variable is the energy-cost share in total costs in 2021. The authors use this pre-crisis measure because changes in energy costs from 2021 to 2022 could themselves be affected by firms' response choices. Higher 2021 energy-cost shares indicate stronger exposure to the 2022 price shock. The models also include climate-change affectedness, cooperation, subsidies, R&D, Covid-19 effects, firm size, sector dummies, and other controls.

For the determinants of binary energy-related measures and energy-source changes, the paper estimates probit models and reports average marginal effects. For short-term performance outcomes from 2022 to 2023, it estimates quantile regressions for sales growth, employment growth, and export-share changes.

The main result is that firms with higher pre-crisis energy-cost shares were more likely to replace equipment with more energy-efficient alternatives, introduce energy management systems, and relocate or stop energy-intensive activities. The magnitude is modest: a one-percentage-point increase in the 2021 energy-cost share is associated with only a small increase in the probability of replacing equipment, while 42.5 percent of the sample implemented this equipment-replacement measure. This means the energy shock triggered adjustment not only among highly exposed firms but across a wider set of firms.

The source also finds that energy-related measures are linked to changes in energy use. Replacing equipment with less energy-consuming alternatives is associated with decreased use of electricity, gas, and oil, and with substitution of fossil energy by renewable energy. Digital applications for energy efficiency are also positively related to renewable substitution and reduced natural-gas use. Smaller energy-saving measures are frequently used and are strongly linked to reduced electricity and gas use.

The performance results show a tradeoff. Firms with higher energy-cost shares, especially above a 4 percent threshold, experienced weaker sales growth from 2022 to 2023. The paper does not find significant negative effects on employment growth, which the authors interpret as evidence that the shock hurt short-term competitiveness of exposed firms but did not produce a similarly strong reduction in medium-term production capacity. They connect this to skilled-labour scarcity, which may have encouraged firms to retain staff.

## Research question

The paper asks how German firms reacted in the short run to the 2022 energy-price shock, which energy-related measures and energy-source substitutions they adopted, what drove those reactions, and how these reactions were associated with sales, employment, and export performance from 2022 to 2023.

## Core argument or contribution

The contribution is firm-level evidence on the immediate behavioral response to a large energy-price shock. The paper argues that energy shocks can accelerate energy-efficiency measures and fossil-to-renewable substitution, but that these environmental and efficiency responses come with negative short-term sales consequences for highly exposed firms.

## Methodology

- Descriptive analysis of German CIS/MIP evidence on energy-source changes and energy-related measures.
- Probit models for binary adoption of energy-related measures and energy-source changes.
- Quantile regressions for performance outcomes, allowing different effects for low-, median-, and high-growth firms.
- The main shock-exposure variable is the 2021 energy-cost share in total costs.

## Datasets/materials used

- German Community Innovation Survey / Mannheim Innovation Panel survey data for reference years 2022 and 2023, collected in 2023 and 2024.
- The reporting-year 2022 MIP sample had 37,980 firms and 8,034 valid responses.
- For the linked performance analysis, 4,443 firms from the 2022 data provided valid 2023 responses.
- Energy suppliers are excluded from the analysis.

## Key findings

- The 2022 shock was large: natural gas prices increased by 89 percent from 2021 to 2022, and other industrial energy prices also rose sharply.
- German firms used multiple short-term energy responses, including smaller savings, equipment replacement, digital applications, supplier changes, energy management systems, and relocation or closure of energy-intensive activities.
- Higher 2021 energy-cost share is associated with equipment substitution, energy management systems, and relocation or stopping of energy-intensive activities.
- Equipment substitution is associated with lower electricity, gas, and oil use and with replacing fossil energy by renewables.
- Digital energy-efficiency applications are associated with renewable substitution and lower natural-gas use.
- Firms with stronger energy-price exposure show weaker sales growth after the crisis, especially among energy-intensive firms.
- The source does not find comparable negative effects on employment growth.
- The authors argue that targeted support would ideally link energy-cost relief to energy-saving measures and replacement of fossil with renewable energy, noting that Germany's 2022 energy-cost subsidies for energy-intensive firms did not establish such a link.

## Limitations

- The analysis is explicitly short-term and does not show whether measures adopted during the crisis create durable transformation.
- The authors state that the cross-sectional character of the data limits causal analysis.
- The study relies on survey-reported firm behavior and observed associations, not experimental variation.
- The focus is Germany's 2022 energy-price crisis, so results may not generalize to slower or smaller price changes.
