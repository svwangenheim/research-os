## Detailed summary

Heder studies how Technology Readiness Levels (TRLs) became an EU innovation-policy tool after originating in NASA space-technology management. The paper is a document-centric historical and policy analysis based on position papers, white papers, government documents, program descriptions, policy documents, and public research-program materials. Its core concern is that TRL was created for specific organizational and technological settings, then abstracted into a broad policy tool without sufficient discipline-specific tailoring.

The paper begins with NASA. TRL emerged from space-program practices around flight readiness and technology readiness reviews. In the late Apollo and post-Apollo period, NASA needed a way to evaluate technologies that were being developed ahead of a specific mission need. The 1989 NASA readiness-level scheme had seven levels, from observed basic principles to system adequacy validated in space. This early scale was not merely a generic maturity ladder; it linked technology readiness to mission risk, technology handoffs, organizational responsibilities, and different readiness requirements for different types of technologies. Heder emphasizes that this sophistication is often lost in later non-space uses.

The nine-level NASA scale followed soon afterward, especially through John Mankins's work and NASA's 1991 Integrated Technology Plan. The later scale added levels for completed and flight-qualified systems and for flight-proven systems in successful mission operations. NASA's approach also treated cost as increasing sharply toward higher readiness levels and used TRLs as part of a broader technology-maturation strategy, not as a simple stand-alone label.

The paper then examines defense acquisition. In the late 1990s, the US Government Accountability Office used TRLs to diagnose Department of Defense acquisition problems, arguing that programs had incorporated immature technologies and then suffered delays and cost overruns while trying to close the readiness gap. The GAO recommended that DoD avoid acquiring technologies below TRL 7 and prefer TRL 8 or 9. In this context, TRL functioned as a risk-control and procurement-governance tool, supported by testing, documentation, and supplier-side maturation.

The scale entered Europe partly through the European Space Agency and the ISO 16290:2013 space-systems standard. Heder stresses that these space-sector versions retained detailed assessment criteria, examples, and documented achievements. The problem arises when TRL is generalized beyond such contexts.

The EU adoption came through the Key Enabling Technologies policy agenda and the Horizon 2020 framework. The High-Level Expert Group on Key Enabling Technologies used TRLs to describe Europe's "Valley of Death" problem: strong low-TRL research outputs that do not become market-ready applications often enough. Heder argues that EU policy adopted TRL as part of a broader industry-oriented innovation strategy, but did so across very different domains such as nanotechnology, advanced materials, biotechnology, micro- and nano-electronics, photonics, advanced manufacturing, informatics, and communication technology. In Horizon 2020, TRL thresholds appeared in work programs as funding boundaries.

The paper's warning is that generalized TRL use can create confusion and abuse. A discipline-specific TRL guide is necessary before applying the scale, because readiness means different things for space hardware, software, social innovation, biotechnology, manufacturing processes, and other research domains. Without specific guides and assessment procedures, applicants may strategically self-report TRLs to fit funding calls, evaluators may apply inconsistent standards, and policy may impose arbitrary transitions between funding modes. Heder therefore treats EU-wide uncritical TRL adoption as an innovation in innovation policy whose risks may exceed its benefits if not accompanied by domain-specific operationalization.

## Research question

How did the TRL scale evolve from NASA space-program technology management into EU public-sector innovation policy, and what risks arise when a domain-specific readiness scale is applied broadly without discipline-specific tailoring?

## Core argument or contribution

The core contribution is a historical and policy critique of TRL migration. Heder argues that the TRL scale's concreteness and sophistication declined as it spread from NASA and space/defense acquisition into EU-wide innovation policy. TRLs can be useful for communicating maturity and managing handoffs, but only when tailored to the relevant discipline and embedded in clear assessment procedures. Uncritical broad adoption creates confusion, arbitrary incentives, and gaming risks.

## Methodology

The paper uses comparative, document-centered historical policy analysis. It reconstructs the evolution of TRL through NASA documents, GAO/DoD materials, ESA and ISO documents, European Commission communications, High-Level Expert Group reports, Horizon 2020 program descriptions, and related policy materials. It does not estimate causal effects or use a quantitative dataset.

## Datasets/materials used

The materials are public policy and program documents: NASA readiness-level and technology-planning documents, GAO/DoD acquisition reports, ESA handbook material, ISO 16290:2013, European Commission communications on Key Enabling Technologies, HLG-KET reports, Horizon 2020 work-program documents, and related public sources. Horizon 2020 is relevant to [[50_datasets/cordis-horizon-europe-h2020.md]] as an EU funding context, but the paper itself is not a CORDIS data analysis. It does not use Foerderkatalog data or LLM annotation datasets.

## Key findings

- TRL originated in NASA space-technology management as an internal readiness and risk-management tool.
- The early NASA version had seven levels; later NASA/Mankins usage developed the familiar nine-level scale.
- NASA and space-sector use linked TRL to mission risk, technology handoffs, testing environments, documented evidence, and organizational responsibilities.
- DoD adoption used TRLs as an acquisition-risk tool after cost overruns and delays in weapons programs.
- ESA and ISO space-sector versions retained more detailed assessment criteria than later generic innovation-policy uses.
- EU policy adopted TRLs through the Key Enabling Technologies and Horizon 2020 agenda to describe and manage the transition from research to market application.
- Heder argues that EU-wide adoption abstracted away important domain-specific detail.
- Without discipline-specific TRL guides, TRLs can produce confusion, inconsistent assessment, arbitrary funding thresholds, and strategic self-reporting by applicants.

## Limitations

The article uses publicly available documents and cannot reconstruct all internal political or administrative reasons behind EU TRL adoption. It also could not assess quantitative impacts because, at the time of writing, Horizon 2020 TRL-mandated projects had only recently begun and few had ended. The paper is therefore a historical and conceptual policy critique, not an outcome evaluation of TRL use.

## Implications for LLM research or the project domain

For the Bundesinnovationshaushalt/TRL project, Heder is essential for scope discipline. The paper supports using TRL cautiously as an analytic classification, but it warns against treating generic TRL labels as self-explanatory across domains. Automated TRL classification from Foerderkatalog project descriptions should therefore include domain-specific coding rules, clear boundary examples, uncertainty categories, and validation against human judgment. The paper does not prove that German Foerderkatalog lacks native TRL reporting; that claim must be established from Foerderkatalog documentation. Its direct contribution is the warning that imported TRL schemes can become vague and gameable without tailored assessment procedures.
