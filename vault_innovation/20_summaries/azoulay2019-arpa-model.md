## Detailed summary

This paper codifies the "ARPA model" of research funding and identifies the conditions under which it is appropriate. The authors — all involved in the National Academies review of ARPA-E (NRC 2017) — define four elements of the ARPA model: (1) organizational flexibility at the administrative level; (2) identification of "technological white space" and program design to fill that void; (3) significant discretion given to program managers to select projects; (4) active project management via specific technical milestones and time commitments.

**The ARPA domain — nascent S-curves:** The paper's central theoretical contribution is identifying the ARPA model's domain as "mission-oriented research on nascent S-curves within an inefficient innovation system" (abstract). Drawing on Foster (1986), the paper describes technology S-curves as sigmoidal functions of research effort: the beginning of a curve represents a novel concept with low returns to effort; the middle (inflection point) yields the highest returns; the final segment entails diminishing returns to effort and slow performance improvements. The ARPA model is optimized for the **beginning of nascent S-curves** — where the technology exists and is relatively unexplored, with high potential for improvement. This maps onto low-to-mid TRL (roughly TRL 1–4): the ARPA model targets not-yet-mature technologies where private markets under-invest most severely.

**Stokes taxonomy:** ARPA sits in Edison's quadrant (applied focus, "use" is central) but within the novel and immature part — it can venture into Pasteur's quadrant (use-inspired basic research) when fundamental scientific challenges must be resolved to advance the technology.

**Program manager model:** DARPA has ~100 program managers with $3bn annual budget. Program managers are expert scientists/engineers with significant autonomy to design programs, select projects, and actively manage them with time-limited appointments (typically 3–5 years). This differs sharply from standard grant agencies where bureaucrats manage large portfolios with little scientific authority.

**Market failure extension:** The standard public R&D market failure argument applies to early-stage upstream research. The paper extends this: it can also apply to later-stage R&D if private markets cannot successfully commercialize the technology — the "inefficient innovation system" condition in their domain definition.

**Challenges:** ARPA-like agencies are hard to evaluate because transformational outcomes are rare by design. Standard metrics (publications, patents, short-run commercialization) are inappropriate for programs making long-term, high-risk investments. A single success (internet, GPS) can justify many failures.

## Research question

What are the defining elements of the ARPA model, and under what conditions (technology type, market context) is it the appropriate innovation policy instrument?

## Core argument or contribution

1. **Four-element codification** of the ARPA model: flexibility, white-space identification, PM discretion, active management with milestones.
2. **Domain specification:** ARPA is suited to mission-oriented research on nascent S-curves (beginning of technological development) in markets with innovation system failures — i.e., low TRL, high uncertainty, high potential, private under-investment.
3. **S-curve as a classification device:** Foster (1986) S-curve provides a principled way to decide whether a research area is ARPA-appropriate (nascent) vs. better suited to other instruments (mature/incremental).

## Key findings

1. **Four elements of the ARPA model** — operational checklist for evaluating any ARPA-like institution.
2. **S-curve domain condition:** ARPA works best at the beginning (nascent phase) of technology S-curves. Not suited to mature, high-TRL technologies where private markets function.
3. **Program manager scale gap:** DARPA has ~100 PMs for $3bn; ARPA-E has far fewer, creating bandwidth constraints. This aligns with Fuest et al. (2024): EIC has ~11 PMs vs. DARPA's ~100.
4. **Private sector under-investment extends beyond basic research:** The market failure justification also applies to nascent-technology commercialization if innovation systems are structurally inefficient.
5. **Evaluation challenge:** Success at ARPA is rare but highly skewed (GPS, internet). Standard program evaluation metrics are inappropriate.

## Limitations

- Primarily descriptive/institutional; no causal evaluation of ARPA-E effectiveness
- S-curve classification is intuitive but not operationalized with TRL numbers or precise quantitative criteria
- Context is US federal government; applicability to EU/EIC governance requires institutional adaptation
- ARPA-E was only ~9 years old at time of writing; long-run evidence limited

## Implications for the Bundesinnovationshaushalt project

1. **Primary S-curve source:** This paper is the primary citation for the S-curve / TRL mapping in `explorations/trl_research_briefing.md`. The specific claim — ARPA operates on nascent S-curves (beginning = low TRL, breakthrough potential high; end = diminishing returns, incremental) — comes from pp. 20–21 of this paper, citing Foster (1986).
2. **PM model benchmark:** Azoulay et al.'s DARPA ~100 PMs figure is the comparator used by Fuest et al. for the EIC's ~11 PMs critique. Useful for German policy context (SPRIND has a similar small-PM structure).
3. **Domain condition test:** Azoulay et al. imply a diagnostic: does a programme target nascent S-curves (= ARPA-appropriate, low TRL) or mature technologies (= market/VC-appropriate, high TRL)? This is exactly what the Förderkatalog TRL classification tests.
