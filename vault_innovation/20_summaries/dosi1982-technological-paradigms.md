## Detailed summary

Dosi's paper develops a conceptual framework for understanding why technical change follows some directions rather than others. It criticizes one-directional explanations of innovation, especially strong demand-pull accounts that treat technology as a flexible response to market signals and strong technology-push accounts that treat science and technology as autonomous forces moving into the economy. Dosi argues that neither perspective adequately explains the emergence of new technological directions, the persistence of established paths, or the interaction of scientific, economic, institutional, and industrial factors.

The paper's central concepts are technological paradigms and technological trajectories. A technological paradigm is an outlook or pattern for solving technological problems. It defines relevant problems, accepted procedures, specific knowledge bases, and criteria of progress. A technological trajectory is the direction of advance within a paradigm. Once a paradigm is established, innovation often proceeds through cumulative search along a trajectory: engineers and firms improve performance according to the paradigm's own technical and economic trade-offs.

This framework explains both continuity and discontinuity. Continuous technical change is associated with movement along established trajectories. Discontinuous change is associated with the emergence of new paradigms. Dosi emphasizes that new paradigms do not arise simply because a market need appears. Their emergence depends on the interplay of scientific advances, economic factors, institutions, and unresolved difficulties on existing technological paths. The paper also connects paradigms to industrial structure: the emergence of a new paradigm is often associated with Schumpeterian entrants or new firms, while established paradigms can become linked to oligopolistic stabilization.

The source is explicitly tentative and interpretive. Dosi states that the paper does not provide a general theory of technical change and offers limited empirical backing in this article. Its value is conceptual: it gives the wiki a vocabulary for distinguishing incremental progress within a technological path from deeper shifts in the problem-solving model itself.

For the Bundesinnovationshaushalt/TRL project, Dosi is relevant when interpreting whether public funding supports path-following improvement or paradigm-shifting exploration. However, Dosi does not provide TRL categories, project-level classification rules, or a dataset. Any mapping from paradigms/trajectories to TRL stages is a project interpretation and must be validated separately.

## Research question

How can economic theory explain the determinants and directions of technical change, including both cumulative improvement along established technological paths and discontinuous emergence of new technological paradigms?

## Core argument or contribution

The paper contributes a paradigm/trajectory framework for technical change. It argues that innovation is structured by problem-solving heuristics, knowledge bases, institutions, industrial structures, and economic selection mechanisms, rather than being simply pulled by market demand or pushed mechanically by science.

## Methodology

- Conceptual and theoretical argument.
- Critical review of demand-pull and technology-push theories.
- Analogy between scientific paradigms/research programmes and technological paradigms.
- Interpretive discussion of industrial structure, Schumpeterian entry, maturity, and oligopolistic stabilization.
- Limited empirical backing in the paper itself; the author points to other work for applications.

## Datasets/materials used

This is a theoretical article and does not use a formal empirical dataset. It refers to existing theory, innovation studies, and examples from technological and industrial history.

## Key findings

- Strong demand-pull theories are inadequate because they treat innovation as too reactive to market signals and struggle to explain major technological breakthroughs.
- Strong technology-push theories are also inadequate because they understate how economic and institutional factors shape technological directions.
- A technological paradigm defines relevant problems, problem-solving procedures, knowledge bases, and criteria of progress.
- A technological trajectory is the direction of advance within a paradigm.
- Continuous innovation often means cumulative progress along an established trajectory.
- Discontinuous innovation involves the emergence of a new paradigm.
- The selection and stabilization of paradigms depend on scientific, economic, institutional, and industrial-structure factors.
- New paradigms are often associated with Schumpeterian firms or entrants, while established paradigms can become linked to mature industrial structures and oligopolistic stabilization.

## Limitations

- The article is conceptual and does not test the framework with a systematic empirical design.
- Dosi explicitly does not claim to provide a complete general theory of technical change.
- The framework is interpretive; applying it to specific sectors, firms, or funding portfolios requires additional empirical evidence.
- The paper does not define TRL levels, subsidy additionality, project-selection rules, or operational coding procedures.
