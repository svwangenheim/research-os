## Detailed summary

Moller, Pera, Dalsgaard, and Aiello study whether computational social science researchers should spend scarce resources on additional human labels or on synthetic data augmentation from large language models. The paper calls this the "parrot dilemma": LLMs can generate more training examples cheaply, but it is unclear when those examples actually improve downstream classifiers relative to human-labeled data.

The authors evaluate ten classification tasks that vary in complexity, class balance, language, sensitivity, and number of classes. The tasks include sentiment, offensive language detection in Danish, social-dimension extraction, emotion classification, empathy, politeness, hyperbole, intimacy, same-side stance, and condescension detection. The paper is explicitly concerned with low-resource and difficult computational social science classification settings rather than only simple benchmark tasks.

The experimental setup simulates a situation where only a small amount of labeled data is initially available. For each task, the authors start from a base set containing 10 percent of the available human-labeled training data. They then compare two ways of expanding the training set: adding more human-labeled examples, or adding synthetic examples generated by GPT-4 or Llama-2 70B Chat. For synthetic augmentation, the prompt includes an original example and label, and the LLM is asked to generate nine similar examples with the same label. The authors use a balanced augmentation strategy so that minority classes are oversampled before synthetic generation.

The downstream classifier is not the LLM itself. The authors train an `intfloat/e5-base` model using the Hugging Face Trainer and evaluate it on held-out test data with macro-F1 and accuracy. They also compare these trained classifiers to zero-shot classification using GPT-4 and Llama-2 70B Chat.

The results are conditional rather than a blanket endorsement of synthetic data. For binary tasks, sensitive tasks, and balanced multi-class tasks, models trained on human-annotated data generally outperform or match models trained with synthetic augmentation, even when the human training set is small. This supports the continued value of human labels.

Synthetic augmentation helps more in complex, imbalanced, multi-class tasks. The paper reports that tasks such as social dimensions and emotions benefit from LLM-generated data, plausibly because balanced augmentation greatly increases the number of minority-class examples. In the emotion task, a classifier trained with Llama-2-generated synthetic data outperforms the other methods.

The paper also finds that zero-shot LLM classification is usually not the best option. GPT-4 and Llama-2 can perform strongly, but specialized models trained on human or synthetic training data generally outperform zero-shot LLMs, especially as even moderate amounts of task-specific training data become available.

## Research question

When computational social science researchers have limited annotation budgets, should they prioritize additional human labels, LLM-generated synthetic augmentation, or zero-shot LLM classification for text classification tasks?

## Core argument or contribution

The paper's contribution is an empirical comparison of human-labeled training data, GPT-4/Llama-2 synthetic augmentation, and zero-shot LLM prediction across ten heterogeneous CSS tasks. It shows that the value of synthetic augmentation depends strongly on task complexity and class imbalance.

## Methodology

- Ten public computational social science classification tasks are selected with varying complexity.
- A small human-labeled base set is created from 10 percent of the original training data.
- Training sets are expanded either with more human-labeled data or with synthetic examples generated by GPT-4 or Llama-2 70B Chat.
- Synthetic augmentation prompts provide an original example and its label and request nine similar examples with the same label.
- Minority classes are oversampled before augmentation to create a more balanced synthetic training set.
- `intfloat/e5-base` classifiers are trained separately for each task and training-set condition.
- GPT-4 and Llama-2 are also evaluated as zero-shot classifiers.
- Main evaluation metric: macro-F1; accuracy is also tracked.

## Datasets/materials used

- Public datasets for ten CSS tasks: sentiment, Danish offensive language, social dimensions, emotions, empathy, politeness, hyperbole, intimacy, same-side stance, and condescension.
- Human-labeled original data from the task datasets.
- GPT-4 synthetic examples.
- Llama-2 70B Chat synthetic examples.
- `intfloat/e5-base` classifier.

## Key findings

- Human-labeled data generally remains superior or comparable for binary tasks, sensitive tasks, and balanced multi-class tasks.
- Synthetic augmentation typically gives little or no gain over human-labeled data in simpler or balanced settings.
- Synthetic augmentation is more useful for complex, imbalanced, multi-class tasks because it can add minority-class examples.
- In the emotion classification task, Llama-2 synthetic augmentation produces the best model among the compared methods.
- Zero-shot GPT-4 and Llama-2 are generally outperformed by specialized classifiers trained on human or synthetic task-specific data.
- The practical recommendation is conditional: use human labels where feasible, consider synthetic augmentation for rare-class expansion in complex imbalanced tasks, and do not assume zero-shot LLMs are sufficient for difficult CSS classification.

## Limitations

- The evaluation covers ten CSS tasks, not all text-classification domains.
- Synthetic augmentation quality depends on prompting, base examples, model choice, and temperature.
- The study evaluates GPT-4 and Llama-2 70B Chat; results may differ for newer LLMs.
- The downstream classifier is `intfloat/e5-base`; different classifiers may interact differently with synthetic data.
- Synthetic data can inherit LLM biases or reduce diversity, so augmentation should be validated against held-out human-labeled data.
- The source does not evaluate TRL classification, Foerderkatalog data, or R&D-funding texts directly.

## Implications for LLM research or the project domain

For the Bundesinnovationshaushalt/TRL project, the source is a caution against assuming that synthetic labels or examples are always better than human annotation. Human-labeled validation data remains important, especially if the task is sensitive, binary, or balanced. Synthetic augmentation is most promising for rare or underrepresented classes, which may matter if some TRL levels are scarce.

The paper does not show that LLM-generated TRL labels are unreliable or reliable for Foerderkatalog. It suggests a validation design: compare human-labeled examples, synthetic augmentation, and zero-shot or few-shot LLM classification on a held-out human-labeled TRL sample, with macro-F1 and rare-class performance reported separately.
