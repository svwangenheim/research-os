## Detailed summary

Naude and Nagler argue that Germany's modern innovation history has two contrasting faces. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth century, Germany rose from an industrial backwater to a leading industrial power through radical innovation, engineer-entrepreneurship, research universities, technical education, industrial laboratories, and state-supported infrastructure. In the post-World War II period, Germany achieved extraordinary economic success, including the Wirtschaftswunder and export-led growth, but the authors argue that its innovation system became comparatively less radical and less dynamic.

The paper is a long-run historical synthesis rather than a single econometric test. It describes Germany's innovation and growth across three periods: 1871-1913, the wars and interwar period, and the post-1950 period. For the first period, it emphasizes the rise of research universities, technical universities, public research laboratories, private industrial laboratories, engineer-entrepreneurs, and a state that supported education and infrastructure. The authors link this institutional configuration to major innovations associated with figures such as Werner von Siemens, Karl Benz, Rudolf Diesel, Robert Bosch, Paul Ehrlich, Heinrich Hertz, Fritz and Felix Hoffmann, and others.

To support the claim of postwar relative decline, the paper assembles several innovation indicators and comparisons. One illustrative count uses a broad list of German inventions and discoveries and finds that the number in 1946-2020 was only 27 percent of the 1870-1945 count, with exceptions in electronics, sports, toys, and computing. The authors also discuss trends in GDP per capita growth, total factor productivity, patents and patent quality, Nobel prizes, R&D researchers, and other indicators. Their overall claim is that the issue is not the absolute absence of innovation, but that innovation has become harder and less radical.

The paper identifies four broad, interrelated explanations. First, Germany's innovation system is said to be locked into incremental innovation. The authors connect postwar success to established large firms, strong manufacturing capabilities, and continuous improvement, but argue that these strengths may also reduce radical renewal. Second, technology diffusion is slowing: new technologies take longer to spread and incumbent structures can delay adoption. Third, the education system has weaknesses, including institutional rigidity, fragmented governance across Laender, and a vocational system tailored strongly to established industrial needs. Fourth, entrepreneurship is stagnating: start-up rates, venture capital, and disruptive Schumpeterian entrepreneurship are weaker than would be expected from Germany's scientific and industrial base.

The policy conclusion is that isolated R&D spending is unlikely to be enough. The authors argue for a broad "big push" of complementary reforms, especially around education and managerial capabilities, venture capital, market contestability, and a social welfare system compatible with entrepreneurial risk-taking. They frame Germany's nineteenth-century rise as an entrepreneurial-state success and suggest that contemporary Germany needs similarly complementary institutional changes to revive radical innovation.

## Research question

Why did Germany's historically strong innovation system become comparatively less radical and dynamic after World War II, and what historical mechanisms might explain the long-run slowdown in innovation and business dynamism?

## Core argument or contribution

The paper's contribution is a long-run historical diagnosis of German innovation slowdown. It argues that Germany's postwar model preserved economic strength while increasingly favoring incremental innovation, slower diffusion, weaker educational dynamism, and stagnating entrepreneurship.

## Methodology

- Historical synthesis of German innovation and growth from 1870 to 2020.
- Periodization into 1871-1913, 1914-1949, and 1950-present.
- Compilation and interpretation of multiple innovation indicators rather than one causal design.
- Discussion of inventions/discoveries, economic growth, productivity, patents, Nobel prizes, R&D researchers, education-system evidence, entrepreneurship indicators, and venture-capital context.
- Historical comparison of institutional arrangements behind Germany's rise and later slowdown.

## Datasets/materials used

- Historical lists of German inventions and discoveries.
- Long-run GDP per capita and productivity evidence from prior studies.
- Patent and patent-citation evidence discussed from the literature.
- Nobel Prize counts discussed from prior scholarship.
- R&D researcher and OECD-country comparison evidence.
- Entrepreneurship, start-up, venture-capital, and institutional indicators from secondary sources.

## Key findings

- Germany's 1871-1913 rise is attributed to a strong combination of education, science, entrepreneurship, business organization, and state support.
- The paper argues that many major radical German innovations clustered in the late nineteenth and early twentieth century.
- A broad count of inventions and discoveries gives only 27 percent as many German inventions/discoveries in 1946-2020 as in 1870-1945.
- Postwar Germany remained economically successful, but the authors argue that its innovation became more incremental.
- The authors identify four explanations: lock-in to incremental innovation, slower technology diffusion, education-system weaknesses, and entrepreneurial stagnation.
- They associate postwar corporate dominance and lower Schumpeterian entrepreneurship with fewer disruptive new firms.
- The paper argues that comprehensive institutional reforms, not only more R&D spending, are needed to revive radical innovation.

## Limitations

- The paper is a discussion paper and historical synthesis, not a causal evaluation.
- Some evidence, such as invention/discovery counts, is illustrative and depends on source construction.
- The paper's diagnosis is broad and interpretive; individual mechanisms require separate empirical testing.
- It focuses on Germany and should not be mechanically generalized to all advanced economies.
- It does not analyze the Foerderkatalog, CORDIS, TRL distributions, or specific R&D subsidy allocation rules directly.
- It does not prove that German federal R&D subsidies cause incremental lock-in.
