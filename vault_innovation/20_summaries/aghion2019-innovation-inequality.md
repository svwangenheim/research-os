## Detailed summary

This paper asks whether innovation-led growth contributes to rising top income inequality, and whether that relationship should be interpreted differently from rent extraction or broad inequality. The authors use U.S. cross-state panel data from 1975 to 2010, measuring innovation through patent-based indicators and top income inequality through the top 1% income share. The paper's core finding is that more innovative states tend to experience higher top income shares. The authors use two instrumentation strategies to argue that the relationship partly reflects a causal effect from innovativeness to top income inequality. In their preferred patent-per-capita measure, innovation accounts for a meaningful share of the rise in the top 1% income share, but the effect is concentrated at the top: innovation does not appear to raise broader inequality measures in the same way. The paper then separates this innovation mechanism from simple rent preservation. It finds that lobbying intensity dampens the positive relationship between innovation and top income shares, and also weakens the relationship between innovation and social mobility. At the commuting-zone level, innovativeness is positively associated with upward social mobility, and this relationship is driven more by entrant innovation than by incumbent innovation. For the vault, the paper is relevant because it distinguishes innovation rents produced by creative destruction from rents protected by incumbent lobbying. It supports a nuanced interpretation: innovation can create top incomes while also improving mobility, especially when entrants are able to challenge incumbents. The policy problem is not "innovation creates inequality" in general, but whether the innovation system supports entrant-driven creative destruction or incumbent rent protection.

## Research question

Does innovation causally increase top income inequality, and how does the relationship differ between entrant innovation, incumbent innovation, lobbying, and social mobility?

## Core argument or contribution

The paper contributes evidence that innovation is associated with higher top income shares, but also with upward mobility when innovation is entrant-driven. It therefore separates Schumpeterian innovation rents from protected incumbent rents.

## Methodology

- Cross-state panel regressions for U.S. states over 1975-2010.
- Patent-based measures of innovativeness, including patent counts and quality-related measures.
- Instrumental-variable strategies to address causality from innovation to top income shares.
- Commuting-zone regressions to analyze social mobility.
- Extensions distinguishing entrant and incumbent innovation and interacting innovation effects with lobbying intensity.

## Datasets/materials used

The paper uses U.S. cross-state panel data for 1975-2010, patent-based measures of innovativeness, top 1% income-share data, broader inequality measures, commuting-zone social-mobility data, measures distinguishing entrant and incumbent innovation, and state-level lobbying-intensity measures. Exact variable construction and instruments should be checked in the source before citation.

## Key findings

- Innovation is positively and significantly correlated with top income inequality across U.S. states.
- Instrumental-variable evidence suggests that part of this relationship reflects a causal effect of innovation on top 1% income shares.
- Innovation does not appear to increase broader inequality measures in the same way.
- Innovativeness is positively correlated with upward social mobility at the commuting-zone level.
- The mobility relationship is driven mainly by entrant innovation rather than incumbent innovation.
- Lobbying intensity dampens the positive effects of innovation on both top income shares and social mobility.

## Limitations

- The setting is U.S. states and commuting zones; transfer to German innovation policy requires institutional caution.
- Patent measures capture only part of innovation activity.
- The paper studies income distribution and mobility, not direct R&D subsidy effects.
- Entrant/incumbent innovation distinctions are conceptually useful for the vault, but not equivalent to German Förderkatalog recipient classes without additional mapping.
