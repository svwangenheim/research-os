## Detailed summary

Building on Cohen and Levinthal (1989, 1990), this paper argues that absorptive capacity (AC) not only enables innovation directly but also *moderates* how effectively firms translate external knowledge spillovers into innovation output. The authors carefully distinguish between the *innovation role* of a firm's knowledge base (internal capability) and its *absorption role* (leveraging external knowledge). By focusing on the moderating rather than the direct effect, they isolate the absorption channel empirically.

Using 2,265 Spanish firms from the Community Innovation Surveys (CIS 2000 and 2002), they interact absorptive capacity with involuntary knowledge spillovers (knowledge that leaks from other firms' research) to predict innovation output. The interaction is positive and significant: firms with higher AC extract more innovation from any given level of external spillover.

The moderation effect varies with the environment: it is significantly larger in sectors with high technological turbulence and strong intellectual property rights (IPR) protection. In turbulent environments, absorbing fast-arriving external knowledge quickly is more valuable. Under strong IPR regimes, formal spillovers are scarcer, so capacity to use what does spill over becomes more valuable.

The paper controls carefully for sample selection and endogeneity between AC and external knowledge flows.

## Research question

Does absorptive capacity moderate — not merely enable — the relationship between external knowledge spillovers and innovation output? And does this moderation vary with the technological and institutional environment?

## Core argument or contribution

1. Firms with higher absorptive capacity extract disproportionately more innovation from external spillovers — a multiplicative moderating effect.
2. Moderation is stronger in high-turbulence and strong-IPR sectors.
3. Empirically separates the innovation channel (own R&D → innovation) from the absorption channel (AC × spillovers → innovation).

## Methodology

- **Sample**: 2,265 Spanish firms, CIS 2000 and CIS 2002 (INE)
- **Key interaction**: Absorptive capacity proxy (internal R&D intensity) × external involuntary spillovers (sectoral measures)
- **Model**: Negative binomial regression with sample-selection correction; IV for endogeneity
- **Heterogeneity**: Subsample analyses by technological turbulence and IPR strength

## Datasets/materials used

- Spanish Community Innovation Surveys (CIS 2000, CIS 2002) — N = 2,265 firms, cross-industry

## Key findings

1. AC positively moderates the spillover → innovation relationship.
2. Effect is stronger in high-turbulence sectors.
3. Effect is stronger under strong IPR protection (somewhat counterintuitive — formal spillovers are scarcer, so AC to access informal spillovers is more valuable).
4. Robust to selection correction and endogeneity controls.

## Limitations

- Spanish firms only; cross-section; causality limited despite IV
- AC proxied by R&D intensity — conflates innovative and absorptive capacity
- Does not distinguish basic from applied research in the AC proxy
- Innovation output by self-report survey

## Implications for the project domain

- Absorptive capacity is a prerequisite for benefiting from publicly funded basic research spillovers — relevant for evaluating whether German firms can absorb Fraunhofer/university research outputs
- Strong IPR environment in Germany amplifies the premium on building AC, because formal spillovers are restricted
- For TRL analysis: firms operating at low TRLs need higher AC to use early-stage public research; funding programs that don't build AC may fail to generate downstream innovation
