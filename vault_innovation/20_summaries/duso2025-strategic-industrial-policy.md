## Detailed summary

This article argues that Germany's industrial problem is not primarily a simple cost problem and cannot be solved by broad horizontal relief alone. The authors acknowledge pressure on German industry, including firm-level announcements of cuts and stagnation in manufacturing investment. But they argue that official statistics do not yet show a straightforward deindustrialisation collapse. The more important issue is an investment and innovation backlog caused by structural transformation, systemic risk, and coordination failures.

The article examines the energy-cost argument directly. Using official statistics and cost-structure data, the authors show that most German manufacturing gross production value is generated in sectors where energy costs account for a relatively small share of total expenses. For many sectors, wages are a much larger cost component. Reducing electricity costs or cutting red tape can help the business environment, but these measures alone will not address the long-term investment blockage.

The core mechanism is a technological investment trap. Decarbonisation, hydrogen, electromobility, battery recycling, resilient supply chains, and strategic infrastructure all require complementary investments by many actors. A firm's payoff depends on whether other firms, infrastructure providers, and value-chain partners also invest. This creates systemic risks that even large firms cannot carry alone. Resilience measures such as stockpiling, insourcing, supplier diversification, nearshoring, and friendshoring also raise private costs, while short-term competition rewards firms that avoid those costs. The result is underinvestment in transformation and resilience.

The authors argue that the state can help solve this market failure by acting as a coordinator, absorbing part of the systemic risk, and sharing costs. But they do not argue for indiscriminate protection or national champions. Their preferred approach is strategic, European, and competition-oriented industrial policy. Public support should focus on future-oriented investment and innovation rather than running costs. Funding should be allocated through transparent, efficient, contestable mechanisms such as open tenders, objective evaluation criteria, open-access obligations for publicly funded infrastructure, and targeted public procurement.

The European level is central because climate-neutral technologies, supply-chain resilience, technological sovereignty, and strategic value chains exceed national capacities. The article identifies IPCEIs as one institutional model for European-coordinated industrial policy. IPCEIs can support industrial use of new technologies across member states and can extend close to commercial use and mass-production capacity. Existing IPCEIs have focused on microelectronics, batteries, and hydrogen. The authors argue that IPCEIs need broader scope, stronger financing, more efficient implementation, and greater transparency.

The financing section adds three layers. First, Germany's EUR 500 billion special infrastructure fund can address horizontal needs such as roads, bridges, schools, rail, grids, hydrogen infrastructure, broadband, and public administration modernization. Second, vertical industrial-policy measures require systematic review of existing programs and subsidies, possible redirection of inefficient spending, debt-financed investment where justified, a revolving fund for key industrial projects, and potentially a European Innovation and Industrial Development Fund financed by common borrowing. Third, defence spending can stimulate innovation through advanced technologies such as AI-driven systems, drones, cryptographic security, and dual-use technologies, but this requires strong European procurement coordination and governance reform.

For the wiki, this article is a governance and policy-design source. It is not a firm-level impact evaluation, not a TRL classification source, and not a project-level Foerderkatalog analysis.

## Research question

How should Germany and Europe design industrial policy to overcome technological investment blockages while avoiding inefficient, market-distorting subsidies and national-champion protection?

## Core argument or contribution

The article's contribution is a policy-design argument for industrial policy that is strategic in priorities, European in scale, and competition-oriented in governance. It links industrial stagnation to coordination failures and systemic risks rather than to costs alone.

## Methodology

- Source type: policy article and institutional-design argument.
- Evidence base: descriptive statistics on real gross value added, energy-cost shares, and equipment investment; examples from German industry; literature and policy references.
- Analytical mechanism: coordination failure, systemic risk, investment trap, and under-provision of transformation/resilience investments.
- Policy comparison: horizontal cost relief versus vertical strategic industrial policy; national versus European scale; incumbent protection versus competition-oriented allocation.

## Key findings

- German industry is under pressure, but the article does not present the evidence as a simple completed deindustrialisation trend.
- Energy costs are not the dominant cost driver for most German manufacturing sectors.
- Broad tax relief, electricity-cost relief, and general investment subsidies can improve conditions but do not solve the technological investment trap.
- Structural transformation creates complementary-investment problems in hydrogen, electromobility, battery recycling, climate-neutral production, and strategic infrastructure.
- Supply-chain resilience creates private costs that firms may underinvest in when competitors avoid those costs.
- The state can reduce systemic risk and coordinate complementary investments.
- Strategic industrial policy should focus on innovation, future technologies, infrastructure, and resilience rather than running-cost subsidies.
- Competition-oriented mechanisms are needed to avoid inefficient support and market distortion.
- IPCEIs are promising but need broader scope, stronger financing, greater efficiency, and more transparency.
- Financing should combine infrastructure investment, subsidy review and reallocation, possible revolving funds, EU-level common instruments, and coordinated defence innovation where appropriate.

## Limitations

- The article is a policy argument, not an econometric evaluation.
- It does not estimate the causal impact of IPCEIs, infrastructure funds, defence procurement, or subsidy review.
- It does not classify individual projects by TRL, radicalness, or public value.
- Its evidence is descriptive and illustrative; implementation would require more detailed legal, fiscal, administrative, and program-evaluation work.
- Its relevance to Foerderkatalog or Bundesinnovationshaushalt analysis is inferential, not based on project-level federal funding data.
