## Detailed summary

Scientific knowledge is a public good — once published, rivals can use it. So why do firms invest in basic research at all? This paper provides a new empirical answer: firms invest in research primarily because they use it in their own inventive activity. Using matched data on corporate scientific publications (Web of Science) and patent front-page citations to those publications, the authors measure (a) internal use (patents by the same firm citing the firm's own science) and (b) spillovers (patents by rival firms citing the focal firm's science).

The central finding is that internal use is the main driver of research investment, not prestige, attracting talent, or absorptive capacity per se. Firms that successfully link their science to their own patents invest more in research. The positive effect of one additional internal citation on future publications is equivalent in magnitude to the combined negative effect of four external citations to competitors. Put differently: spillovers discourage research, but if a firm can use its science internally, the private return is high enough to sustain investment despite the leakage.

The paper also documents a significant selection mechanism: research that is internally used is both more highly valued (as measured by patent citations) and more productive in generating follow-on research.

## Research question

Why do firms invest in scientific research, given that knowledge is a public good and leaks to competitors?

## Core argument or contribution

Corporate investment in research is primarily driven by the private return from internal use in inventive activity. The internal appropriation channel — firms building their patents on their own science — is the key mechanism. This shifts the explanation away from absorptive capacity (Cohen-Levinthal) or reputation effects and toward a direct input view of corporate science.

## Methodology

- Panel regressions on 4,736 U.S. publicly listed, R&D-performing companies, 1980–2006
- Corporate research measured by scientific publications authored by firm employees (Web of Science)
- Internal use measured by self-citations in firm patents to firm publications
- Spillovers measured by citations from rival firms' patents to focal firm's publications
- Identification via within-firm variation; instrumental variable checks

## Datasets/materials used

- Web of Science (WoS): corporate scientific publications
- USPTO patent data with front-page non-patent literature (NPL) citations
- Compustat: firm financials
- Sample: ~300,000 corporate publications; 50,494 cited at least once in patents

## Key findings

1. **Internal use drives research investment**: firms producing science they can use in own patents invest more in research.
2. **Quantified private return**: one internal self-citation offsets the deterrent effect of four external competitor citations.
3. **Research quality premium**: internally used research is more productive and more highly cited.
4. **Implication**: firms invest in research not primarily to absorb external knowledge but to produce knowledge they can use themselves — consistent with Griliches' (1986) view that industrial research is tied to product/process development.

## Limitations

- U.S. large publicly listed firms only — may not generalize to SMEs or non-U.S. contexts
- Publication-based measure misses tacit knowledge transfers within firms
- Period 1980–2006 predates recent shifts in open innovation intensity
