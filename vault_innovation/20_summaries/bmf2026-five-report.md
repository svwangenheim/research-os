## Detailed summary

The FIVE report argues that Europe has a scaleup-financing problem: it can generate startups and research output, but too rarely converts them into global technology champions. The report frames this as a competitiveness, productivity, sovereignty, and capital-market problem. Its policy focus is late-stage financing for innovative companies, especially growth and pre-IPO finance, rather than early research grants or project-level technology readiness.

The report starts from a diagnosis of Europe's missing technology champions. It states that over the past 50 years Europe has produced only a handful of firms with market capitalisation above EUR 10 billion and none above EUR 100 billion, while the United States has produced hundreds of EUR 10 billion-plus firms and all six trillion-euro companies. It links this to productivity: euro-area labour productivity was broadly on par with the United States at the end of the twentieth century but is now about 20 percent lower, with the gap attributed largely to the tech sector.

The report calls this situation paradoxical because Europe has a strong early-stage startup ecosystem, a large base of scientific research, and high-quality higher-education institutions. The problem is not presented as a lack of startups alone, but as a failure to finance and scale promising firms at the point where they need large, repeated funding rounds, integrated markets, global distribution, and deep exit channels.

The central mechanism is the "scaleup gap". European companies struggle in the transition from proven innovation to large-scale production and global distribution. The report argues that Europe critically lacks domestic late-stage venture capital: EU venture investment is reported at about 0.2 percent of GDP, compared with 0.7 percent in the United States, with the shortfall especially severe at growth and pre-IPO stages. European VC funds are also smaller on average, limiting their ability to support successive large rounds or tickets above EUR 100 million.

The report identifies several structural causes. First, Europe lacks deep pools of funded pension assets. EU pension assets are reported at about 25 percent of GDP, compared with 150 percent in the United States; only Denmark, Sweden, and the Netherlands have large developed funded pension systems. The report argues that funded retirement savings are well suited to venture and growth finance because they have long investment horizons. Second, insurers and other institutional investors remain underallocated to VC and growth equity because of risk preferences, guaranteed products, and prudential constraints, though Solvency II changes may help. Third, Europe's equity markets are fragmented and less liquid, with many listing and trading venues, clearing counterparties, and securities depositories. Fourth, single-market fragmentation, regulatory complexity, divergent corporate law, employee-participation rules, procurement systems, and stock-option regimes raise the cost of scaling across borders.

The report's reform agenda is organized around five policy areas. It recommends ambitious supplementary pension reforms, deployment of Tibi/WIN-style initiatives to mobilize institutional capital, continued public support for scaleups through instruments such as ETCI 2.0 and better coordinated national promotional institutions, an optional "28th" EU corporate-law regime, and improvements to listing and trading frameworks. Public financing is treated as important and catalytic, but insufficient on its own if Europe does not mobilize private institutional capital and deepen capital markets.

## Research question

How can Europe close its late-stage scaleup-financing gap so that innovative startups can become global technology leaders, and what reforms to pensions, institutional investment, public finance, corporate law, and capital markets are needed?

## Core argument or contribution

The report argues that Europe's innovation problem is partly a scaleup-financing and market-fragmentation problem. Europe has research output and early-stage startups, but lacks the deep, patient, integrated capital pools and legal-market infrastructure needed to support large innovative firms through late-stage growth and public-market transitions.

## Methodology

The report combines expert assessment, policy synthesis, existing data and reports cited in footnotes, and stakeholder consultation. The preface states that the work was informed by more than 50 interview partners across Europe, including investors, entrepreneurs, policymakers, regulators, and industry experts.

This is a policy report and reform proposal. It is not an econometric evaluation, does not estimate causal treatment effects, and does not analyze a project-level grants dataset.

## Datasets/materials used

The report relies on secondary evidence and cited policy/market reports, including sources such as ECB productivity analysis, European Parliament analysis of software/cloud dependencies, Atomico's State of European Tech, IMF work on venture capital in Europe, JRC work on EU unicorns, and the Draghi competitiveness report. It also uses stakeholder interviews and expert assessment.

No canonical dataset note is linked because the report does not use CORDIS, the Foerderkatalog, or another vault dataset as its empirical base.

## Key findings

- Europe has produced only a handful of firms above EUR 10 billion market capitalisation in the past 50 years and none above EUR 100 billion; the United States has produced hundreds above EUR 10 billion and all six trillion-euro companies.
- Euro-area labour productivity is described as about 20 percent below the US level, with the widening gap attributed mainly to the tech sector.
- Europe has a strong early-stage startup ecosystem and research base, but this does not translate reliably into global technology champions.
- EU venture investment is reported at about 0.2 percent of GDP compared with 0.7 percent in the United States, with a particularly severe shortage at growth and pre-IPO stages.
- European pension assets are reported at about 25 percent of GDP, compared with 150 percent in the United States; the report treats this as a structural constraint on patient innovation finance.
- European institutional investors, especially insurers and pension funds, remain underallocated to VC and growth equity.
- Fragmented public equity markets, many listing/trading venues, and post-trade complexity reduce liquidity and IPO attractiveness.
- Single-market fragmentation, regulatory complexity, divergent corporate-law regimes, procurement differences, and fragmented stock-option regimes raise scaleup costs.
- Recommended responses include funded supplementary pensions, Tibi/WIN-style institutional-capital mobilization, ETCI 2.0 and better public catalytic finance, a 28th corporate-law regime, and listing/trading framework improvements.

## Limitations

- The report is a policy diagnosis and recommendation document, not a peer-reviewed empirical study.
- It relies on secondary evidence, stakeholder interviews, and expert assessment rather than a single transparent microdataset.
- Its causal claims about scaleup barriers are plausible policy synthesis, but not treatment-effect estimates.
- It focuses on European scaleup finance and capital-market infrastructure, not on early-stage R&D grants, TRL classification, or German project-funding allocation.
- It does not analyze CORDIS, the Foerderkatalog, PROFI, or specific public subsidy recipients.

## Implications for LLM research or the project domain

For the Bundesinnovationshaushalt/TRL project, the report is useful for a complementary layer of interpretation: even well-targeted R&D and transfer funding may not produce European champions if late-stage finance, pensions, institutional-investor allocation, public-market liquidity, and single-market rules remain weak.

The source should not be used to claim that Foerderkatalog projects crowd out private finance, that TRL 3-6 or TRL 7-9 is the right funding target, or that a specific German grant portfolio is misallocated. Those claims require separate project-level and evaluation evidence. The defensible project use is to show that scaleup finance is a distinct constraint downstream of research, validation, and early commercialization.
