## Detailed summary

Beck, Lopes-Bento, and Schenker-Wicki evaluate where public R&D support has its strongest effects. Instead of asking only whether subsidies increase total R&D or total innovation output, they distinguish between incremental innovation close to the market and radical innovation that is farther from the market, riskier, and more affected by market failure.

The paper's theoretical starting point is standard market-failure reasoning. Because firms cannot appropriate all social benefits of R&D and because radical projects face higher uncertainty, risk, financing constraints, and longer horizons, private firms may underinvest especially in radical innovation. If public R&D support is well targeted, the effect should therefore be strongest where market failures are strongest: in radical innovation rather than incremental innovation.

The institutional setting is Swiss innovation policy. Switzerland has two major public R&D funding agencies in the source's description: the Swiss National Science Foundation, which mainly funds public research institutions and individual researchers, and the Commission for Technology and Innovation (CTI), which is responsible for private-sector R&D projects and is therefore the main subsidy source in the study. CTI supports applied and market-oriented projects as well as high-risk but promising projects, encourages collaboration between companies and public research institutes, and provides matched grants after external and internal evaluation.

The empirical design uses representative firm-level data from the Swiss innovation survey covering 1999-2011, accessed through KOF Swiss Economic Institute. The authors first estimate input additionality: whether subsidized firms invest more in R&D than comparable non-subsidized firms would have done. Because subsidies are not randomly assigned, they use propensity-score matching to construct a counterfactual comparison group. They then analyze output additionality with heterogeneity-robust Tobit models, distinguishing radical and incremental product innovation. The analysis also considers collaboration patterns and partner diversity because Swiss policy encourages collaborative R&D.

The main finding is that Swiss public R&D support increases R&D investment on average and that publicly induced R&D affects radical innovation, not incremental innovation. In contrast, privately motivated R&D matters for both radical and incremental innovation. Collaborative R&D shows significant effects mainly for incremental innovation, and the paper finds no evidence that collaboration strengthens the effect of public R&D policy on radical innovation. The authors conclude that Swiss public R&D policy is effective in stimulating R&D and in intervening where market imperfections are highest, but that encouraging R&D collaboration does not appear to enhance these policy effects.

## Research question

Does public R&D support have its strongest effect on incremental innovation close to the market or on radical innovation that is riskier, farther from the market, and more affected by market failure?

## Core argument or contribution

The paper contributes an additionality evaluation that separates innovation outcomes by degree of novelty. Its core contribution is evidence that public R&D support in Switzerland primarily affects radical innovation, which is where market-failure theory predicts public intervention should matter most.

## Methodology

- Representative firm-level Swiss innovation survey data, 1999-2011.
- Propensity-score matching to estimate input additionality while accounting for selection into subsidy receipt.
- Treatment-effect estimation of how subsidies affect firms' R&D investments.
- Heterogeneity-robust Tobit models for output additionality.
- Separate outcomes for radical and incremental product innovation following OECD/Oslo Manual style definitions.
- Collaboration analysis by type/diversity of collaboration partners.
- Robustness checks including endogeneity checks for collaboration variables.

## Datasets/materials used

- Swiss innovation survey firm-level data, accessed via KOF Swiss Economic Institute at ETH Zurich.
- Swiss CTI policy and project-funding context.
- Firm-level measures of R&D expenditure, subsidy receipt, collaboration, radical innovation, and incremental innovation.
- Institutional information from CTI reports and Swiss public R&D funding statistics.

## Key findings

- Subsidized firms increase R&D investment relative to matched counterfactual firms, indicating input additionality.
- Publicly induced R&D has a significant effect on radical innovation.
- No significant effect of policy-induced R&D is found for incremental innovation.
- Privately motivated R&D is significant for both radical and incremental innovation.
- Collaborative R&D is significant mainly for incremental innovation.
- The impact of public R&D policy is not improved by the collaboration patterns examined.
- The results support the claim that Swiss R&D support "hits" where market failure is highest.

## Limitations

- The empirical setting is Switzerland and mainly CTI-linked private-sector R&D support; results should not be transferred mechanically to Germany.
- Radical and incremental innovation are survey-based product-innovation outcomes, not TRL classifications.
- Propensity-score matching controls for observed differences, but unobserved selection remains a possible concern in any nonexperimental evaluation.
- The paper evaluates firm-level R&D policy effects, not administrative project text, funding-recipient concentration, or Foerderkatalog portfolios.
- Collaboration effects depend on measured partner categories and may not capture all collaboration quality or governance differences.

## Implications for LLM research or the project domain

For [[70_projects/bundesinnovationshaushalt-trl.md]], this paper motivates distinguishing public funding by innovation novelty rather than treating all R&D support as equivalent. It does not validate TRL as a measure of radical or incremental innovation, and it does not analyze German Foerderkatalog data. The correct project bridge is methodological: use the paper to justify testing whether German public funding is associated with more radical or more incremental project profiles, while keeping the German evidence separate.
