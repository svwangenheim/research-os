## Detailed summary

Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen, and Webb ask whether sustained economic growth increasingly requires more research effort because research productivity is falling. The paper starts from idea-based growth models in which growth equals research productivity multiplied by the effective number of researchers. If growth remains stable while the number of researchers rises sharply, then the implied productivity of research effort must be falling.

The paper's core contribution is to treat this not only as an aggregate macroeconomic accounting issue, but as an empirical question that can be studied in multiple domains where idea outputs and research inputs are measurable. The authors argue that aggregate evidence alone could be misleading because new product varieties or composition effects might hide stable micro-level idea productivity. They therefore examine several more disaggregated settings: semiconductors and Moore's Law, agricultural yields, medical innovations, and firm-level data.

The semiconductor case is the most vivid. Moore's Law delivered a roughly steady exponential increase in transistor density for decades, but maintaining that pace required a much larger research workforce over time. The paper reports that the number of researchers needed to achieve the same chip-density doubling rate became more than 18 times larger than in the early 1970s, implying a sharp decline in research productivity in semiconductors.

The same broad pattern appears outside semiconductors. In agriculture, the paper studies yield growth for crops such as corn, soybeans, cotton, and wheat and compares output improvements to research effort. In medical innovation, it studies innovation related to cancer and heart disease mortality improvements. In firm-level data, it uses Compustat to examine whether firms' research productivity declines as R&D effort rises. The authors report substantial heterogeneity across firms, but declining research productivity in a large majority of the firm sample.

At the aggregate US level, the authors revisit the Jones-style observation that long-run growth has not accelerated despite a very large expansion in the number of researchers. The paper reports that aggregate US research productivity declined by a factor of about 41 since the 1930s, equivalent to an average decline of more than 5 percent per year. The authors' interpretation is not that research has become useless; rather, more researchers are required to sustain a given rate of proportional improvement because easier ideas have already been found or because progress moves the frontier into harder problems.

For innovation-policy use, the paper is important because it weakens the simple inference that more R&D spending automatically implies more growth. Rising R&D effort may be necessary just to maintain existing growth rates. However, the paper does not itself evaluate public R&D subsidies, German funding programs, TRL allocation, or firm-selection rules.

## Research question

Are ideas becoming harder to find, in the sense that maintaining a given rate of technological or productivity improvement requires substantially more research effort over time?

## Core argument or contribution

The paper provides a cross-domain empirical argument that research effort is rising while research productivity is falling. Stable exponential progress in several domains is therefore sustained by increasing research input, not by stable or improving idea productivity.

## Methodology

The authors use an idea-production-function and growth-accounting framework. Conceptually, growth or idea output is decomposed into research productivity and research effort. Empirically, they construct domain-specific measures of idea output and research input, then examine how output per unit of research effort changes over time.

Applications include:

- aggregate US productivity growth versus the number of researchers;
- Moore's Law, using chip-performance progress and semiconductor research effort;
- agricultural yield improvements and agricultural research effort;
- medical innovation related to cancer and heart disease outcomes;
- firm-level R&D and productivity evidence using Compustat.

The method is descriptive and accounting-based, not a causal evaluation of a policy intervention.

## Key findings

- Aggregate US research productivity is estimated to have declined by a factor of about 41 since the 1930s, an average decline of more than 5 percent per year.
- In semiconductors, sustaining Moore's Law required more than 18 times as many researchers as in the early 1970s; the paper reports an average research-productivity decline of about 6.8 percent per year in that case.
- Agricultural seed-yield research productivity also declines substantially, with the paper reporting a decline around 5 percent per year.
- Medical-innovation cases for cancer and heart disease show a similar pattern of rising research effort relative to outcome improvements.
- Firm-level Compustat evidence is noisier and heterogeneous, but the paper reports declining research productivity for more than 85 percent of firms in the sample and an average decline around 10 percent per year.
- The general pattern is that observed growth can remain approximately exponential because research effort rises enough to offset declining research productivity.

## Limitations

- The paper's measures of "ideas" are necessarily proxies: transistor density, crop yields, mortality improvements, productivity measures, and firm-level outcomes each capture only part of innovation output.
- The case studies are selected because inputs and outputs are measurable; they may not represent all innovation domains.
- Aggregate estimates depend on assumptions about the link between researchers and productivity growth.
- Firm-level evidence from Compustat is acknowledged as lower quality than the detailed case studies.
- The paper does not distinguish all reasons why research productivity falls, and it does not directly test specific policy remedies.
- It does not evaluate German R&D funding, Foerderkatalog projects, TRL targeting, or SME/incumbent allocation.

## Implications for LLM research or the project domain

For the Bundesinnovationshaushalt/TRL project, this paper supports a caution: aggregate R&D spending alone is not a sufficient success metric. If research productivity is falling, increasing R&D inputs may be necessary but not enough to raise productivity growth. A funding analysis should therefore distinguish input volume from output quality, additionality, spillovers, and project positioning.

The paper does not prove that German public funding is misallocated, that incumbent recipients are unproductive, or that a specific TRL range should receive more funding. Those are project hypotheses that require separate evidence from funding data, firm-level innovation outcomes, and policy-evaluation sources.
