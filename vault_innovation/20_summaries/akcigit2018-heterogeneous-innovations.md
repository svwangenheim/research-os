## Detailed summary

Akcigit and Kerr develop a Schumpeterian/endogenous-growth model that distinguishes between two forms of innovation. Internal innovations improve products or technologies already owned by an incumbent firm. External innovations add new product lines and can allow firms or entrants to capture markets from others. This distinction matters because innovations differ not only in quality but also in type, and because firm size affects incentives to pursue each type. The paper's central theoretical move is to extend the Klette-Kortum multi-product firm framework so that firms of different sizes can choose different innovation strategies. Large firms have stronger incentives to improve existing product lines because they can apply improvements over a larger portfolio. Entrants and smaller firms, by contrast, are relatively more connected to external innovations and major advances because they have fewer existing lines to optimize and because their growth depends more on acquiring new lines. The empirical and quantitative part links the model to U.S. Census Bureau operating data and patent data. Patent citations are used to connect innovation outcomes to growth-relevant spillovers and to discipline the distribution of innovation quality. The paper reports that internal innovation scales moderately faster with firm size than external innovation. It also shows that small firms grow faster on average, have higher R&D intensity relative to sales, and have a higher conditional probability that a successful innovation is a major innovation. A useful headline decomposition from the paper is that aggregate growth due to innovation comes from multiple sources: internal efforts by incumbents, external efforts by incumbents, and entry. The introduction reports a decomposition of 19.8 percent from internal incumbent efforts, 54.5 percent from external incumbent efforts, and 25.7 percent from new entrants. This is important for the vault because it means the paper is not a simple "small firms good, large firms bad" argument. Incumbents also perform external innovation, and internal innovation accounts for a meaningful share of growth. The policy-relevant point is more precise: innovation type and firm dynamics matter, so aggregate R&D spending alone is a weak guide to social growth value.

## Research question

How do different types of innovation vary across firm size, and what are the aggregate growth implications when firms undertake both internal improvements to existing products and external innovations that add or replace product lines?

## Core argument or contribution

The paper's contribution is to model, measure, and quantify heterogeneous innovation types within an endogenous-growth framework. It shows that the firm-size distribution is not neutral for growth: different-sized firms choose different mixes of internal and external innovation, and these choices shape growth, creative destruction, and the prevalence of major innovations.

## Methodology

- Endogenous-growth model with multi-product firms.
- Distinction between internal innovation and external innovation.
- Patent citation structure used to connect innovation outcomes to quality and spillover content.
- Quantitative model estimated/calibrated using matched U.S. Census Bureau operating data and patent data.
- Indirect inference and model moments based on firm size, growth, R&D intensity, patenting, and citation patterns.
- Counterfactual/theoretical analysis of how firm-size scaling in external innovation changes growth dynamics.

## Key findings

- Innovation differs by both quality and type; a one-type innovation model misses important firm-size dynamics.
- Internal innovation improves existing products and scales more strongly with firm size.
- External innovation adds product lines, can involve creative destruction, and is relatively more important for entrants and smaller firms.
- Small firms have higher average growth and higher R&D intensity relative to sales in the model and data patterns discussed by the paper.
- Conditional on successful innovation, smaller firms have a higher probability of producing major innovations.
- The paper reports that aggregate innovation-driven growth is decomposed across internal incumbent efforts, external incumbent efforts, and entrants; all three matter.
- The paper supports policy analysis that distinguishes the allocation and type of R&D from aggregate R&D volume.

## Limitations

- The paper is a structural growth-model and innovation-economics contribution, not a direct evaluation of a specific subsidy program.
- Results depend on model assumptions about innovation production, patent-citation interpretation, and mapping patent outcomes to growth spillovers.
- The empirical setting is U.S. firm and patent data; transfer to German public R&D funding requires care.
- Internal/external innovation is related to, but not identical with, TRL level, incumbent status, or radical/incremental innovation in every empirical setting.

## Implications for LLM research or the project domain

For [[70_projects/bundesinnovationshaushalt-trl.md]], this paper is useful as a conceptual model for why the type and recipient of innovation funding matter. It supports coding distinctions between incumbent-oriented internal improvements and more external/disruptive innovation trajectories. However, the mapping from internal/external innovation to TRL classes is inferential rather than directly estimated in the paper. The correct use in the project is as theory for why a funding portfolio dominated by incumbent incremental projects may have lower growth potential than its aggregate R&D volume suggests.
