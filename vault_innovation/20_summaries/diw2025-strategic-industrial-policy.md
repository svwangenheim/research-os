## Detailed summary

This DIW brief argues that Germany's industrial-policy debate is too focused on general cost relief and not focused enough on the structural investment and innovation blockages facing industry. The authors accept that German industry is under pressure from high costs, structural change, and global disruption, but they reject the idea that the central answer is broad tax relief, general investment subsidies, or lower electricity prices for all firms. Those measures may improve production conditions, but the authors argue that they do not solve the deeper technological investment trap.

The source first tempers the deindustrialization narrative. It notes that industrial employment and output indicators show stress but not yet a simple picture of comprehensive deindustrialization. It then uses official statistics on energy-cost shares to argue that energy prices are not the main cost problem for most manufacturing sectors. Horizontal measures such as lower electricity taxes, lower grid fees, or general bureaucracy reduction may help the business environment, but they are not enough to resolve long-term innovation and investment blockages.

The main diagnosis is a coordination failure around structural transformation. Decarbonizing industrial production, building resilient supply chains, and investing in new green technologies require many complementary investments across firms, infrastructure providers, and value chains. Electric vehicles need charging infrastructure and battery recycling; climate-neutral steel depends on hydrogen production and transport; resilient supply chains require firms to bear costs for diversification, inventories, or regional sourcing even when short-term competition rewards firms that do not pay those resilience costs. These interdependencies create systemic risks that individual firms, including large firms, cannot solve alone.

The policy response proposed by the authors is a European, competition-oriented, strategic industrial policy. They argue against creating national champions as the main goal. Instead, public support should use competition-oriented mechanisms where possible, including open calls, objective evaluation criteria, open-access obligations for publicly financed infrastructure, and public procurement with clear targets. The brief emphasizes future-oriented investment and innovation rather than subsidizing running costs.

The authors also argue that Germany cannot solve these problems alone. The relevant challenges involve technological sovereignty, strategic value chains, infrastructure, and EU-wide fragmentation. The European level should coordinate resources, reduce risk, and prevent fragmented industrial-policy competition among member states. The brief identifies Important Projects of Common European Interest (IPCEIs) as one practical instrument. Existing IPCEIs in microelectronics, battery-cell production, and hydrogen show the institutional form, but the authors argue that IPCEIs need broader scope, stronger financing, more efficient implementation, and more transparency.

For the wiki, this source is most useful as a policy-argument note on why project-based, competition-oriented, European industrial policy may be preferable to generic horizontal support. It is not an econometric evaluation of subsidy effects, not a TRL measurement method, and not a dataset source.

## Research question

How should Germany and Europe respond to industrial investment blockages caused by decarbonization, geopolitical risk, resilient supply chains, and technological coordination failures?

## Core argument or contribution

The brief's core contribution is a diagnosis and policy design argument: Germany's industrial weakness is partly a technological and systemic investment trap, so the policy response should be strategic, European, competition-oriented, and focused on future technologies and infrastructure rather than broad cost subsidies or national-champion protection.

## Methodology

- Source type: applied policy brief.
- Evidence base: descriptive industrial indicators, official statistics on industrial employment and energy-cost shares, policy examples, and institutional comparison.
- Analytical mechanism: coordination failure and systemic risk in structural transformation.
- Policy design comparison: horizontal cost relief versus strategic vertical industrial policy; national approaches versus European coordination; generic subsidies versus competitive project-based support.

## Key findings

- Germany's industry is under pressure, but the source does not present the situation as simple completed deindustrialization.
- Energy-cost relief is not enough because many manufacturing sectors have relatively low energy-cost shares compared with other cost components.
- Broad horizontal policies may strengthen production conditions but do not resolve the investment and innovation backlog.
- Decarbonization, resilient supply chains, electric mobility, hydrogen, batteries, and strategic infrastructure require complementary investments across value chains.
- These complementarities create coordination failures and systemic risks that individual firms cannot resolve alone.
- The state can act as a coordinator by reducing systemic risks, sharing costs, and supporting complementary investments.
- Industrial policy should avoid distorting competition where possible and should use competitive, transparent allocation mechanisms.
- Strategic support should focus on future-oriented investments and key technologies rather than subsidizing running costs.
- Germany needs a European policy approach because technological sovereignty and resilient value chains exceed national capacities.
- IPCEIs are a relevant instrument but need broader scope, more financing, and more efficient and transparent implementation.

## Limitations

- The source is a policy brief, not a causal impact evaluation.
- It does not estimate treatment effects of IPCEIs or other industrial-policy instruments.
- It does not provide project-level data, TRL labels, or machine-readable classification rules.
- The descriptive statistics support the argument but do not by themselves prove the effectiveness of the proposed policy mix.
- The source's recommendations are institutional and strategic; implementation details would require additional legal, fiscal, and program-design analysis.
