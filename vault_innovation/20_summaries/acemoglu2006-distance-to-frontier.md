## Detailed summary

Acemoglu, Aghion, and Zilibotti develop a theory of why the growth strategy that is appropriate for a country depends on its distance from the world technology frontier. The paper contrasts an investment-based strategy, which relies on long-term relationships, incumbent firms, large investments, and limited selection, with an innovation-based strategy, which relies more heavily on selecting high-skill managers and allowing younger firms and entrants to replace less successful incumbents.

The central mechanism is that backward economies can grow by adopting and adapting existing frontier technologies. In that setting, experience, retained earnings, large incumbents, and state-supported investment can raise growth even if competition and selection are muted. As an economy approaches the frontier, however, copying becomes less sufficient and productivity growth depends more on innovation. Innovation requires better selection of managers and firms, so policies that protect incumbents, reduce competition, or preserve an investment-based regime become increasingly costly.

The paper also adds a political-economy mechanism. Policies that were useful during catch-up can create powerful incumbent beneficiaries. Those beneficiaries may then resist institutional change and keep an economy stuck in an investment-based regime after it should have switched toward innovation-based growth. This produces non-convergence traps and a form of leapfrogging: economies that initially grow quickly under protected investment regimes can later stagnate and be overtaken by more competitive, selection-oriented economies.

## Research question

When should an economy rely on investment-based catch-up institutions, and when does proximity to the technology frontier make innovation-based selection, entry, and competition more important for long-run growth?

## Core argument or contribution

The paper's contribution is a distance-to-frontier theory of institutional choice. It explains why policies that encourage incumbent investment and technology adoption can be growth-enhancing for countries far from the frontier but growth-reducing for countries near the frontier, where innovation and selection become more important. It also shows how political capture by beneficiaries of earlier policies can prevent the necessary institutional switch.

## Methodology

Formal growth theory with political economy. The model compares investment-based and innovation-based growth strategies and studies how the optimal strategy changes with distance to the technology frontier. The mechanism turns on a tradeoff between experience/investment and selection: experienced incumbent managers can invest more, but stronger selection of managers and firms becomes more valuable when innovation rather than imitation drives growth. The paper then endogenizes policy persistence by allowing beneficiaries of investment-based institutions to use political influence to maintain those institutions.

## Datasets/materials used

The source is primarily theoretical. It uses historical examples, cross-country growth patterns, and references to cases such as nineteenth-century European catch-up, Japan, South Korea, Latin American import-substitution experiences, Hong Kong, and Singapore to motivate and interpret the model. It is not a reusable empirical dataset note and should not be linked to a canonical dataset unless a later note separately extracts the empirical sources used in the paper.

## Key findings

- Economies far from the technology frontier can benefit from investment-based institutions that support adoption, large incumbent investment, and long-term relationships.
- As economies approach the frontier, the returns to selection rise because growth increasingly depends on innovation rather than imitation.
- Near-frontier economies therefore need more innovation-based institutions: more entry, more competition, more managerial and firm selection, and less protection of unsuccessful incumbents.
- A country can switch out of the investment-based strategy too early or too late, but failure to switch eventually can create a non-convergence trap.
- Political economy makes late switching more likely: incumbent beneficiaries of investment-based policies may use political influence to preserve institutions that are no longer growth-enhancing.
- The model provides a theory of leapfrogging in which initially successful protected catch-up economies later stagnate and are overtaken by more competitive economies.

## Limitations

- The paper is a theoretical model, not a direct evaluation of any specific R&D subsidy program.
- Its historical and cross-country examples are used to motivate and interpret the mechanism; they are not a project-level causal estimate.
- The concepts of investment-based and innovation-based strategy do not map mechanically onto TRL levels, firm age, or subsidy recipients.
- Applying the paper to Germany or the Förderkatalog requires separate evidence that Germany is near the frontier and that funding allocation protects incumbents or weakens selection.
