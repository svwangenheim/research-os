## Detailed summary

Malerba proposes the concept of a sectoral system of innovation and production as a way to analyze sectors dynamically and multidimensionally. The paper responds to two gaps in earlier research. Industrial economics had studied sector structure, entry, strategic interaction, and technical progress, but often treated sector boundaries as static and gave limited attention to learning, non-firm organizations, institutions, and sector transformation. Sector case-study research had produced rich evidence, but in fragmented ways that made cross-sector comparison difficult.

The paper defines a sectoral system as a set of new and established products for specific uses, together with the agents carrying out market and non-market interactions for the creation, production, and sale of those products. A sectoral system has a specific knowledge base, technologies, inputs, and demand. Its agents include firms, firm subunits, users, consumers, entrepreneurs, scientists, universities, financial institutions, government agencies, associations, and other non-firm organizations. These agents have specific learning processes, competencies, beliefs, objectives, organizational structures, and behaviors.

Interactions are central. Agents communicate, exchange, cooperate, compete, and command, and their interactions are shaped by institutions such as rules, regulations, norms, routines, and standards. Sectoral systems are not static containers. Their boundaries, agents, knowledge bases, technologies, demand, and institutions co-evolve over time. Change comes through variety creation, replication/inertia, selection, entry, exit, learning, and dynamic complementarities across products, technologies, and activities.

Malerba positions the sectoral-system framework as complementary to national, regional, and technological innovation-system approaches. National systems focus on country-level institutions and non-firm organizations; regional systems focus on locality; technological systems focus on networks around technologies. Sectoral systems instead start from products, demand, technologies, knowledge, and agent interactions in a sector. The same sector may cut across national boundaries, while national institutions can still affect how that sector performs in different countries.

The article is conceptual and methodological rather than empirical in the narrow sense. It proposes a framework, identifies key building blocks, and outlines research questions. The building blocks include products; agents; knowledge and learning processes; technologies, inputs, demand, and complementarities; interactions; institutions; and processes of selection and variety generation. Malerba argues that using these building blocks can improve understanding of sector boundaries, innovation patterns, firm and country performance, and policy proposals.

The paper gives examples of how technological regimes and knowledge bases shape sectoral dynamics. Traditional sectors, machinery, automobiles, and modern microelectronics/software/microcomputers are associated with different opportunity conditions, appropriability, cumulativeness, tacitness, innovator concentration, and spatial boundaries. These examples support the broader claim that sectors differ in systematic ways and that policy and analysis should not assume one universal innovation process.

## Research question

How can sectors be conceptualized as dynamic systems of innovation and production that integrate products, agents, knowledge, technologies, demand, institutions, and interactions rather than treating sectors as static market categories?

## Core argument or contribution

The paper's contribution is the sectoral-system framework: a sector should be analyzed as a co-evolving configuration of products, agents, knowledge bases, technologies, demand, interactions, and institutions. This gives a richer view of innovation and production than static industrial classifications or isolated sector case studies.

## Methodology

- Conceptual and methodological framework development.
- Synthesis of evolutionary economics, innovation-systems literature, industrial dynamics, and sector case-study traditions.
- Definition of core building blocks of sectoral systems.
- Use of examples from technological regimes and sectors to illustrate how sectoral systems differ.
- No new econometric identification or standalone dataset is introduced.

## Datasets/materials used

This is primarily a theoretical/conceptual article. It draws on prior empirical sector studies and the ESSY project context but does not introduce a new dataset for independent analysis.

## Key findings

- Sector boundaries should not be treated as fixed; they can shift as products, technologies, demand, and complementarities change.
- Innovation is shaped by firm and non-firm agents, including universities, public agencies, financial institutions, associations, users, and suppliers.
- Knowledge bases differ across sectors in tacitness, specificity, cumulativeness, sources, and learning processes.
- Institutions shape interactions among agents and can affect sectoral performance.
- Sectoral systems change through co-evolution among knowledge, technology, demand, agents, networks, institutions, variety creation, and selection.
- National and regional systems matter, but sectoral patterns can also have cross-country regularities.
- Public policy should be informed by sector-specific knowledge about agents, institutions, learning processes, and bottlenecks rather than assuming all sectors innovate in the same way.

## Limitations

- The article is conceptual and does not provide a causal empirical test.
- It outlines a broad framework whose operationalization requires sector-specific empirical work.
- It does not provide a ready-made taxonomy for coding every sector or technology.
- Some dimensions, such as within-sector firm heterogeneity, relationship structures, institutions, variety creation, selection, and co-evolution, are identified as needing deeper study.
- The source does not make specific claims about German Foerderkatalog allocations, TRLs, or the current German industrial structure.

## Implications for LLM research or the project domain

For the Bundesinnovationshaushalt/TRL project, Malerba is useful as a conceptual warning against one-size-fits-all interpretation of funding portfolios. A project's sector may shape the relevant knowledge base, expected innovation pathway, actor structure, and plausible TRL trajectory. Classification and synthesis should therefore avoid interpreting all technologies through the same innovation model.

The source does not itself show that German funding is biased toward incumbents or incremental innovation. Those are project-level hypotheses that require evidence from German portfolio, firm, or sector data.
