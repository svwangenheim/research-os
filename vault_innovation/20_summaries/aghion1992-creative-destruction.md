## Detailed summary

Aghion and Howitt build a formal endogenous-growth model in which sustained growth comes from vertical product innovations. Each innovation improves the productivity of intermediate goods, but it also makes the previous generation of technology obsolete. This is the paper's formalization of Schumpeterian creative destruction: growth is generated by the same process that destroys incumbent rents.

The model has a competitive research sector and a sequence of innovations. Successful innovators obtain temporary monopoly rents from the improved intermediate good until a later innovation replaces them. Current research therefore depends on expected future research: if future research is intense, the expected duration of current monopoly rents is shorter, which discourages current research. The paper also includes a general-equilibrium wage channel because skilled labor can be allocated between research and manufacturing.

The paper's normative result is that laissez-faire growth can be either too low or too high relative to the social optimum. Private innovators do not capture all knowledge spillovers, which can lead to too little research. But they also do not internalize the destruction of existing rents, which creates a business-stealing effect and can lead to too much research or innovations that are too small when innovation size is endogenous. This makes the paper important for the vault because it warns against treating more R&D or more innovation incentives as automatically socially optimal.

## Research question

How can a model of endogenous technical change explain sustained growth when innovation both raises productivity and destroys the rents attached to earlier technologies?

## Core argument or contribution

The paper contributes a canonical Schumpeterian growth model. Its core contribution is to make creative destruction endogenous: research competition generates vertical innovations, innovations create temporary monopoly rents, and later innovations destroy those rents. This links growth theory, patent races, monopoly rents, obsolescence, and welfare analysis in a single framework.

## Methodology

Formal theoretical modeling. The paper specifies a model with final goods, intermediate goods, skilled labor, a competitive research sector, stochastic innovation arrival, monopoly rents for successful innovators, and vertical quality improvements. It analyzes stationary equilibria, possible cyclical equilibria, growth-rate determinants, and welfare comparisons between laissez-faire and socially optimal research.

## Datasets/materials used

No empirical dataset. The source is a theoretical model. Its materials are the model assumptions, mathematical derivations, equilibrium analysis, and welfare comparison.

## Key findings

- Vertical innovations are the underlying source of long-run growth in the model.
- Innovations generate creative destruction: each successful innovation improves productivity while reducing or ending the rents from the previous technology.
- The expected amount of future research can discourage current research because future innovations shorten the expected duration of current monopoly rents.
- The average growth rate depends on innovation size, the skilled labor force, research productivity, and time preference.
- Laissez-faire growth can be below or above the social optimum because spillovers and business-stealing effects operate in opposite directions.
- When innovation size is endogenous, business stealing can make innovations too small.

## Limitations

- The paper is theoretical and does not estimate empirical subsidy effects, firm behavior, or country-level policy outcomes.
- It abstracts from many institutional details that matter for real innovation systems.
- It does not directly analyze SMEs, German innovation policy, TRL stages, public procurement, or the Förderkatalog.
- Use later empirical papers for evidence on subsidy additionality, firm selection, or patenting outcomes.
