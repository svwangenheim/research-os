# Killing the Golden Goose? The Decline of Science in Corporate R&D

- Source URLs: https://doi.org/10.1002/smj.2693
- Source status: Published. Strategic Management Journal 39(1): 3-32, 2018. DOI: 10.1002/smj.2693.

- Authors: Arora, Ashish; Belenzon, Sharon; Patacconi, Andrea
- Year: 2018
- Venue: Strategic Management Journal, Vol. 39, No. 1

## Detailed summary

This paper documents the long-run decline of corporate basic research in the United States and argues that this decline threatens the innovation system's productivity through two mechanisms: direct loss of breakthrough-oriented R&D, and weakened absorptive capacity at the science-industry interface.

**The empirical finding:** The share of corporate R&D devoted to basic research declined from approximately 30% of US BERD in 1985 to less than 20% by 2015. Publications per firm (a proxy for basic research orientation) fell by roughly 20% per decade over this period. This trend is concentrated in large firms and in sectors with strong science-industry linkages (pharmaceuticals, chemicals, electronics).

**The mechanism — markets for ideas:** The paper argues that the primary driver is the development of markets for ideas (open innovation, licensing, startup acquisitions). As external basic research (from universities, startups) became more accessible and cheaper to buy than to produce internally, large firms rationally substituted external sourcing for internal basic research. This is individually rational but collectively problematic because it reduces the number of organizations actively working on fundamental problems.

**The productivity consequence:** The authors link the decline in corporate basic research to observed productivity slowdowns. Consistent with Bloom et al. (2020) on declining research productivity, and with Cohen & Levinthal (1990) on absorptive capacity, they argue that firms that abandon basic research progressively lose both the outputs of that research and the ability to understand and exploit external science. Once a firm exits basic research, re-entry is costly due to lost human capital and institutional knowledge.

**The policy implication:** The paper argues that the market failure is not just in basic research production (the standard public goods argument) but also in the maintenance of absorptive capacity. Public policy should not assume that basic research is only a university/government function — firms conducting basic research generate positive externalities for the entire innovation ecosystem by maintaining the interface between fundamental science and commercial application.

## Research question

Why has corporate basic research declined in the US, and what are the consequences for innovation productivity?

## Core argument or contribution

1. **Empirical documentation:** First systematic evidence of the long-run decline in corporate basic research orientation (publications per firm, basic R&D share of BERD) from 1985-2015.
2. **Mechanism identification:** Markets for ideas (not regulatory burden or financialization alone) explain firms substituting external for internal basic research.
3. **System-level consequence:** Individual firm rationality produces collective irrationality — the science-industry interface degrades when firms stop doing basic research, even if universities expand.
4. **Policy implication:** Basic research is not substitutable from a systems perspective; maintaining corporate basic research capacity requires direct incentives beyond generic R&D tax credits.

## Methodology

- Panel data on US public firms (Compustat-linked), 1980-2015
- Dependent variables: publications per firm (Web of Science), basic R&D share of total R&D expenditure
- Identification: difference-in-differences with sector and time fixed effects; instrument = Bayh-Dole Act shock to university patenting (changes university-industry interface)
- Robustness: alternative publication/citation measures; subsample analyses by sector

## Key findings

1. Corporate basic research share fell from ~30% to <20% of US BERD (1985-2015)
2. Publications per firm fell ~20% per decade
3. Decline concentrated in large firms, not startups
4. Markets for ideas (patent licensing, startup acquisitions) explain most of the decline
5. Sectors with strong university linkages (pharma, chem, electronics) show steepest declines — consistent with substitution, not general retrenchment

## Limitations

- US context; European applicability uncertain (different university-industry interface, different VC markets)
- Publication-based proxy for basic research is imperfect — captures only publicly disclosed outputs
- Causal interpretation of markets-for-ideas mechanism relies on Bayh-Dole instrument, which has contested validity in literature
- Does not directly measure absorptive capacity loss — this connection is inferential

## Implications for the Bundesinnovationshaushalt project

1. **Structural argument for TRL 1-3 company funding:** Germany's 0% company share at TRL 1-3 in Förderkatalog is consistent with the global trend Arora et al. document — but the policy response should be to counteract it, not to accept structural absence. The market failure has both supply-side (positive externalities from basic research) and demand-side (absorptive capacity maintenance) components.
2. **Cannot assume university basic research compensates:** Even if DFG/BMBF-funded university TRL 1-3 research is strong, Arora et al. imply that firm-level basic research is not a substitute because it is the interface function, not just the output, that matters.
3. **SBIR design implication:** A German SBIR-Äquivalent specifically targeting TRL 1-3 for companies would directly address the gap Arora et al. identify. Phase II null result in Howell (2017) additionally warns against weighting grants toward late-stage (TRL 7+) work.
