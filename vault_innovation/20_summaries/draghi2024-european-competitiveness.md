## Detailed summary

The Draghi report argues that Europe faces a structural competitiveness problem rooted in weak productivity growth. Since 2000, the EU-US gap in GDP and household income has widened, driven mainly by a stronger slowdown in European productivity. The report stresses that Europe can no longer rely on population growth to support GDP: by 2040, the workforce is projected to shrink substantially. If productivity growth remains weak, Europe will struggle to finance decarbonisation, digitalisation, defence, social inclusion, and debt sustainability at the same time.

The report identifies three broad areas for action. First, Europe must close the innovation gap with the US and China, especially in advanced technologies. It argues that Europe has strong researchers and entrepreneurs but fails to translate innovation into commercialisation and scale. The report describes Europe as stuck in a more static industrial structure, with too few new companies disrupting existing sectors or creating new growth sectors. It highlights the EU's weakness in tech, foundational AI, cloud, and other future growth areas, while noting that Europe's leading R&I investors remain concentrated in mature sectors such as automotive.

Second, the report calls for a joint plan for decarbonisation and competitiveness. Decarbonisation can be a growth opportunity if policy is coordinated, but it can also raise costs and weaken industry if energy, clean-tech, grid, automotive, trade, and industrial policies are fragmented. The source notes that EU firms still face higher electricity and gas prices than US firms. It also warns that Chinese competition in clean tech and electric vehicles combines industrial policy, subsidies, rapid innovation, raw-material control, and continental scale.

Third, the report argues that security and reduced dependencies are now preconditions for sustainable growth. Europe depends on concentrated suppliers for critical raw materials and strategic technologies. The report therefore calls for EU-level statecraft: preferential trade agreements, direct investment with resource-rich countries, stockpiles in selected areas, industrial partnerships, stronger investment screening, and better coordination of foreign direct investment decisions.

A cross-cutting theme is that Europe is not acting enough as a community. The report criticizes fragmented regulation, incomplete Single Market integration, insufficient EU-level public investment in breakthrough technologies, and weak coordination in defence procurement and innovation. It argues that EU public R&I spending is comparable to the US as a share of GDP, but only a small share is spent at EU level, limiting scale and spillover capture. It also argues that regulatory burdens are especially harmful for SMEs and digital-sector firms.

The financing argument is central. The report states that the investment share in Europe must rise by around five percentage points of GDP to meet digitalisation, decarbonisation, and defence needs. It emphasizes Capital Markets Union but says private finance alone will not carry the investment burden without public support. Joint funding is presented as necessary for key European public goods such as breakthrough innovation, defence spending/procurement, and cross-border grids. The widely cited additional investment need of around EUR 800 billion per year comes from the broader Draghi package and should be cited carefully with the relevant part of the source.

For the wiki, the report is a high-level strategy source. It is useful for the macro frame around innovation gaps, EU public goods, deep-tech commercialization, capital markets, regulatory fragmentation, and strategic industrial policy. It is not a paper-level empirical evaluation of any specific subsidy program and does not provide project-level TRL labels or Foerderkatalog/CORDIS coding rules.

## Research question

What strategy should Europe pursue to restore competitiveness, raise productivity, finance large investment needs, close the innovation gap, manage decarbonisation, and reduce strategic dependencies?

## Core argument or contribution

The report's core contribution is a comprehensive EU competitiveness diagnosis and strategy. It links weak productivity to Europe's failure to scale advanced technologies, fragmented markets and regulation, underdeveloped capital markets, high energy costs, insufficient EU-level coordination, and under-provision of European public goods.

## Methodology

- Source type: strategy report and policy synthesis.
- Evidence base: macroeconomic comparisons, productivity data, sectoral examples, firm and R&I spending comparisons, investment simulations, stakeholder consultations, institutional analysis, and policy recommendations.
- Analytical structure: diagnosis of Europe's productivity gap, identification of three action areas, and cross-cutting governance/financing recommendations.
- Not a causal program evaluation or econometric study of one intervention.

## Key findings

- Europe's growth problem is primarily a productivity problem, and the EU-US productivity gap widened after the mid-1990s.
- The tech sector explains much of the EU-US productivity divergence.
- Europe is weak in emerging technologies that will drive future growth; the report notes that only a small number of the world's top tech companies are European.
- EU firms are more specialized in mature technologies, while US leading R&I investors have shifted toward tech.
- Europe has ideas and research capacity, but commercialization and scaling are blocked by regulation, fragmentation, weak capital markets, and insufficient high-growth projects.
- Decarbonisation can become a competitiveness opportunity only if paired with coherent energy, industry, clean-tech, automotive, and trade policy.
- EU energy prices remain higher than US prices, which weighs on competitiveness during the transition.
- Security dependencies in critical raw materials and strategic technologies require coordinated EU economic statecraft.
- EU-level action is needed because many relevant investments are European public goods.
- Private finance and Capital Markets Union are necessary but insufficient without public support and productivity-enhancing reform.
- The report calls for greater EU coordination and, where conditions permit, common funding for breakthrough innovation and other public goods.

## Limitations

- The report is a strategic policy synthesis, not a peer-reviewed causal evaluation.
- It combines diagnosis, modelling, consultation, and policy advocacy; individual claims should be traced to their underlying data source before formal citation.
- The report is EU-level and does not directly evaluate German project-level funding allocation.
- It does not classify projects by TRL, radicalness, or program budget category.
- It does not provide a machine-readable dataset for Foerderkatalog, CORDIS, or national innovation budgets.
- Recommendations depend on political and institutional feasibility, including EU governance and funding arrangements.

## Implications for LLM research or the project domain

For the Bundesinnovationshaushalt/TRL project, Draghi provides the macro-European rationale for asking whether public innovation budgets support frontier technologies, commercialization, scaleup finance, and European public goods. It supports treating German project funding as part of a broader EU competitiveness and industrial-policy problem. It does not prove that German Foerderkatalog funding is biased toward incremental innovation; that requires the project's own portfolio analysis and evidence from German sources.
