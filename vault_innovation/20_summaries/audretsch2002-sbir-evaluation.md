## Detailed summary

Audretsch, Link, and Scott evaluate public support for private-sector R&D through the U.S. Department of Defense's Small Business Innovation Research program. The paper starts from the policy rationale for SBIR: if social returns to R&D exceed private returns, small firms may underinvest in commercially and socially valuable research. A public/private technology partnership can be justified if it supports projects that would not otherwise be undertaken and if the resulting social benefits exceed the cost of public funds.

The source is careful about what is being evaluated. It does not evaluate all SBIR agencies, and it does not rely on a single econometric estimate. Instead, it uses a broad evaluation strategy because program data are limited and because commercialization, additionality, and social returns cannot be captured well by one measure. The paper combines survey evidence from DoD SBIR Phase II awardees with case-based evidence on whether firms would have undertaken the research without SBIR support and with estimates of social rates of return for a sample of projects.

The program context matters. SBIR began at NSF and was expanded through the Small Business Innovation Development Act of 1982. Its objectives include stimulating technological innovation, using small business to meet federal R&D needs, encouraging participation by minority and disadvantaged persons, and increasing private-sector commercialization of federally supported R&D. In DoD SBIR, Phase I awards test feasibility; Phase II awards finance the R&D work intended to produce a commercializable product or process; Phase III commercialization is not financed by DoD SBIR itself.

The empirical evidence comes partly from a National Academy of Sciences mail survey of DoD SBIR companies that had received Phase II awards since 1992. The survey was sent to 379 companies, 232 responses were returned at least partly completed, and 112 complete observations are used for the commercialization analysis. The authors examine sales realized from the DoD-funded Phase II technology and use econometric analysis to study commercialization outcomes. This survey evidence indicates that SBIR awardees do commercialize products and services based on SBIR-sponsored research.

The evaluation also uses case-study evidence. The authors rely on case-based information to assess whether the projects would have been undertaken without SBIR and to estimate lower-bound social rates of return for 44 projects. The firms in that sample reported that without SBIR support they would not have undertaken the research, or would have done so only on a limited scale. For the average project in the 44-project sample, the estimated private rate of return without SBIR support is below the firms' reported hurdle rate, while the lower-bound expected social rate of return is at least 84 percent. The authors emphasize that this social-return estimate is conservative because it excludes consumer surplus.

The paper concludes that DoD SBIR stimulated R&D and commercialization activity that would not otherwise have occurred and that the sampled projects generated substantial positive net social benefits. It also stresses that the findings are specific to DoD SBIR and that other agencies' SBIR programs require their own evaluations.

## Research question

Does the DoD SBIR program stimulate small-firm R&D and commercialization that would not otherwise occur, and do SBIR-supported projects generate social benefits large enough to justify public support?

## Core argument or contribution

The paper contributes an evaluation framework for public/private technology partnerships that combines commercialization evidence, project additionality evidence, and social-rate-of-return analysis. Its substantive contribution is evidence that DoD SBIR can overcome private underinvestment in socially valuable small-firm R&D.

## Methodology

- Program evaluation of DoD SBIR as a public/private technology partnership.
- National Academy of Sciences survey of DoD SBIR Phase II awardees.
- Econometric analysis of realized sales from SBIR-supported technologies.
- Case studies of entrepreneurial behavior and project counterfactuals.
- Case-based estimation of prospective lower-bound social rates of return for 44 SBIR projects.
- Comparison of private hurdle rates, estimated private returns without SBIR, and lower-bound social returns.

## Datasets/materials used

- DoD SBIR budget and award data.
- NAS survey of 379 DoD SBIR Phase II companies, with 232 partial or complete responses and 112 complete observations used in the sales analysis.
- Case-study sample of 44 SBIR projects used for social-rate-of-return calculations.
- Firm-reported information about commercialization, sales, project costs, private hurdle rates, and counterfactual project behavior without SBIR.

## Key findings

- DoD SBIR awardees commercialize products and services based on SBIR-sponsored research.
- The evidence supports two SBIR objectives: stimulating technological innovation and increasing commercialization of innovations derived from federal R&D.
- Case-based evidence indicates that firms would not have pursued the Phase II research, or would have pursued it only on a limited scale, without SBIR support.
- For the 44-project sample, the estimated private rate of return without SBIR support is below the firms' average private hurdle rate.
- The lower-bound expected social rate of return for the sampled projects is estimated at least 84 percent, above the public-investment benchmark discussed in the paper.
- The authors interpret the results as evidence that DoD SBIR helps overcome market failure in small-firm R&D.

## Limitations

- The conclusions are specific to DoD SBIR and should not automatically be generalized to SBIR programs at other agencies.
- The survey respondent sample is limited; the sales analysis uses 112 complete observations.
- The 44-project social-return sample is not statistically representative of all SBIR projects.
- Case studies can suffer from representativeness and interview-bias concerns.
- Counterfactual claims about what firms would have done without SBIR rely partly on firm reports and case-based evidence.
- The paper evaluates commercialization and social returns, not German policy instruments, TRL distributions, or Foerderkatalog project classifications.

For [[70_projects/bundesinnovationshaushalt-trl.md]], the paper is useful as an international benchmark for evaluating small-business R&D support and public/private technology partnerships. It should be used to frame questions about additionality, commercialization, and social returns, not as direct evidence about German Foerderkatalog projects. The project-relevant comparison is methodological: an A-tier German funding analysis should distinguish allocation, additionality, commercialization outcomes, and social value rather than only counting funded projects.
