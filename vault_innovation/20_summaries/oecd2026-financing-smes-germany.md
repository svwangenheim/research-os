## Detailed summary

This OECD Scoreboard monitors SME and entrepreneurship finance across countries using indicators on debt, equity, asset-based finance, framework conditions, and policy responses. The Germany country profile describes SMEs as the backbone of the German economy: 3.84 million SMEs with turnover up to EUR 500 million account for 99.95% of all companies. The source emphasizes that financing conditions improved slightly in 2025 as interest rates fell relative to 2024, but credit standards for German SMEs remained very high and uncertainty continued to weigh on a strong recovery in bank credit.

The Germany profile shows that bank finance remains important but is not the only financing source. At the start of 2025, only one in five companies reported participating in bank-loan negotiations. The report interprets weak borrowing partly as a demand-side issue: many SMEs remain reluctant to borrow, and firms with weaker finances may face denial because of low credit standing. In 2024, German SMEs borrowed EUR 89 billion from savings banks and banks to finance investments. The profile also reports that the average new bank loan for SME investment in 2023 was EUR 152,000, while microloans dominated numerically: 69% of investment loans were at most EUR 50,000 and 79% were at most EUR 100,000.

The financing mix is strongly internal-finance and bank-loan based. In 2023, internal financing from profits, depreciation, amortization, and provisions accounted for around 51% of SME investment financing. Bank and savings-bank loans covered around 32%. Public funding covered 13%. Other sources, including mezzanine, equity finance, loans from credit funds, crowdfunding, and related alternatives, accounted for only about 4% of the total investment-financing volume. The source therefore supports a cautious version of the equity-gap argument: alternative and equity-based finance exist, but they remain secondary for the broad German SME sector.

The broader Scoreboard chapter on venture capital adds the innovation-finance dimension. Governments increasingly use public development banks and public VC vehicles to participate in venture markets, often indirectly through fund-of-funds structures. Germany is presented as a case where KfW Capital invests indirectly in private VC funds, while the High-Tech Gruenderfonds invests directly in start-ups and the DeepTech & Climate Fund co-invests directly in deep-tech companies. The report describes a policy shift toward later-stage and growth-stage finance: the Future Fund, GFF-EIF Growth Facility, ERP/Future Fund Growth Facility, and Venture Tech Growth Financing Programme are all presented as instruments aimed at larger rounds, growth-stage companies, bridge loans, late-stage firms, or post-IPO debt loans.

The chapter also discusses Germany's WIN Initiative. Launched in 2024, WIN includes measures to strengthen growth and innovation capital. One measure is an amendment to the Investment Ordinance raising the quota for risk-capital investments from 35% to 40%, making it easier for small insurers and pension funds to invest in venture capital; the change came into force in February 2025. The report also notes planned or ongoing work to mobilize public institutional investors, build investor know-how through KfW Capital's VC Academy and potential-limited-partner training, and use formats such as the Family Office Initiative and Foundation Round Table to attract additional investor groups.

For the vault, this source should be used as an SME-finance and venture-capital policy reference, not as evidence that a specific German innovation grant programme succeeds or fails. It helps connect public R&D subsidies to the broader financing stack: internal funds, bank debt, public support, guarantees, venture capital, growth capital, and institutional-investor mobilization.

## Research question

What do current OECD SME-finance indicators and policy developments show about German SME access to finance, and how are governments, including Germany's, trying to improve venture-capital and growth-finance conditions for innovative SMEs and start-ups?

## Core argument or contribution

The source contributes comparative SME-finance monitoring and a Germany-specific financing snapshot. For Germany, the main message is that SMEs remain highly reliant on internal financing and bank loans, while public funding is a meaningful complement and alternative/equity sources remain relatively small for broad SME investment finance. For innovative and high-growth firms, the broader Scoreboard highlights policy efforts to strengthen VC markets, mobilize institutional investors, and support growth-stage finance through public development banks and targeted initiatives.

## Methodology

The source is an OECD Scoreboard, not a causal evaluation. It compiles official country data and policy inputs from participating countries, financial institutions, statistical offices, public authorities, and external sources. The report uses a standard set of indicators covering SME loans, new lending, outstanding loans, guarantees, direct public loans, interest rates, non-performing loans, collateral, venture and growth capital, leasing, factoring, payment delays, and bankruptcies. It also includes national policy snapshots and a thematic chapter on venture capital policy.

## Key findings

- German SMEs remain central to the economy: the profile reports 3.84 million SMEs with turnover up to EUR 500 million, equal to 99.95% of all companies.
- German SME credit conditions improved slightly in 2025 because interest rates fell relative to 2024, but credit standards remained very high and uncertainty limited a strong recovery.
- Only one in five German companies reported participating in bank-loan negotiations at the start of 2025, pointing to both weak loan demand and difficult access for firms with weaker credit standing.
- In 2024, German SMEs borrowed EUR 89 billion from savings banks and banks to finance investments.
- In 2023, German SME investment finance was mostly internal finance (around 51%) and bank/savings-bank loans (around 32%), with public funding at 13% and other sources at only 4%.
- Microloans dominate the number of investment loans: 69% of investment loans in 2023 were no more than EUR 50,000 and 79% were no more than EUR 100,000.
- The Scoreboard's VC chapter presents Germany as using multiple public VC channels: indirect fund investment via KfW Capital, direct early-stage investment through High-Tech Gruenderfonds, direct deep-tech co-investment through the DeepTech & Climate Fund, and growth-stage instruments under the Future Fund.
- German VC policy has increasingly targeted later-stage and growth finance, including larger financing rounds and growth-stage support.
- The WIN Initiative is presented as a policy effort to mobilize more growth and innovation capital, including regulatory changes for insurers and pension funds, investor education, and outreach to family offices and foundations.

## Limitations

- The Germany profile is descriptive and indicator-based; it does not estimate causal effects of financing instruments.
- Cross-country comparisons in the Scoreboard are limited by differences in SME definitions, loan definitions, bankruptcy rules, reporting practices, and data availability.
- The profile covers broad SME finance, not only innovation-active SMEs or LLM/technology firms.
- The report does not directly evaluate whether Foerderkatalog grants lead to higher growth, patents, private finance, or technology readiness.
- Some 2025 statements are policy/market updates rather than complete annual statistical series.

## Implications for LLM research or the project domain

For the Bundesinnovationshaushalt/TRL project, this source implies that public R&D grants should be interpreted as one layer in a broader financing stack. A project-level funding corpus can identify public support, but it cannot by itself show whether firms have access to internal finance, bank debt, venture capital, growth capital, guarantees, or institutional-investor-backed scale-up finance.

For LLM-assisted classification, the source suggests useful coding dimensions: SME financing instrument, public grant/subsidy, bank-loan orientation, guarantee, VC/growth-capital relevance, start-up/scale-up stage, institutional-investor mobilization, public development bank role, and whether a measure targets broad SME investment or innovation-intensive growth.
