## Detailed summary

Audretsch presents SBIR as a U.S. policy response to a changed innovation economy. The article begins with the competitiveness concerns of the 1970s and early 1980s and argues that the later U.S. recovery was unexpectedly driven in large part by small firms rather than by the large corporations that had dominated older industrial-policy thinking. The conceptual background is a shift in comparative advantage: high-wage economies can no longer rely primarily on traditional production inputs and must instead compete through knowledge-based activity and innovation.

The article explains why small firms matter in a knowledge economy. Traditional innovation theory often starts from the firm and treats R&D as an input into a knowledge production function. Yet formal R&D is concentrated in large firms, while empirical work shows that small firms account for a disproportionate share of new product innovations relative to their R&D expenditure. Audretsch uses this to motivate a policy shift from large-firm-centered competitiveness policy toward instruments that recognize entrepreneurship and small-firm innovation.

SBIR is the institutional example. Congress created SBIR as a response to the loss of U.S. competitiveness in global markets and mandated major federal R&D agencies to allocate part of their research budgets to innovative small firms. The article describes SBIR's three-phase structure. Phase I tests scientific and technical merit and feasibility; in the source's description it lasts six months and originally could not exceed $70,000. Phase II develops the technological idea and emphasizes commercialization; it lasts up to 24 months and generally did not exceed $600,000, with roughly 40 percent of Phase I awards continuing into Phase II. The 1992 legislation increased Phase I funding to $100,000 and Phase II funding to $750,000. Phase III involves private-sector funding and commercial market application rather than an SBIR award from the agency.

The article also situates SBIR within the broader ecosystem of public SME finance. It presents SBIR as a major public small-business finance mechanism, with an emphasis on early-stage finance that private venture capital often ignores. It lists Apple, Chiron, Compaq, and Intel as examples of innovative American companies that received early-stage finance from SBIR. It also discusses the role of SBIR in biomedical and defense-related small-firm innovation, including NIH SBIR grants and DoD biotechnology awards.

For evaluation, Audretsch draws on the National Academy's STEP survey and case-study evidence from prior SBIR assessments. He reports that SBIR benefits extend beyond individual recipient firms, that social returns exceed private returns, that there was no evidence of negative rates of return in the cited evaluation evidence, and that SBIR contributed to the U.S. biotechnology industry. The article emphasizes mechanisms such as higher survival and growth for recipients compared with non-recipients, career shifts by scientists toward commercialization, startup creation by scientists who otherwise lacked alternative funding, and demonstration effects on other researchers.

## Research question

Why did SBIR become an important U.S. innovation-policy instrument, and how does it link small-firm innovation, knowledge-based comparative advantage, and national competitiveness?

## Core argument or contribution

The article's contribution is an institutional and policy-economics account of SBIR. It argues that small firms became central to U.S. innovation and competitiveness, and that SBIR is a policy instrument designed to finance and commercialize high-technology small-firm innovation.

## Methodology

- Conceptual policy analysis of globalization, knowledge-based comparative advantage, and entrepreneurship.
- Program description of SBIR history, mission, financing, and phase structure.
- Review and synthesis of prior empirical/evaluation evidence, including National Academy STEP survey and case-study work.
- Comparative-policy framing between traditional large-firm competitiveness policy and explicit small-firm innovation support.

## Datasets/materials used

- JSTOR/Small Business Economics article text.
- Prior SBIR evaluation evidence from Wessner (2001) and related studies.
- National Academy STEP survey evidence referenced in the article.
- Case-study evidence from more than fifty SBIR-assisted firms referenced in the source.
- Program examples from NIH and DoD SBIR activity.

## Key findings

- SBIR was created as a policy response to competitiveness concerns and the growing innovation role of small firms.
- The program institutionalizes a federal channel for financing innovative small businesses.
- SBIR uses a staged structure: feasibility assessment, technology development/commercialization, and later private-sector commercialization.
- The article presents SBIR as emphasizing early-stage finance, a segment often neglected by private venture capital.
- Cited evaluation evidence suggests SBIR helped create and grow high-technology small firms, induced scientists toward commercialization, and generated demonstration effects.
- The article concludes that SBIR generally accomplished its mission by contributing to high-technology small-firm creation and U.S. competitiveness.

## Limitations

- The article is a short policy and evaluation synthesis, not a new microeconometric causal study.
- Some impact claims rely on cited evaluation literature rather than original analysis in this article.
- The source is about the United States and should not be generalized directly to German funding instruments.
- It does not analyze TRL distributions, German Foerderkatalog data, ZIM, or BMBF project texts.
- It should be paired with dedicated SBIR evaluation papers for detailed additionality, commercialization, and social-return estimates.
