## Detailed summary

This report studies the drivers and barriers of innovation activity among German Mittelstand firms, defined for the study as companies with annual turnover up to EUR 500 million. Its central motivation is the long-running decline in innovation participation among smaller German firms. The report asks why innovation activity differs across firms, which barriers prevent or reduce innovation activity, and which policy conclusions follow for strengthening SME innovation.

The empirical basis is the German Innovation Survey/Mannheim Innovation Panel (MIP). The authors use around twenty years of data to analyze determinants of innovation activity, the spread and importance of innovation barriers, and types of Mittelstand firms by innovation behavior. They estimate random-effects panel regressions for 2004-2020 and separate analyses for 2004-2012 and 2013-2020 where relevant. The study measures innovation activity through several outcomes, including innovation expenditure, investment expenditure, R&D and non-R&D innovation activities, product and process innovations, and innovation success measures.

The first major finding is structural: innovation activity tends to rise with firm size and decline with firm age. Larger Mittelstand firms are more likely to innovate and spend more on innovation. Older firms tend to show lower innovation and investment activity. Because the average firm age in the MIP rose from 26.6 years in 2004 to 32.5 years in 2020, the report argues that the aging of the firm population contributes negatively to aggregate innovation participation. The authors estimate that this aging effect explains around one percentage point of the decline in the innovator rate.

The second major finding is that human capital is central. The report highlights qualified workers and skills as especially important for R&D activity and product innovation. At the same time, skill shortages are identified as the currently largest innovation barrier in the Mittelstand. The policy implication is broader than R&D subsidies: education, training, continuing education, and skilled migration are part of the innovation-policy agenda because they determine whether firms can actually carry out innovation.

The third major finding concerns finance. Financial resources affect innovation activity materially. A favorable credit rating, interpreted as an indicator of above-average profitability, is associated with greater innovation activity and higher innovation expenditure. Lack of financial resources remains a relevant barrier. The report says financing barriers rose strongly after the 2008/09 financial crisis and then remained at a stable level. Public financial support for R&D and innovation is therefore still relevant, but finance is not the only or always the dominant barrier.

The fourth major finding is about digitalization. The report treats digitalization as one of the most important contemporary drivers of innovation. Firms that use digital possibilities show higher innovation activity. The authors argue that digitalization support should focus on more demanding applications, such as the use of digital elements in business models, because these have stronger innovation effects than basic digitization.

The fifth major finding is that laws and regulation have become more important as perceived innovation barriers. The report specifically notes data-protection law as one relevant area and suggests a reporting/contact point where firms could name concrete legal and regulatory barriers and propose solutions. The point is not that regulation should be removed wholesale, but that specific rules may create innovation barriers that need diagnosis and targeted adjustment.

The study also develops a typology of six innovation types among Mittelstand firms: firms with continuous R&D; firms with occasional R&D; product innovators without R&D; pure process innovators; firms without R&D/innovations but with innovation orientation; and firms without innovation orientation. Continuous R&D firms account for the largest share of Mittelstand innovation expenditure. Non-R&D firms spend much less, but still contribute to innovation outcomes such as sales from new products and cost reductions through new processes. With the exception of continuous R&D firms, type membership is relatively unstable, which the authors interpret as an opportunity for policy to move firms toward more ambitious or more regular innovation activity.

For the vault, this report is a key source on the German innovation paradox at the SME margin. It explains why innovation participation can decline even when aggregate innovation or R&D resources remain significant: firm size, firm aging, skills, finance, digital capability, regulation, and sector/market structure all shape whether SMEs innovate.

## Research question

Which firm characteristics, resources, competencies, markets, and barriers explain innovation activity in the German Mittelstand, and what policy measures could strengthen innovation participation among SMEs?

## Core argument or contribution

The report's core contribution is an empirical decomposition of SME innovation participation into structural drivers and barriers. It shows that declining innovation participation is not reducible to a single funding gap. Firm size, firm age, human capital, financial resources, digitalization, sectoral structure, exports, regulation, and different innovation types all matter. The policy implication is that innovation policy must combine financial support with growth policy, start-up and succession policy, skills policy, digital capability building, and barrier-specific regulatory diagnosis.

## Methodology

- Analysis of German Innovation Survey/Mannheim Innovation Panel data.
- Random-effects panel regressions for 2004-2020.
- Separate period comparisons for 2004-2012 and 2013-2020 where relevant.
- Regression analysis of innovation expenditures, innovation activities, product/process innovations, and innovation success.
- Analysis of innovation barriers reported by firms.
- Typology of Mittelstand firms by innovation activity and strategy.
- Policy synthesis from empirical results.

## Key findings

- Innovation activity rises with firm size and tends to decline with firm age.
- The average age of firms in the MIP increased from 26.6 years in 2004 to 32.5 years in 2020; the report estimates that this aging effect accounts for roughly one percentage point of the decline in the innovator rate.
- Human capital is especially important for R&D activity and product innovation.
- Skill shortages are the currently largest innovation barrier in the Mittelstand and require education, training, continuing education, and skilled-migration responses.
- Financial resources matter: favorable credit ratings and profitability are associated with stronger innovation activity and expenditure.
- Financing barriers increased after the 2008/09 financial crisis and then remained at a stable level, so public innovation finance remains relevant but is not the only binding constraint.
- Digitalization is a major innovation driver, especially when firms use digital elements in business models and more demanding applications.
- Laws and regulations have recently become more important as perceived innovation barriers, with data-protection law noted as one relevant area.
- The six innovation-type groups show that continuous R&D firms dominate innovation expenditure, but non-R&D innovators still contribute meaningfully to innovation results.
- Except for continuous R&D firms, innovation-type membership is relatively unstable, leaving room for policy to shift firms toward more ambitious innovation activity.

## Limitations

- The study is observational and based on firm survey/panel data; it should not be read as a causal evaluation of a specific funding programme.
- The MIP captures measured innovation behavior and reported barriers; both may be affected by survey response and measurement choices.
- The analysis covers Mittelstand firms, not all German firms and not only start-ups or high-tech firms.
- The report does not classify projects by technology readiness level and does not analyze the Foerderkatalog.
- Policy conclusions are broad and require programme-specific design before implementation.

## Implications for LLM research or the project domain

For [[70_projects/bundesinnovationshaushalt-trl.md]], this source argues against treating funding allocation alone as the full innovation-policy question. A project-level funding analysis should ask whether portfolios reach smaller firms, younger firms, firms without continuous R&D, firms with digital business-model needs, and firms facing skill or regulatory constraints. If the funded population is dominated by older, larger, already R&D-active firms, that would be consistent with a "deepening rather than broadening" pattern.

The report supports adding firm age, firm size, prior R&D status, human-capital/skill constraints, digitalization relevance, and financing-barrier indicators to future analyses where those variables are available. It does not itself prove that any specific Foerderkatalog allocation is defensive or incumbent-protecting; those are project hypotheses requiring separate data.
