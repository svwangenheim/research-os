# Germany Innovation Policy Evidence Map

## Scope

This synthesis is the main retrieval hub for the Germany innovation-policy part of the wiki. It connects paper summaries, concepts, methods, datasets, and the active Bundesinnovationshaushalt project so that `wiki-pull` can retrieve relevant knowledge from a project query without relying on isolated paper notes.

## What we know so far

The current evidence base points to a multi-mechanism diagnosis of Germany's innovation problem:

1. Germany is strong on inputs and established industrial capabilities, but does not rank with Europe's Innovation Leaders.
2. Aggregate R&D and innovation expenditure can hide allocation problems.
3. Innovation participation among SMEs is weakening, while expenditure is becoming more concentrated.
4. Commercialization and technology transfer are underdeveloped relative to research strength.
5. Equity and growth finance constrain start-ups, spin-outs, and scale-ups.
6. Digital and AI adoption in production depends on complementary capabilities, especially data, digital systems, and workforce training.
7. Strategic industrial policy needs to solve coordination failures, not just lower costs for incumbents.

This is the same "Viel Wissen, wenig Wachstum" diagnosis used in the project: Germany is strong upstream in research and institutions, but weaker downstream in translation into firms, markets, and productivity growth.

The evidence therefore supports an "allocation and translation" interpretation of the German innovation paradox. The question is not simply whether Germany spends enough, but whether funding reaches the right firms, stages, technologies, and commercialization bottlenecks.

## Cross-paper findings

### Mitteltechnologie-Falle: Germany as extreme case of EU-wide mid-tech lock-in (added 2026-05-05)

[[20_summaries/dietrich_et_al_2024_europes_middle_technology_trap.md]] and [[20_summaries/fuest_et_al_2024_how_to_escape_the_middle_technology_trap.md]] establish that Germany is not just an underperformer within the EU — it is the most extreme case of mid-tech lock-in within an already mid-tech-heavy EU. Germany's private R&D is 57% mid-tech (dominated by automotive); the EU average is ~50%; the US is 85% high-tech. The top-3 German R&D spenders in 2022 are identical to 2003 (VW, Mercedes, Bosch). This path dependence is consistent with Acemoglu (2023) and directly connects to the project's Förderkatalog analysis: if public funding reinforces this sectoral lock-in rather than counteracting it, it amplifies the structural problem.

Fuest et al. (2024) adds a key decomposition: 60% of the US-EU R&D intensity gap is explained by sectoral composition (the US simply has more high-tech industry); only 40% reflects within-sector intensity differences — and this within-sector gap emerged only after 2013. This means the structural problem predates any recent productivity divergence and is not primarily a firm-level competitiveness issue.

### Benchmarking: Germany is strong but not leading

[[20_summaries/ec2025-eis-scoreboard.md]] establishes that Germany is in the Strong Innovator group, while Sweden, Denmark, the Netherlands, and Finland are Innovation Leaders. This supports the project framing: Germany has serious innovation capacity but underperforms relative to the top European benchmark group.

### Allocation: spending can be trapped in low-growth uses

[[20_summaries/akcigit2024-innovation-paradox.md]] and [[90_synthesis/innovation-paradox-incumbent-lock-in.md]] show why aggregate R&D spending is not enough. If R&D flows to incumbents that use it defensively, productivity and disruptive innovation may remain weak.

### SMEs: broad participation is weakening

[[20_summaries/kfw2024-sme-innovation.md]] shows that the SME innovator share fell to 39 percent and that innovation expenditure is concentrated in the largest 2 percent of SMEs. This is not identical to incumbent capture, but it is consistent with a narrowing innovation base.

### Commercialization: transfer is an institutional bottleneck

[[20_summaries/biodeutschland2024-tech-transfer.md]] provides a concrete mechanism for the [[30_concepts/commercialization-gap.md]]: weak entrepreneurial culture, underfunded technology-transfer offices, insufficient validation funding, and weak growth-finance conditions for spin-outs.

### Strategic industry transformation: coordination failure matters

[[20_summaries/diw2025-strategic-industrial-policy.md]] argues that energy costs and generic relief are insufficient. Decarbonization, batteries, hydrogen, microelectronics, and infrastructure require coordinated investment across value chains.

### Governance and evaluation: missions need data and learning

[[20_summaries/efi2023-gutachten.md]] and [[20_summaries/efi2024-gutachten.md]] connect the evidence map to [[30_concepts/mission-oriented-innovation-policy.md]], [[30_concepts/data-access-for-innovation-policy.md]], and [[40_methods/causal-evaluation-innovation-policy.md]]. The key addition is that a funding portfolio can only become strategic if it is governed across ministries, linked to mission roadmaps, supported by usable data, and evaluated with designs that can distinguish causal effects from selection.

### AI and digital production: complementary capabilities condition diffusion

[[20_summaries/heimberger2024-ai-production.md]] shows that AI adoption in manufacturing is uneven and depends on digital production systems, usable data, firm size, and workforce training.

## Main points of agreement

- Germany's innovation problem is not a simple lack of research effort.
- The policy problem is allocation, translation, coordination, and diffusion.
- Funding design should distinguish incumbents from entrants, R&D from non-R&D innovation, and early research from commercialization.
- Project-level analysis needs to connect TRL, recipient type, technology field, and expected commercialization path.

## Main disagreements

- Some sources emphasize broad SME innovation participation, while others emphasize radical entrants and high-growth scale-ups.
- Some sources support strategic industrial policy, while the innovation-paradox literature warns that strategic policy can be captured by incumbents.
- Some bottlenecks are supply-side capabilities; others are finance, regulation, procurement, or market coordination.

## Open questions

- Does the Foerderkatalog portfolio fund the capabilities that the sources identify as missing?
- Does public funding reach young firms, spin-outs, and broad non-R&D innovators, or mainly established formal R&D performers?
- Are AI, biotech, hydrogen, battery, and microelectronics projects funded at the right TRL stages?
- Which German funding instruments address validation and commercialization rather than only research?
- How much of the German gap is explained by weak scale-up finance versus weak project selection?

## Implications for my work

The Bundesinnovationshaushalt project should treat the wiki evidence as a set of testable hypotheses:

- TRL hypothesis: funding may be concentrated in applied development rather than exploratory or commercialization-critical stages.
- Recipient hypothesis: funding may favor established applicants and prior recipients.
- Technology hypothesis: projects may support incumbent trajectories rather than strategic new systems.
- Commercialization hypothesis: projects may stop before validation, demonstration, and scale-up.
- Capability hypothesis: projects may name AI or green technologies without building the complementary data, skills, or infrastructure needed for adoption.
