# International Innovation Policy Benchmarks

## Scope

This synthesis documents policy instruments from GII top-10 countries (Switzerland, Sweden, USA, Korea, UK, Finland, Netherlands, Denmark, China) that address three structural weaknesses in the German innovation system. It serves as the retrieval bridge for questions about international policy comparisons, SME/entrant targeting, incumbent capture, technology prioritization, and knowledge transfer infrastructure.

GII 2025 ranking reference: CHE (1), SWE (2), USA (3), KOR (4), SGP (5), UK (6), FIN (7), NLD (8), DNK (9), CHN (10). Germany: rank 11.

---

## Problem 1: Structural Disadvantage of Young and Small Firms

### Theoretical Foundation

[[20_summaries/acemoglu2006-distance-to-frontier.md]]: Near-frontier economies need entrant selection, not incumbent protection. [[20_summaries/akcigit2018-heterogeneous-innovations.md]]: Entrants contribute 25.7% of aggregate innovation-driven growth (vs. 74.3% for incumbents combined); smaller firms are relatively more associated with major external innovations conditional on success. No "3×" claim — that figure does not appear in the paper. [[20_summaries/akcigit2024-innovation-paradox.md]]: R&D misallocated toward incumbent defense reduces aggregate innovation quality.

### German Diagnosis

[[20_summaries/aschhoff2010-who-gets-the-money.md]] (ZEW): Prior scheme experience is the dominant predictor of new award receipt — stronger than firm merits. Large firms receive more. ZIM 2024 Evaluation (Stehnken et al.): 60% of ZIM recipients are repeat beneficiaries. ZIM 2025 Richtlinie reform introduces a 3-year exclusion period after ZIM receipt — implicit acknowledgment of state-dependence bias.

KfW Innovation Report 2024: German SME innovator share fell to 39% (2021–2023), down 3 percentage points from pre-COVID baseline. Top 2% of SMEs account for 56% of total SME innovation expenditure. Only 3% of SMEs conduct continuous in-house R&D.

### Country Instruments

#### USA — SBIR/STTR (Gold Standard)

**Legal basis:** Small Business Act (15 U.S.C. § 638). Mandatory statutory set-aside: 11 federal agencies with external R&D budgets >$100M must obligate ≥3.2% to SBIR awards. Not discretionary. FY2022: $2.24B+ allocated.

**Structure:** Phase I = TRL 1–4, up to $200–275k, 6–12 months (feasibility). Phase II = TRL 4–7, up to $1–1.84M, 2 years (development). Phase III = TRL 7–9, funded entirely from private capital or non-SBIR federal procurement. Government exits after Phase II — no permanent subsidy dependence.

**Causal evidence:** Howell (2017), *American Economic Review* 107(4): 1136–64. RDD on DOE SBIR, 7,436 firms, $884M awards, 1983–2013. Phase I award approximately doubles VC follow-on probability (10% → 19%); +1.5 citation-weighted patents. Effects strongest for youngest, most financially constrained firms and in immature technologies. "Open topic" competitions (Air Force) show large positive causal effects; narrow-topic awards show zero effect — openness matters.

**Anti-capture mechanism:** Statutory size cap (≤500 employees) locks out large incumbents at the eligibility level. Phase I small awards target the pre-VC stage. Phase III private funding forces market validation without continuing subsidy.

Sources: https://www.aeaweb.org/articles/pdf/doi/10.1257/aer.20150808 | https://www.nber.org/reporter/2022number4/mechanisms-and-impacts-innovation-policy | https://www.sbir.gov/sites/default/files/SBA_FY22_SBIR_STTR_Annual_Report.pdf

#### Korea — TIPS (Technology Incubator Program for Startup)

**Structure (since 2013):** Certified accelerators/VCs invest KRW 100M (~$77k) private capital in a startup first, then nominate it for TIPS government matching of up to KRW 900M (~$700k) in R&D funds. Total support: ~KRW 1B (~$770k) per startup over 2–3 years. 10% royalty repayment only upon success.

**Key structural innovation:** Private first-selection outsources the anti-incumbent selection problem to incentivized private actors. VCs invest own capital before government funds flow — ensuring market-validated early-stage firms receive support, not bureaucratically favored applicants.

**TRL targeting:** TRL 1–5 (pre-revenue to early revenue; explicitly pre-Series A gap).

**Causal evidence:** World Bank RDD: +13.2% employment, positive VC follow-on investment. Korean PSM-DiD (KCI 2023): significant increases in sales, assets, and patents; effects persist and compound 1–9 years post-support.

Sources: https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/73dbd889-9fe7-426d-91fd-2e08d187cdef/content | https://www.jointips.or.kr/support_en.php

#### Finland — Business Finland / NIY

**Young Innovative Company (NIY):** Explicitly restricted to firms under ~4 years old with fewer than 250 employees. Three-phase structure. Up to 100% public funding at earliest stages.

**Innovation Vouchers:** 100% funded preliminary study with research partner. 56% of recipients are companies under 10 years old; 75% increased technological maturity by ~2 TRL levels.

**Causal evidence:** Business Finland Co-Innovation DiD study: significant positive effects on turnover, job creation, and export performance. NIY Impact Study 2024: associated with stronger foundational growth than comparable non-NIY firms.

Sources: https://www.businessfinland.fi/48dce8/globalassets/julkaisut/business-finland/vaikuttavuus/impact_study_of_economic_growth-1_2024.pdf

#### Switzerland — Innosuisse Start-up Innovation Projects

**Structure:** Available only to firms "prior to first market entry." Up to 70% government co-financing. ~10% acceptance rate. BRIDGE Proof of Concept funds pre-firm stage (from academic discovery to startup formation).

**Causal evidence:** ETH/KOF DiD (December 2024): +21% firm sales, +18% employment over 5 years vs. comparable non-funded firms. Effects increase over time.

Sources: https://www.innosuisse.admin.ch/en/start-up-innovation-projects | https://www.aramis.admin.ch/Texte/?ProjectID=56145

#### UK — SBRI / Contracts for Innovation

**Design:** Demand-led, challenge-based. Phase 1 = up to £100k feasibility. Phase 2 = up to £1M development. 100% public funding; government as "launching customer" in Phase 3.

**Key structural weakness vs. US SBIR:** No statutory set-aside. Fully discretionary — departments choose whether to run SBRI competitions. Connell Review (2017) identified limited departmental uptake as the primary structural weakness. Benefits concentrated in young, small firms in practice. BCR 1.53–4.07 (UKRI/Steer evaluation 2022).

Sources: https://www.ukri.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/UKRI-130522-AnEvaluationoftheSBRIJanuary2022-WEB-FINAL.pdf

#### Comparative Key Finding

**Only the USA has a statutory mandatory set-aside.** All other GII top-10 countries rely on discretionary budget allocations. This is the most structurally significant design difference: the US 3.2% quota removes political discretion and creates a guaranteed market for early-stage SME innovation. Korea's TIPS compensates for the absence of a quota by outsourcing first-stage selection to incentivized private capital — the most innovative structural design after SBIR.

## Problem 2: Wrong Technology Focus / Incumbent Capture in High-Tech

### Evidence of Structural Underfunding of Key Technologies

**Fuest/Dietrich et al. (2024):** EU BERD: 45% mid-tech vs. US 85% high-tech. Germany most extreme: 57% mid-tech (automotive dominant); high-tech share only 36%. Same companies have dominated EU innovation activity for >20 years. Mid-tech R&D intensity structurally capped at ~3% revenue globally (maturity effect); US high-tech R&D intensity rose from 8% to 13% (2002–2022). [[20_summaries/fuest_et_al_2024_how_to_escape_the_middle_technology_trap.md]]

**EFI 2025 Gutachten:** Germany shows "significant weaknesses" in AI, biotech, materials science, production technologies; dynamics in quantum technologies "significantly weaker" than China and South Korea. https://www.e-fi.de/fileadmin/Assets/Gutachten/2025/EFI_Gutachten_2025_30125.pdf

**ifo Institut (September 2025):** "Private R&D spending is currently concentrated in traditional sectors such as the automotive industry or mechanical engineering, while future fields remain underfunded." https://www.ifo.de/en/press-release/2025-09-15/germany-needs-changes-to-its-innovation-policy

### Country Instruments

#### Korea — KIAT/KEIT (Legally Mandated Technology Priority Lists)

**Legal architecture:** Industrial Technology Innovation Promotion Act + Special Measures Act for "Strategic Technologies." Ministry of Trade, Industry and Resources (MOTIR) has explicit statutory authority to designate technology development priority areas and guarantee R&D budget allocation.

**KEIT 2026 budget: 4.18 trillion KRW (~$2.79B USD)** across 163 sub-programmes with published sector allocation: materials/components (41.1%), transport vehicles (13.4%), manufacturing base tech (14.1%), bio-health (6.7%), smart electronics/AI semiconductors (9.0%), carbon-neutral industry tech (6.2%). SME-related R&D: 50.9% of total KEIT budget.

**KIAT:** TRL 5–9, technology commercialization. Annual "Top 10 Promising Industries" list (2024: next-gen batteries, SMRs, Software-Defined Mobility, digital therapeutics, cybersecurity, generative AI, intelligent semiconductors, micro displays, intelligent service robots).

**Anti-capture mechanism — tiered subsidy rates:**
- Large firms: government ≤33%, private cash co-financing ≥60%
- Mid-sized firms: government ≤50%, private cash ≥50%
- SMEs: government ≤67%, private cash ≥40%

**Technology priority-setting process:** Megatrend analysis → patent concentration analysis → industry support factor mapping → expert deliberation → annual "Top 10" confirmation.

Sources: https://www.keit.re.kr/menu.es?mid=a20202000000 | https://elaw.klri.re.kr/eng_mobile/viewer.do?hseq=18579 | https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2023/07/oecd-reviews-of-innovation-policy-korea-2023_6517d469/bdcf9685-en.pdf

#### USA — DARPA / ARPA-E (Anti-Incumbent by Structural Design)

**DARPA anti-capture principles:**
1. PM autonomy: Program Managers from outside government on fixed 3–5 year terms; unilateral authority over programme design and project selection.
2. Flat organisation: ~120 technical staff, one management layer.
3. OTA contracting: Startups participate without FAR compliance overhead.
4. Time-bounded programmes: no permanent incumbent subsidies.

**ARPA-E:** Explicit criterion — funds technologies "not likely to be pursued by industry, Federal agencies, or DOE applied R&D programmes." TRL 2–4 only. Cumulative (FY2023): $3.76B invested → $12.1B follow-on private investment (3:1 leverage), 154 companies formed, 1,073+ patents. Capital gains taxes from exits exceed total ARPA-E expenditure.

Sources: https://arpa-e.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2025-09/ARPA-E%20FY%202023%20Annual%20Report.pdf | https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK36337/

#### Sweden — Vinnova Strategic Innovation Programs (SIPs)

**17 programmes** for strategic sectors. Actor-driven governance (stakeholders developed agendas). Mandatory three-year evaluation cycle conditioning continued funding.

**Meta-evaluation 2023:** "Effective for building national capacity and competitiveness." First 5 completed programmes: SEK 5.4B total, 1,400+ innovation projects, ~1:1 public-private leverage.

**2024 Government technology prioritisation mandate:** Six strategic areas: AI/autonomous systems, advanced digital technology, quantum technology, energy technology, materials/production technology, biotechnology. >SEK 500M/year for "Clusters of Excellence."

**Anti-capture limitation:** Actor-driven model reduces but does not eliminate incumbent governance capture within programme boards.

Sources: https://www.vinnova.se/en/m/strategic-innovation-programmes/ | https://externwebb-prod.vinnova.se/en/publications/strategic-technology-areas-for-sweden/

#### Warning: Netherlands Top Sector Policy — Documented Failure Case

The Dutch Top Sector model was officially discontinued in 2024. AWTI advisory body (2023) stated explicitly: "Established parties sit around the table...there is little scope for new players...the result is incremental improvements and optimisation of the status quo." Textbook example of how sectoral coordination policy produces incumbent lock-in without governance safeguards.

Sources: https://www.awti.nl/documenten/adviezen/2023/12/14/advisory-report-shaping-the-future---from-optimisation-to-transformation

## Problem 3: Knowledge Transfer Gap and Declining SME Innovation Participation

### Problem Scale

**KfW Innovation Report 2024:** German SME innovator share: 39% (2021–2023), −3 pp from pre-COVID baseline. Top 2% of SMEs = 56% of all SME innovation expenditure. Only 3% of SMEs conduct continuous in-house R&D. https://www.kfw.de/PDF/Download-Center/Konzernthemen/Research/PDF-Dokumente-Innovationsbericht/KfW-Innovationsbericht-EN/KfW-SME-Innovation-Report-2024_EN.pdf

### Approach A: Mandatory SME-University Cooperation as Eligibility Condition

#### Switzerland — Innosuisse (Strongest Causal Evidence)

**Mechanism:** Hard eligibility condition — an application is valid only if both an implementation partner (SME/startup) and a research partner (accredited Swiss university) are present. Solo applications rejected at formal check. No thematic restrictions. Innovation Cheque entry point: CHF 15,000, 100% funded, for firms without prior research experience.

**Financial structure:** Implementation partner bears 40–60% of total costs; Innosuisse covers 40–60% of research partner costs. Scale: CHF 319–335M/year.

**Causal evidence — Beck, Lopes-Bento & Schenker-Wicki (2016), *Research Policy* 45(4): 869–883:**
- Significant positive effect on **radical innovation** (market novelties)
- **No significant effect on incremental innovation**
- The mandatory cooperation acts as a quality/additionality filter ensuring public money funds genuinely science-based projects. The collaboration structure forces science-based framing, preventing firms from substituting public for private money on routine projects.

**KOF ETH KTT Monitoring 2024:** Private-sector university R&D funding approximately doubled; Switzerland leads Europe in spinout value per capita.

Sources: https://www.innosuisse.admin.ch/en/innovation-project-with-implementation-partner | https://ub-madoc.bib.uni-mannheim.de/37424/1/dp14106.pdf | https://kof.ethz.ch/en/news-and-events/kof-bulletin/kof-bulletin/2024/10/knowledge-and-technology-transfer-the-key-to-explaining-switzerlands-innovative-prowess.html

#### Finland — Business Finland Bridging Call (Novel Entry-Barrier Design)

**Standard Co-Innovation:** Mandatory consortium of ≥2 companies + research organization. Research org: 80% cost coverage; SMEs: 35–50%.

**Innovation Bridge Call (2025):** Only research organizations receive funding (80% of costs); ≥3 SMEs on steering group, no mandatory cash co-financing from SMEs. Target group: SMEs that "have not yet systematically utilized research expertise." First-contact mechanism — lowering entry threshold before expecting full co-financing.

Sources: https://www.businessfinland.fi/en/services/funding/funding-services/cooperation-between-companies-and-research-organizations/co-innovation

#### Sweden — Vinnova Partnership Requirements

Multi-actor consortium requirements embedded across programs. SIPs require industry-academia-organisation consortia. Impact Innovation (2024) requires at least one "need owner" (customer) and one "solution owner" (manufacturer). Weaker structural enforcement than Innosuisse bilateral model.

### Approach B: Transfer Infrastructure

#### UK — Catapult Network (Largest Scale, Strongest Additionality Evidence)

**Structure:** 9 Catapults across 65+ locations: High Value Manufacturing, Digital, Energy Systems, Cell and Gene Therapy, Offshore Renewable Energy, Compound Semiconductor Applications, Connected Places, Medicines Discovery, Satellite Applications. Independent not-for-profit companies. Budget model: one-third public core / one-third commercial / one-third collaborative R&D.

**Budget:** £1.6B over 5 years (April 2023–March 2028), +35% vs. prior period.

**Cumulative network results (Catapult Network Prospectus, Spring 2025):**
- 11,916 SMEs supported; 18,785 industry collaborations; 5,560 academic collaborations
- £8B GVA generated for UK economy
- **80% of projects would not have happened, or would have happened more slowly, without Catapult support** (strongest additionality claim in this dataset)
- 50% growth in businesses supported vs. comparable non-Catapult firms after 6 years

**CSA Catapult (independent evaluation, Harlow Consulting 2023):** Catapult-engaged firms averaged £82.9M annual private investment vs. £17.6M counterfactual; 67% of projects advanced 1–3 TRL levels.

**Structural principle:** "Neutral convener" — not beholden to any single industry player, enabling pre-competitive R&D collaboration across supply chains.

Sources: https://catapult.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/Catapult-Network-Prospectus-Spring-2025-DIGITAL.pdf | https://csa.catapult.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/CSA_Report_Final-5-compressed.pdf

#### Denmark — GTS Institutes (Most Efficient Market-Discipline Model)

**7 GTS institutes** (Danish Technological Institute, FORCE Technology, DHI, DBI, Alexandra Institute, Bioneer, DFM): non-profit, publicly approved RTOs.

**Financing model: 8% public performance-contract income / 92% commercial revenue.** Public subsidy maintains frontier expertise and public-good functions; market competition ensures ongoing relevance to industry needs.

**Scale (2024):** Total revenue ~DKK 4B; 20,648 unique private customers/year; **89% of private customers are SMEs.** Public subsidy: 369M DKK/year.

**Key design insight:** Market-competitive structure creates accountability ensuring services match SME demand rather than academic supply. Contrast with Fraunhofer: primarily university-adjacent rather than industry-demand-driven.

Sources: https://gts-net.dk/english/the-gts-system/ | https://ufsn.dk/english/research-and-innovation/framework-for-research-and-innovation/technological-services-and-knowledge-based-networks/the-danish-gts-institutes/

#### Netherlands — TNO and NWO Demonstrator

**TNO Fast Track:** No-obligation consultation for SMEs to validate concepts; ~3,000 organizations annually. Fieldlabs operate as public-private SME cluster platforms.

**NWO Demonstrator Programme:** Valley-of-death bridge. Maximum €160,000 per project (18 months), targeting TRL 4. "Show" (building MVP) + "Transfer" (demonstration for third-party uptake) structure.

Sources: https://www.tno.nl/en/collaboration/sme/ | https://www.nwo.nl/en/researchprogrammes/demonstrator

#### Korea — Technopark + INNOPOLIS System

**19 Technoparks** providing SMEs with shared R&D facilities, tech transfer support, consulting for IPR, prototyping, and investment linkage. **INNOPOLIS** manages commercialization of public research institute technologies: supply-demand matching, R&D funding for startups pursuing tech transfers from public labs, research-based spin-off support (GRI holds 10–20% equity).

**GRI technology transfer statistics (2023):** 1,946 total transfers; ~KRW 124B (~$95M) total royalty income.

Sources: https://www.innopolis.or.kr/menu?menuId=MENU00665 | https://www.nst.re.kr/eng/contents.do?key=186

## Problem 4: Company Exclusion from TRL 1-3 — The Early Valley of Death (added 2026-05-14)

### Problem Definition

Germany's Förderkatalog TRL classification (39,555 active federal projects) shows **~0% of company-received grants at TRL 1-3** and ~90% at TRL 4-6. This structural absence follows from institutional architecture: DFG is inaccessible to companies by statute; ZIM explicitly excludes basic research by guideline. German companies have no domestic instrument for early-stage (TRL 1-3) public co-funding.

**Absorptive capacity — the second-order cost:** Cohen & Levinthal (1990, ASQ 35:1) establish that absorptive capacity for external knowledge is built through own R&D, especially basic research. Path-dependent lock-out: firms that exit basic research progressively lose ability to exploit public science, even as university output grows. Arora, Belenzon & Patacconi (2018, SMJ 39:1) document the consequence longitudinally: US corporate basic research share fell from ~30% to <20% of BERD (1985–2015); productivity slowdown followed. Germany's structural 0% at TRL 1-3 accelerates this dynamic with no domestic policy lever to counteract it.

**Valley of death starts earlier for deeptech:** For biotech, advanced materials, and quantum technologies, the valley begins at TRL 2-3. Companies need early-stage co-funding to build the absorptive capacity that makes later TRL 4-6 grants effective. A portfolio funding companies only from TRL 4 onward solves the second half of the valley while ignoring the first.

### Country Instruments for Company TRL 1-3 Funding

#### USA — SBIR Phase I (Strongest Causal Evidence)

**Phase I:** Up to $275k; 6–12 months; TRL 1–4 (feasibility). Statutory set-aside (≤500 employees) locks out incumbents. Phase III: zero government funding — private capital must validate.

**Causal evidence — Howell (2017, AER):** Phase I doubles VC follow-on probability (10%→19%, +9pp); effects strongest for youngest firms and immature technologies. **Critical warning:** Phase II (TRL 4–7, up to $1.84M) shows **null result** on VC follow-on. The early-stage targeting of Phase I is what drives the effect. Germany's ~90% company grant concentration at TRL 4-6 replicates the Phase II design — the one that shows zero causal effect.

Sources: [[20_summaries/howell2017-financing-innovation.md]]

#### Israel — IIA Tnufa Programme

**Structure:** Israel Innovation Authority Tnufa funds TRL 1-3 feasibility for companies: ~$50–80k per award, 85% of approved expenses, royalty repayment only upon commercialization (success-contingent). Accessible to individual inventors and very early-stage companies. Part of a graduated ladder: Tnufa → R&D Fund → Nofar → MAGNET → BIRD.

**System context:** IIA spends ~$180M/year total. Israel ranks among the highest globally in VC investment per GDP; the TRL 1-3 on-ramp for companies is a structural contributor. Royalty structure avoids windfall subsidy and creates mild selection discipline without equity dilution.

**German relevance:** Demonstrates that small grants at TRL 1-3 with success-contingent repayment are administratively feasible at national scale.

#### EU — EIC Pathfinder + Transition (Insufficient)

**EIC Pathfinder** (TRL 1-4): ~€200M/year (2024). >80% of recipients are universities or research institutes. Companies rarely lead. Replicates the German institutional architecture at EU level.

**EIC Transition** (TRL 3-6): ~€135M/year (2024). Better company participation than Pathfinder, still predominantly research-institution driven.

**Overall verdict (Fuest et al. 2024):** Despite Pathfinder + Transition, <5% of Horizon Europe total budget reaches breakthrough technologies at early TRL. Instruments exist in name; company access at TRL 1-3 remains structurally marginal.

Sources: [[20_summaries/fuest_et_al_2024_how_to_escape_the_middle_technology_trap.md]]

#### Sweden — Vinnova Innovativa Startups (Company TRL 1-3, Broad Access) (added 2026-05-16)

**Structure:** Grants up to SEK 500k (~€45k), 100% public financing, up to 9 months. Eligibility: companies with <10 employees, <SEK 10M annual revenue. Multiple windows per year. Targets the pre-prototype stage for early-stage companies — functionally equivalent to a light TRL 1-3 company grant. Concept Development Grants available at SEK 300k for early proof-of-concept. No mandatory company co-financing — removes cash-flow barrier for very small firms.

**Relevance for Germany:** Demonstrates administrative feasibility of broad-access, high-frequency small grants for companies at TRL 1-3. Simple eligibility criteria; no consortium requirement at this stage.

Sources: https://www.vinnova.se/en/apply-for-funding/find-funding/innovativa-startups/

#### Germany — SPRIND (Right Direction, Insufficient Scale)

**SPRIND:** €229M/year (2024; source: SPRIND PCGK-Bericht 2024). Freiheitsgrade-Gesetz (2023). Fraunhofer ISI + Technopolis Deutschland evaluation (January 2025): positive — PM model agile, mission-oriented; 14 subsidiaries founded in 5 years; ~108 validation contracts since founding; 90 active validation contracts; ~2,100 applications processed. TRL range: primarily 2-6. Scale relative to DARPA: ~1:17–20 by budget; ~1:5 relative to UK ARIA.

**Structural limitation:** At €220M/year, SPRIND is ~5.8% of DARPA's scale ($3.8B/year). A proof-of-concept, not a system-level instrument. Not designed as a broad-based company grant program — too few PM slots, too domain-concentrated. Cannot fill the TRL 1-3 company gap at volume.

Sources: [[20_summaries/azoulay2019-arpa-model.md]]

### Comparative Assessment

| Instrument | TRL range | Company-eligible | Scale | Causal evidence | Anti-capture |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SBIR Phase I (USA) | 1-4 | Yes (≤500 employees, statutory) | $2.24B/year | Strong — Howell (2017, AER): +9pp VC probability | Statutory size cap |
| Vinnova Innovativa Startups (SE) | 1-3 | Yes (<10 employees, 100% funding) | SEK 500k/project | None (no RDD) | Size cap; no incumbent eligibility |
| Israel IIA Tnufa | 1-3 | Yes (individuals + early startups) | ~$20–30M est. | System-level only | Royalty repayment on success |
| Korea TIPS | 1-5 | Yes (via VC co-selection) | KRW 500–800M/project | World Bank RDD: +13.2% employment | Private-first co-selection |
| EIC Pathfinder (EU) | 1-4 | Consortia, rarely lead | ~€200M/year | None (no RDD) | None structural |
| SPRIND (DE) | 2-6 | Via subsidiaries | €229M/year (2024) | Positive (Fraunhofer ISI 2025) | PM autonomy |
| ZIM (DE) | 4-7 | Yes | ~€600M/year | 65% additionality (Stehnken 2024) | None for incumbents |

**Key finding:** Germany has ZIM (TRL 4-7, strong, broad) and SPRIND (TRL 2-6, nascent, underfunded). It has no instrument equivalent to SBIR Phase I: a company-facing, small-grant, early-stage (TRL 1-3), statutorily set-aside program. SPRIND partially covers the TRL range but is a PM-driven breakthrough agency, not a volume company grant program.

**Policy recommendation:** A German SBIR-Äquivalent for TRL 1-3 — statutory minimum allocation (e.g., 2% of federal R&D budget ≈ €200–300M/year), grants of €50–150k per project, companies ≤250 employees eligible, success-contingent royalty repayment, open-topic competitions preferred (Howell 2017: open > narrow-topic for causal effects). This fills the structural gap identified in Förderkatalog data without requiring SPRIND to scale as a volume program.
