# Innovation Paradox and Incumbent Lock-In

## Scope

This synthesis connects the vault's innovation-policy literature around one question: why can high R&D spending coexist with weak productivity, weak disruptive innovation, or weak commercialization? It is designed as a retrieval bridge for `wiki-pull`: future projects asking about Germany's innovation weakness, R&D subsidy allocation, incumbent lock-in, TRL bias, or radical versus incremental innovation should land here and then follow links to the relevant summaries and concepts.

## What we know so far

The literature does not point to a single "Germany spends too little on R&D" explanation. The stronger shared claim is that innovation outcomes depend on allocation, selection, commercialization, and sectoral direction.

For the Foerderkatalog paper, the working thesis is "Viel Wissen, wenig Wachstum": Germany is strong in upstream research and institutions, but weaker in translation into new firms, new markets, and productivity growth.

[[20_summaries/akcigit2024-innovation-paradox.md]] gives the clearest policy statement. The United States increased R&D intensity, but productivity slowed because innovative resources shifted toward large incumbents that often use R&D defensively. The mechanism is not only patenting or spending; it includes inventor reallocation, wage premia, political connections, and policy instruments that are easier for large firms to capture.

[[20_summaries/acemoglu2006-distance-to-frontier.md]] gives the macro policy logic: near-frontier economies should shift from investment-based incumbent support to innovation-based entrant selection. [[20_summaries/akcigit2018-heterogeneous-innovations.md]] adds the firm-level mechanism: internal innovation improves existing product lines, while external innovation adds product lines and can generate creative destruction; firm size shapes the mix. [[20_summaries/bloom2020-are-ideas-getting-harder.md]] provides a complementary mechanism, declining research productivity, which means that simply increasing inputs may be insufficient even without misallocation.

For Germany, the same conceptual pattern appears as an input-output gap. Germany has strong research and industrial capabilities, but the vault sources repeatedly point to weak commercialization, scale-up finance, digital/platform lag, incumbent-heavy trajectories, and policy systems that may reward established applicants.

## Cross-paper findings

1. Input metrics alone are misleading. R&D intensity, researcher counts, and patent counts need to be interpreted alongside allocation and output.
2. Incumbent dominance is a recurring mechanism. It appears in US inventor reallocation, German subsidy state dependence, and European industrial-policy debates.
3. Radical versus incremental innovation is the operational distinction that connects theory to the Bundesinnovationshaushalt project. TRL distribution can help test whether funding supports early-stage frontier exploration or later-stage incremental development.
4. Commercialization is a separate bottleneck. Even good research and early-stage innovation may not become market-changing firms without scale-up finance, demand, procurement, regulatory pathways, and transfer institutions.
5. A useful policy analysis must combine project type, recipient type, technology field, and stage of maturity.
6. Incumbent lock-in is not just an innovation-policy problem — it is a structural exit barrier. Paleschke (2026) shows historically that incumbents trapped in old trajectories rarely return to full technological leadership; the best achievable outcome is profitable downsizing. This makes the allocation question sharper: R&D funding that reproduces incumbent lock-in is not neutral — it may make the structural transition harder.

## Main points of agreement

- More R&D spending is not automatically better innovation policy.
- Young firms, entrants, and external innovation often have higher social returns than incumbent-protective innovation.
- Broad instruments can be captured by large firms unless allocation rules deliberately offset that advantage.
- Germany's problem is plausibly an allocation and commercialization problem rather than only an input shortage.

## Main disagreements

- The relative weight of misallocation versus declining research productivity is unresolved. [[20_summaries/bloom2020-are-ideas-getting-harder.md]] implies that innovation is getting harder across sectors; Akcigit emphasizes resource allocation and incumbent behavior.
- Some subsidy-evaluation papers find additionality and patent effects from public R&D support. That does not by itself answer whether the supported innovation is radical, incremental, entrant-led, or incumbent-defensive.
- Industrial-policy sources differ on whether strategic sector support can overcome lock-in or risks deepening it.

## Tensions and unresolved mechanisms

- Incumbents can be both necessary and problematic. Germany's large Mittelstand and industrial champions have engineering capacity, but they may also reinforce old trajectories.
- Later-stage TRL funding is not inherently bad. The question is whether late-stage funding accelerates commercialization of new technologies or subsidizes routine development for established firms.
- Patent output can signal innovation, but it may also reflect defensive patenting.

## Open questions

- Does the German Foderkatalog funding portfolio overrepresent incumbents relative to young firms?
- Does the portfolio overrepresent TRL 4-9 relative to TRL 1-3?
- Do projects by younger recipients differ systematically in TRL, technology field, and topic language?
- Are Germany's weak commercialization outcomes mainly about finance, regulation, public procurement, technology transfer, or incumbent protection?
- Which innovation-leader countries allocate differently, and can those differences be observed in comparable data?

## Discussion snapshot

This discussion started from a simple diagnostic question: what is the base problem behind German innovation policy that makes a large R&D system still produce weak innovation outcomes?

The current working answer is that the problem is not a single input shortage. It is a bundle of allocation and translation failures:

- public funding can be concentrated among established recipients rather than entrants,
- funding can stay in incremental or near-market stages without reliably creating new market positions,
- commercialization infrastructure can be too weak to turn research into products, firms, or deployment,
- and Germany's sectoral strengths can reinforce older technological trajectories instead of opening new ones.

That framing matters for the project because it converts a broad "Germany is not innovative enough" complaint into testable hypotheses. The TRL pipeline can check stage distribution, recipient analysis can check incumbent bias, and the concept layer can separate commercialization failure from frontier-selection failure and from declining research productivity.

The synthesis therefore keeps two claims apart:

1. Germany likely does not have a pure R&D quantity problem.
2. Germany may have a portfolio-quality problem, where public support is too weakly oriented toward entrants, commercialization, and paradigm-shifting innovation.

## Implications for my work

The Bundesinnovationshaushalt project should treat the innovation paradox as a testable allocation hypothesis. The core empirical design should connect:

- TRL classification of projects.
- Recipient age, size, and incumbent status.
- Prior funding state dependence.
- Technology-field classification.
- International comparison with innovation leaders.

The expected contribution is not just to show that Germany has an innovation gap, but to identify whether federal project funding helps correct or reinforce the allocation pattern that the innovation-paradox literature warns about.
