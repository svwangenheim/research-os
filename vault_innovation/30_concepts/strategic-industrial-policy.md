# Strategic Industrial Policy

## Definition

Strategic industrial policy is targeted public policy that aims to coordinate investment, innovation, infrastructure, and capability-building in areas where private actors underinvest because of spillovers, coordination failures, systemic risk, security concerns, or transformation pressure.

In this wiki, the concept is deliberately narrower than generic subsidies. It excludes purely horizontal cost relief, routine business support, or subsidies that preserve existing structures without solving a market or coordination failure.

## Scope boundaries

Included:

- Project-based support for strategic technology systems.
- Coordination of complementary investments across firms, infrastructure, and public institutions.
- Instruments such as IPCEI where they support cross-border strategic technology projects.
- Competition-oriented support that avoids entrenching inefficient incumbents.

Excluded:

- General tax cuts or electricity-price relief without technology-system targeting.
- Incumbent bailouts with no innovation or transformation logic.
- Local pork-barrel subsidies with no spillover or coordination rationale.

## Mechanism

Strategic industrial policy is justified when market actors cannot coordinate on a socially valuable transformation path. Examples include batteries, hydrogen, microelectronics, green industrial processes, digital infrastructure, and resilient supply chains. Firms may not invest if complementary assets are missing, if standards are uncertain, if infrastructure is unavailable, or if competitors can free-ride on pioneer investments.

The state can reduce this coordination problem by sharing risk, setting standards, funding infrastructure, supporting demonstration projects, and aligning cross-border investments. The risk is that the same instruments can be captured by incumbents or used to protect old business models. The concept therefore links directly to [[30_concepts/innovation-paradox.md]].

## How the papers use the concept

The DIW/Duso line uses strategic industrial policy to distinguish transformation-oriented public coordination from generic subsidy politics. Draghi uses a broader EU competitiveness frame, emphasizing scale, financing, and strategic sectors.
