# Basic Research in Firms

## Definition

**Basic research in firms** refers to systematic investigation by private-sector organizations aimed at advancing fundamental knowledge or understanding, without a specific commercial application as the primary objective. This distinguishes it from **applied research** (aimed at solving a specific, recognized need) and **development** (converting research into products/processes).

The NSF/OECD definition: "systematic study to gain more comprehensive knowledge or understanding of the subject under study without specific applications in mind."

In practice, most corporate basic research occupies what Stokes (1997) calls **Pasteur's Quadrant** — use-inspired basic research that is directly influenced by a quest for fundamental understanding but inspired by applied problems. Examples: Bell Labs researchers doing basic astrophysics to advance satellite communication; DuPont funding Carothers' superpolymer research that led to nylon.

## Why Firms Invest in Basic Research

The puzzle: if basic research produces knowledge that is a public good (publishable, patentable, diffusible to competitors), why do rational profit-maximizing firms invest at all? Three non-mutually-exclusive explanations:

**1. Internal appropriation (dominant — Arora et al. 2017)**
Firms invest in research primarily to use it in their own inventive activity. Patent-to-publication citation data shows firms producing science they can build on in their own patents invest more in research. The private return from internal use outweighs the cost of spillovers: one internal citation offsets the deterrent effect of four competitor citations. Research that is internally used is more productive and more highly valued.

**2. Absorptive capacity building (Cohen & Levinthal 1990; Martinez-Senra et al. 2011)**
Research activity builds the firm's capacity to recognize, assimilate, and exploit *external* knowledge. Basic research in particular increases knowledge stock and knowledge flows, improving absorptive capacity for up to 3 years after the investment. This dual return (internal output + absorption capability) makes basic research more privately valuable than the appropriability argument alone suggests.

**3. Reputation and talent attraction (Hicks 1995; Audretsch & Stephan 1996)**
Publishing scientific results signals quality to investors, customers, and high-skilled researchers who value publication norms. Less empirically dominant but relevant for prestige-sensitive sectors.

## How Basic Research Improves Innovation Output

The mechanism chain documented by these papers:

```
Basic research → [internal appropriation] → patents / inventions (Arora et al. 2017)
Basic research → [absorptive capacity building] → exploitation of external knowledge
              → product innovation (Martinez-Senra et al. 2011)
Basic research → [cross-industry spillovers] → follow-on innovations economy-wide
              (Akcigit et al. 2021)
Absorptive capacity × spillovers → innovation output (Escribano et al. 2009; Duan et al. 2021)
```

**Temporal structure**: The basic research → absorptive capacity effect persists for up to 3 years (Martinez-Senra et al. 2011). Basic research builds cumulative knowledge stock that sustains innovation capacity beyond the investment period.

## Basic vs. Applied Research: The Composition Problem

A major finding from Akcigit, Hanley & Serrano-Velarde (2021) is that the dominant market failure in R&D is not *insufficient total R&D* but *wrong composition*:

- Basic research generates spillovers *across industries* (not just within-industry as applied research does)
- 68% of cross-industry spillovers from basic research are not internalized
- Welfare loss: 4.6 pp consumption-equivalent from dynamic research misallocation
- **Overinvestment** in applied research due to strategic complementarity with basic research spillovers
- Optimal policy: subsidize basic research 49%, applied research 11%
- Uniform R&D subsidies *worsen* the misallocation by subsidizing over-provided applied research

## The Decline of Corporate Basic Research

Arora, Belenzon & Patacconi (2018) document that corporate basic research has declined since the 1980s as firms shifted toward closer-to-market development and outsourced basic research to universities. This is partly an institutional response to the inability to appropriate returns, partly capital market pressure for short-term results. See [[20_summaries/arora_belenzon_patacconi_2018_killing_golden_goose.md]].

## Implications for TRL-Based Innovation Policy

- **Basic research = TRL 1–3**: conceptual overlap is strong — funding basic research in firms is equivalent to funding low-TRL activities
- **Welfare case for public funding**: the Akcigit et al. result (68% uninternalized spillovers) provides the welfare-economic rationale; firms underinvest because social return exceeds private return
- **Absorptive capacity as the transmission belt**: funding TRL 1–3 projects also builds AC that enables firms to use TRL 4–7 public research (Fraunhofer, university outputs)
- **Composition matters for Germany**: if the Förderkatalog heavily weights TRL 4–9, it may subsidize the already over-provided applied research category

| Paper | Key Contribution |
|-------|----------------|
| Arora, Belenzon, Sheer (2017) | Internal appropriation is the main driver of corporate research investment |
| Akcigit, Hanley, Serrano-Velarde (2021) | 68% of basic research spillovers uninternalized; optimal: 49% basic vs. 11% applied subsidy |
| Martinez-Senra et al. (2011) | Basic research → absorptive capacity → product innovation; 3-year persistence |

| Cohen & Levinthal (1990) | Foundational: dual R&D role; path dependence in absorptive capacity |
