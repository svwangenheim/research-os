# Absorptive Capacity

## Definition

**Absorptive capacity (AC)** is a firm's ability to recognize the value of new, external knowledge, assimilate it, and apply it to commercial ends (Cohen & Levinthal 1990). It determines how much a firm benefits from knowledge outside its boundaries — in competitors' research, public science, universities, and industry spillovers.

Cohen & Levinthal's original formulation emphasized that AC is largely a byproduct of a firm's own R&D investment: investing in research builds the knowledge stock needed to understand and use external research outputs. This creates a *dual role for R&D*: it produces new knowledge directly (innovation role) and it builds the capacity to absorb external knowledge (absorption role).

Later work (Zahra & George 2002; Lane et al. 2006) extended to four dimensions:
1. **Acquisition**: identifying and acquiring external knowledge
2. **Assimilation**: analyzing and interpreting external knowledge
3. **Transformation**: combining new and existing knowledge
4. **Exploitation**: commercializing transformed knowledge

## What Builds Absorptive Capacity

**Basic research is a primary driver** (Martinez-Senra et al. 2011 — see [[20_summaries/martinez_senra_et_al_2011_basic_research_firms_spain.md]]):
- Basic research in firms increases knowledge stock and knowledge flows
- This improves all AC dimensions
- The effect persists for up to **3 years** after the basic research investment
- The effect generalizes across all industries (not only pharma/biotech)

**Path dependence**: AC is cumulative and difficult to build quickly. Firms that stop investing in basic research progressively lose AC. This creates a lock-in risk: once corporate basic research declines (documented by Arora et al. 2018), firms lose the capacity to absorb the very public science they are meant to commercialize.

## What Absorptive Capacity Enables

**1. Exploiting external knowledge spillovers** (Escribano et al. 2009 — [[20_summaries/escribano_fosfuri_tribo_2009_absorptive_capacity_external_knowledge.md]])
AC positively moderates the spillovers → innovation relationship. Firms with higher AC extract disproportionately more innovation from any given level of external spillover. Effect is stronger in:
- High-turbulence sectors (fast-moving external knowledge)
- Strong-IPR sectors (formal spillovers are scarcer; informal spillovers more valuable)

**2. Technology search effectiveness** (Duan et al. 2021 — [[20_summaries/duan_et_al_2021_absorptive_capacity_tech_search.md]])
AC moderates the technology search → innovation quality relationship non-linearly:
- At low search levels: higher AC accelerates the positive search-quality relationship
- At high search levels (beyond the optimum): higher AC mitigates the negative over-search effect

**3. Product innovation output** (Martinez-Senra et al. 2011)
AC mediates the basic research → product innovation chain. Without sufficient AC, even heavy basic research investment may not translate to product innovation.

## Absorptive Capacity and Innovation Policy

**The absorption gap**: publicly funded research (universities, Fraunhofer, Helmholtz) produces knowledge only if recipient firms can absorb it. Firms without AC cannot use what they are given. This creates a dual mandate for effective innovation policy:
1. Produce publicly available knowledge (public labs, universities)
2. Ensure private firms maintain internal research activity sufficient to build AC

The Akcigit et al. (2021) macro model encodes this: public basic research raises welfare only if the private sector can interact productively with it. This is the welfare-economic argument for subsidizing *private* basic research (not just public research) — it builds the AC necessary to leverage the public research system.

**For Germany**: the Fraunhofer model relies on firms having sufficient AC to absorb Fraunhofer outputs. If firms have shifted too far toward development and away from research (consistent with Arora et al. 2018 findings), the Fraunhofer transfer pipeline may become ineffective, not because Fraunhofer output declines but because recipient AC declines.

## Measurement

Common proxies (ordered by precision):

| Proxy | Pros | Cons |
|-------|------|------|
| R&D intensity (R&D/sales) | Observable, widely available | Conflates innovation and absorption roles |
| Multi-item index (Martinez-Senra) | Captures stock, flows, exploitation | Complex; not in standard datasets |
| Patent citations to external science | Directly measures absorption | Requires patent-publication data matching |
| Human capital (PhDs, researchers per employee) | Captures tacit knowledge capacity | Not specific to external knowledge absorption |

## Key Papers

| Paper | Key Contribution |
|-------|----------------|
| Cohen & Levinthal (1990) | Foundational; dual role of R&D; path dependence in AC building |
| Escribano, Fosfuri, Tribó (2009) | AC as moderator of spillovers → innovation; IPR and turbulence as contingencies |
| Martinez-Senra et al. (2011) | Basic research → AC → product innovation; 3-year temporal persistence |
| Duan et al. (2021) | AC as heterogeneous moderator of technology search non-linearity |
| Zahra & George (2002) | Four-component AC framework (acquisition, assimilation, transformation, exploitation) |

## Open Questions

- Can AC be built through subsidized external research participation alone, or does it require own basic research?
- How does AC interact with TRL stage — do firms need different types of AC at different TRL levels?
- For Germany: is the Fraunhofer transfer model effective at building firm-level AC, or does it substitute for it?
