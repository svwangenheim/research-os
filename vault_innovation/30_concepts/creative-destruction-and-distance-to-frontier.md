# Creative Destruction and Distance to Frontier

## Definition

Creative destruction is the Schumpeterian growth mechanism in which new technologies, firms, or business models replace older ones and thereby generate productivity growth. Distance-to-frontier theory adds that optimal policy depends on how close an economy is to the world technology frontier. Far-from-frontier economies may grow through imitation and investment in existing activities; near-frontier economies need innovation, experimentation, competition, and entrant selection.

Together, the concepts explain why incumbent-protective policy can become harmful in advanced economies. Once an economy is near the frontier, growth depends less on preserving existing firms and more on allowing new technologies and entrants to challenge them.

## Scope boundaries

Included:

- Schumpeterian creative destruction and business-stealing dynamics.
- Distance-to-frontier arguments about policy regime shifts.
- Entrant versus incumbent selection near the frontier.
- Links between radical innovation, market power, competition, and productivity.

Excluded:

- Any generic discussion of innovation novelty without the replacement or frontier mechanism.
- Sectoral path-dependence alone, which belongs partly in [[30_concepts/radical-vs-incremental-innovation.md]] and the Dosi/Malerba summaries.
- Additionality of subsidies unless the question is whether support enables frontier-shifting activity.

## Papers that discuss this concept

- [[20_summaries/aghion1992-creative-destruction.md]]: formalizes creative destruction in endogenous growth theory.
- [[20_summaries/acemoglu2006-distance-to-frontier.md]]: argues that policy should shift from investment-based to innovation-based strategies as an economy approaches the frontier.
- [[20_summaries/akcigit2018-heterogeneous-innovations.md]]: distinguishes internal/incumbent innovation from external/entrant innovation and estimates stronger growth effects for external innovation.
- [[20_summaries/aghion2019-innovation-inequality.md]]: connects innovation, rents, market power, and inequality.
- [[20_summaries/akcigit2024-innovation-paradox.md]]: uses the frontier/incumbent logic to explain why rising R&D can coincide with slowing productivity.
- [[20_summaries/beck2016-radical-or-incremental.md]]: empirically distinguishes radical from incremental innovation in subsidy effects.

## How the papers use, support, challenge, or modify it

Aghion and Howitt establish the destructive side of innovation as a growth mechanism. Acemoglu, Aghion, and Zilibotti turn this into a policy rule: close-to-frontier economies should favor innovation-based selection rather than investment-based protection. Akcigit and Kerr provide a firm-level distinction that matters for the Foerderkatalog project: internal/incumbent innovation can be privately useful but less growth-enhancing than external/entrant innovation.

The concept supports the vault's core innovation-paradox thesis. If German federal R&D funding disproportionately supports late-stage, incumbent, or incremental projects, then it may be additional in a narrow sense while still failing to generate frontier-shifting growth.

- How close is Germany to the frontier in each technology field rather than in aggregate?

- How should public funding balance stability in industrial capabilities against the need for disruptive experimentation?
- Can TRL distributions reveal whether funding supports frontier exploration or late-stage incumbent development?
- Does strategic industrial policy solve coordination failures or preserve existing technology trajectories?
