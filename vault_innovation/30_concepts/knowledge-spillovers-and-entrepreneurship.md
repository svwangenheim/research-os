# Knowledge Spillovers and Entrepreneurship

## Definition

Knowledge spillovers and entrepreneurship describe the mechanism by which research knowledge, firm R&D, university discoveries, skilled labor, and regional capabilities create economic value beyond the organization that produced them. Entrepreneurs, start-ups, spin-outs, and new firms can commercialize knowledge that incumbents, universities, or public research organizations do not fully exploit. The concept is central to explaining why high research inputs may still fail to generate growth if spillovers are blocked by weak start-up formation, transfer institutions, finance, or market entry.

## Scope boundaries

Included:

- University and public-research spillovers into start-ups and regional innovation.
- Entrepreneurial commercialization of unused or underused knowledge.
- Regional innovation systems, start-up ecosystems, and knowledge filters.
- Links between R&D, new ventures, technology transfer, and growth.

Excluded:

- Any generic externality from innovation without an entrepreneurship or commercialization channel.
- Technology transfer administration alone, which belongs in [[30_concepts/technology-transfer-and-translation.md]].
- Scale-up finance alone, which belongs in [[30_concepts/equity-gap-sme-financing.md]].

## How the papers use, support, challenge, or modify it

Audretsch and Lehmann provide the core theory: knowledge generated in universities and firms can spill into entrepreneurial activity. Akcigit and Kerr connect that idea to growth by distinguishing internal innovation, which improves existing product lines, from external innovation, which adds product lines and can generate creative destruction. German policy reports then turn the theory into a diagnosis: Germany may have strong research institutions and incumbent capabilities, but weak transfer, start-up, and scale-up channels.

The concept therefore connects [[30_concepts/commercialization-gap.md]] and [[30_concepts/innovation-paradox.md]]. If knowledge spillovers are blocked, high research spending can coexist with weak disruptive firm creation and weak productivity growth.
