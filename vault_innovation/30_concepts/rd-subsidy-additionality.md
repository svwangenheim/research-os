# R&D Subsidy Additionality

## Definition

R&D subsidy additionality asks whether public support creates research, innovation, investment, knowledge, patents, commercialization, or social value that would not have happened without the support. A grant can be additional in several ways: it can increase the amount of private R&D, shift firms toward riskier or earlier-stage projects, accelerate development, signal quality to private investors, enable collaborations, or generate spillovers. The opposing concern is crowding out: public money may finance activity that firms would have funded anyway.

## Scope boundaries

Included:

- Input additionality: extra R&D spending, labor, projects, or collaborations.
- Output additionality: extra patents, products, sales, employment, follow-on finance, or commercialization.
- Behavioral additionality: changes in risk appetite, cooperation, project selection, or technological ambition.
- Selection problems: who receives funding and whether recipients differ from non-recipients.
- Crowding-out and windfall concerns.

Excluded:

- General innovation performance without a public-support counterfactual.
- TRL classification alone. TRL can describe project maturity, but it does not estimate additionality.
- Broad industrial-policy arguments unless they make claims about public support causing additional activity.

## Papers that discuss this concept

- [[20_summaries/czarnitzki2004-rd-subsidies-zew.md]]: studies German manufacturing R&D subsidies and asks whether they complement or crowd out private R&D.
- [[20_summaries/czarnitzki2014-funding-source.md]]: compares national and EU funding and links funding source to patent outcomes.
- [[20_summaries/czarnitzki2018-additionality-firm-size.md]]: German firm-level analysis showing positive additionality on average but stronger effects for small firms than large firms. Implies that the observed large-firm concentration in German grant allocation (Aschhoff 2010) depletes realized social returns. [Added 2026-05-11 from trl_research_briefing]
- [[20_summaries/dechezlepretre2023-rd-tax-spillovers.md]]: UK RDD showing low-TRL innovations generate approximately 30% higher social returns than high-TRL innovations due to larger spillovers at early TRL stages. The primary empirical anchor for TRL-differentiated policy interpretation. [Added 2026-05-11 from trl_research_briefing]
- [[20_summaries/earto2014-trl-policy-tool.md]]: EU policy reference establishing the Low/Mid/High TRL grouping; maps TRL to EU State Aid intensity limits; identifies the valley of death at Mid TRL 4–6 as the zone with highest additionality. [Added 2026-05-11 from trl_research_briefing]
- [[20_summaries/kleer2010-rd-subsidies-signal.md]]: treats subsidies as quality signals that can attract private investment, especially for smaller firms.
- [[20_summaries/howell2017-financing-innovation.md]]: uses SBIR grants to estimate effects on venture capital follow-on finance, patents, and firm outcomes.
- [[20_summaries/audretsch2002-sbir-evaluation.md]] and [[20_summaries/audretsch2003-sbir.md]]: describe SBIR as a high-risk early-stage funding instrument with strong additionality logic.
- [[20_summaries/prognos2019-zim-evaluation.md]], [[20_summaries/stehnken2024-zim-evaluation.md]], and [[20_summaries/struss2016-zim-wirkungsanalyse.md]]: evaluate ZIM and report additionality, project-success, cooperation, and firm-behavior evidence.
- [[20_summaries/hesse-grashof2020-zim-incrementalism.md]]: ZIM evaluation showing positive innovation effects but predominantly incremental output. Additionality and novelty are independent dimensions. [Added 2026-05-11 from trl_research_briefing]
- [[20_summaries/aschhoff2010-who-gets-the-money.md]]: focuses on selection into funding and shows why recipient persistence matters for additionality interpretation.
- [[20_summaries/efi2024-gutachten.md]]: stresses causal evaluation and better data access for innovation-policy learning.
- [[20_summaries/arora2018-corporate-basic-research.md]]: documents the long-run decline of corporate basic research in the US (30%→<20% of BERD, 1985–2015) and links it to productivity slowdown. Market failure is dual: direct output loss and absorptive capacity degradation. Implies that basic research support for companies addresses a market failure that generic R&D tax credits and late-stage grants do not. [Added 2026-05-14]
- [[20_summaries/cohen-levinthal1990-absorptive-capacity.md]]: establishes that firms build absorptive capacity for external knowledge through their own basic R&D; path-dependent lock-out effect. Second-order additionality channel: TRL 1-3 company grants maintain the interface capacity that makes all subsequent TRL 4-6 grants effective. [Added 2026-05-14]

## How the papers use, support, challenge, or modify it

The subsidy-evaluation literature generally supports the idea that public R&D can be additional, but it also shows that additionality is not automatic. Czarnitzki and Kleer emphasize complementarity and signaling. Howell and the SBIR evidence emphasize early-stage finance constraints and follow-on investment. ZIM evaluations report substantial project additionality, cooperation effects, and R&D-intensity gains, but their evidence still needs interpretation against selection, project type, maturity, and whether programs mostly deepen activity among already R&D-active firms. Aschhoff complicates the picture by showing persistent recipient selection: if the same firms repeatedly get funding, then additionality must be interpreted alongside incumbent access and administrative capability.

This concept links directly to [[30_concepts/innovation-paradox.md]]. Even an additional subsidy may not solve the innovation paradox if the additional activity is incremental, incumbent-led, late-stage, or weakly connected to commercialization.

## Open questions and tensions

- Can a project be additional but still low social return because it is late-stage or incumbent-protective?
- Do tax incentives and direct grants differ in their additionality by firm size, age, or technology maturity?
- How should evaluations account for spillovers that do not appear in firm-level outcomes?
- Does Germany's data infrastructure allow credible enough counterfactual evaluation?
- How should additionality evidence be combined with TRL portfolio mapping?
